# LQR — Flywheel Control

<Difficulty level="Hard" />

<Abstract>
The modeling checkpoint introduced a gain matrix $K$, and the observer checkpoint supplied the state
estimate it acts on. Where should the gains come from? LQR answers by reframing the problem. Instead of picking gains, you price the two things you
actually care about — error and effort — and the math solves for the $K$ that minimizes the total
bill. Bryson's rule turns that pricing into two numbers any team can name: the error you can tolerate
and the volts you have.
</Abstract>

<Problem>

## A gain matrix is worse to hand-tune than three PID knobs

PID tuning is at least famous. Everyone knows the folklore: raise kP until it oscillates, add kD, add
a little kI. A gain *matrix* has no folklore. For a two-state elevator, $K$ has two coupled entries,
and nudging one changes what the other should be. Guessing pole locations is the same problem wearing
a math costume.

What a team can state confidently is not gains. It is tolerances. "The flywheel needs to stay within
about 8 rad/s or the shot spreads." "We have 12 volts, and browning out is unacceptable." LQR is the
tool that converts exactly those two sentences into a gain.

</Problem>

<Theory>

## Price the error, price the effort, minimize the bill

LQR (the **l**inear-**q**uadratic **r**egulator) scores every possible controller by a running cost. This
page designs directly for the robot's 20 ms sampled loop:

$$
J_d = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbf{x}_k^\top Q\,\mathbf{x}_k
+ \mathbf{u}_k^\top R\,\mathbf{u}_k\right)
$$

<EquationLegend
  title="Symbols in the cost function"
  items={[
    {symbol: 'J_d', meaning: 'the total cost of a whole run: the number LQR minimizes. The subscript d marks it as the discrete, sampled version'},
    {symbol: 'Q', meaning: 'the price of state ERROR. Raise it and the controller works harder to stay on target'},
    {symbol: 'R', meaning: 'the price of CONTROL EFFORT. Raise it and the controller becomes gentler. Only the ratio Q/R matters, not either alone'},
    {symbol: 'x\u1d40Qx', meaning: 'the quadratic form: squares the error so that sign does not matter and large errors cost disproportionately more'},
    {symbol: 'the sum to infinity', meaning: 'the cost is charged over all future loops, which is why the resulting gain is a single constant rather than a schedule'},
  ]}
/>

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'x_k', meaning: 'the state at sampled loop k. The cost is written about the origin; for tracking, apply the same K to the error r_k - x_k and supply the equilibrium input as feedforward'},
    {symbol: 'u_k', meaning: 'the feedback-correction effort at sampled loop k, in volts'},
    {symbol: 'Q', meaning: 'how much you are charged for error, per state'},
    {symbol: 'R', meaning: 'how much you are charged for effort, per input'},
    {symbol: 'J_d', meaning: 'the sampled total bill; discrete LQR finds the K that minimizes it'},
  ]}
/>

Squaring does two jobs: big errors cost disproportionately more than small ones, and sign stops
mattering. The controller that minimizes $J_d$ over all time turns out to be plain state feedback,
$u_k = K(r_k - x_k)$, with $K$ computed from $A_d$, $B_d$, $Q$, and $R$ by solving the discrete Riccati
equation. You
will never solve it by hand for real systems; WPILib and every controls library does it in one call.
The design work is choosing $Q$ and $R$.

### Bryson's rule: tolerances in, weights out

Bryson's rule makes the choice concrete. Set each weight to one over the square of the largest value
you are willing to see:

$$
Q = \frac{1}{(\text{tolerable error})^2},
\qquad
R = \frac{1}{(\text{available volts})^2}
$$

Now the knobs mean something physical. Tighten the error tolerance and error gets expensive, so the
optimizer buys a bigger gain. Shrink the voltage budget and effort gets expensive, so it accepts a
lazier response. Only the *ratio* $Q/R$ matters — double both and the same $K$ falls out.

<LqrExplorer />

The explorer starts from the one-state flywheel model ($k_V = 0.02$, $k_A = 0.005$, so $A=-4$,
$B=200$) and exactly discretizes it at $\Delta t=0.020$ s:

$$
A_d=e^{A\Delta t}=0.9231163,\qquad
B_d=\frac{e^{A\Delta t}-1}{A}B=3.8441827.
$$

For the scalar discrete Riccati equation, define
$c=R(1-A_d^2)-QB_d^2$. Its stabilizing positive solution and feedback gain are

$$
P=\frac{-c+\sqrt{c^2+4B_d^2QR}}{2B_d^2},
\qquad
K=\frac{A_dB_dP}{R+B_d^2P}.
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'P', meaning: 'the solution of the Riccati equation, an intermediate quantity on the way to K. This is not the covariance P from the Kalman lessons'},
    {symbol: 'c', meaning: 'a grouping defined just above purely to keep the formula readable. It carries no physical meaning'},
    {symbol: 'K', meaning: 'the resulting feedback gain, in volts per unit of state error. This is the one number the robot actually needs'},
  ]}
/>

The default is reproducible without the widget: tolerance $8$ rad/s gives $Q=1/64$, and budget $12$ V
gives $R=1/144$. Substitution yields $P\approx0.0160140$ and

$$
K\approx0.2332876\ \frac{\mathrm V}{\mathrm{rad/s}},
\qquad A_d-B_dK\approx0.0263163.
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'A_d \u2212 B_dK', meaning: 'the closed-loop pole. In discrete time a magnitude below 1 means errors shrink every loop; above 1 means they grow. At 0.026 an error is almost gone in a single 20 ms sample'},
  ]}
/>

The closed-loop sampled pole has magnitude below one, so the unsaturated linear error decays. This $Q/R$
is explicitly the per-sample cost above. A library that discretizes a continuous cost more exactly may
report a different gain; keep its model, cost convention, and runtime gain together.

The 12 V Bryson number is the scale used to penalize the **feedback correction**; it is not 12 V of extra
headroom on top of feedforward. At the 300 rad/s target, the model-inversion hold term is
$k_Vr=6$ V. The widget and FTC loop add that hold term to feedback and then clamp the **total** request to
the available rail. Positive correction therefore has about 6 V of nominal headroom at that operating
point, and saturation is part of the result you must inspect.

A shot steals 60 rad/s at the dashed line; the green trace is the recovery under the LQR gain, and
the amber trace is what it costs in volts. Drag the error tolerance down and watch the gain grow, the
recovery sharpen, and the voltage slam into the 12 V ceiling. That ceiling is the honest limit of the
whole method: LQR assumed the actuator can deliver what it asks for, and while the command is pinned
at 12 V the controller is no longer optimal, just saturated. If your "optimal" design lives on the
ceiling, you asked for more than the motor has — relax $Q$ or admit you need a bigger motor.

<Callout type="warn" title="Do not paste a continuous gain into this loop.">
For the same $A$, $B$, $Q$, and $R$, the continuous Riccati formula gives $K\approx1.4801$. At 20 ms that
would produce the sampled pole $A_d-B_dK\approx-4.77$, outside the stable interval $(-1,1)$. The earlier
number is not a faster version of this controller; it is a different design that is unsafe at this period.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warn" title="Optimal for the model, not for the robot.">
LQR minimizes the cost <em>for the A and B you gave it</em>. A sloppy
<a href="../control-theory/system-identification">system-identification</a> fit produces a gain that is
precisely optimal for a robot that does not exist. Garbage model in, optimal garbage out.
</Callout>

</Theory>

<Deploy>

## The full loop in WPILib, one line in FTC

FRC ships the complete stack: the model, the Kalman filter that estimates the state, the LQR that
computes $K$, and a wrapper that runs predict–correct–control every loop.

<JavaCode league="FRC" label="Flywheel LQR, the standard WPILib pattern">{`// Model straight from characterization (volts per rad/s, volts per rad/s²).
LinearSystem<N1, N1, N1> plant = LinearSystemId.identifyVelocitySystem(kV, kA);

// Observer: how much to trust the model vs. the encoder.
KalmanFilter<N1, N1, N1> observer = new KalmanFilter<>(
    Nat.N1(), Nat.N1(), plant,
    VecBuilder.fill(3.0),    // model trust: rad/s of state std-dev
    VecBuilder.fill(0.01),   // encoder noise std-dev
    0.020);

// LQR: Bryson's rule, stated as tolerances.
LinearQuadraticRegulator<N1, N1, N1> lqr = new LinearQuadraticRegulator<>(
    plant,
    VecBuilder.fill(8.0),    // Q: tolerable velocity error, rad/s
    VecBuilder.fill(12.0),   // R: available control effort, volts
    0.020);

LinearSystemLoop<N1, N1, N1> loop =
    new LinearSystemLoop<>(plant, lqr, observer, 12.0, 0.020);`}</JavaCode>

<JavaCode league="FRC" label="...and every robot loop">{`loop.setNextR(VecBuilder.fill(targetRadPerSec));   // where we want the state
loop.correct(VecBuilder.fill(encoder.getRate()));  // fold in the measurement
loop.predict(0.020);                               // advance model + compute u
flywheelMotor.setVoltage(loop.getU(0));`}</JavaCode>

WPILib performs the sampled design internally from the period and its own cost-discretization convention;
use the gain produced by that complete library configuration. On FTC, the scalar per-sample design derived
above needs no runtime matrix library. Its controller is one line plus measured-loop guarding and
[voltage compensation](../control-theory/voltage-compensation.mdx):

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="The same 20 ms discrete controller, gain precomputed">{`static final double K = 0.2332876;    // volts per (rad/s) of velocity error
static final double DESIGN_PERIOD = 0.020;            // what K was designed for
static final double MAX_VALID_LOOP_SECONDS = 0.030;   // margin below instability

private final ElapsedTime timer = new ElapsedTime();

void start() {
    timer.reset();
}

void run(double targetVelocity) {
    double dt = timer.seconds();
    timer.reset();
    if (!Double.isFinite(dt) || dt <= 0 || dt > MAX_VALID_LOOP_SECONDS) {
        flywheel.setPower(0);            // the timing contract is broken
        return;
    }

    double measured = flywheel.getVelocity(AngleUnit.RADIANS);
    double requested = K * (targetVelocity - measured)   // the LQR feedback
            + kV * targetVelocity;                       // feedforward, cruise

    double vbat = voltageSensor.getVoltage();
    if (!Double.isFinite(vbat) || vbat < MIN_VALID_BATTERY_VOLTS) {
        flywheel.setPower(0);
        return;
    }

    double available = Math.min(12, vbat);   // never claim more than the rail
    double volts = clamp(requested, -available, +available);
    flywheel.setPower(clamp(volts / vbat, -1, +1));
}
`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="Why the loop-time guard is the point of the block">

A gain computed for a 20 ms sample is not valid at 45 ms. Work out what happens to a discrete
controller when the real period drifts past its design point, and why refusing to act beats applying a
stale design.

<Solution>

The gain encodes how much correction is appropriate *per sample*. Stretch the sample and the same
correction is applied over more than twice the time it was designed for, so the effective loop gain
rises — and past some period the discrete system goes unstable regardless of how conservative the
continuous design was. Applying it anyway means the controller diverges precisely when the robot is
already struggling. Commanding zero is a mechanism that coasts; commanding a stale gain is one that
oscillates harder each loop. Look up "discrete-time stability" and "sample rate and gain margin."

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why K is precomputed">

Solving the LQR problem means solving a Riccati equation. Look up what that involves and estimate how
it would fit in a 20 ms budget on a Control Hub. Then work out what the runtime form actually collapses
to for a single-state system.

<Solution>

The Riccati solve is iterative matrix algebra — repeated multiplications and an inverse, run to
convergence. Even at 1×1 it is wasted work, and at realistic state counts it does not belong in a
control loop that also has to read sensors and drive motors.

For one state, the whole LQR collapses to one multiplication. What distinguishes it from a P controller
is not the runtime form at all but where the gain came from: solved from a measured model and a stated
cost, rather than turned up until it looked right.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Callout type="idea" title="So how is this different from the PF controller you already had?">
Structurally it isn't: gain times error, plus feedforward. What changed is the origin story. The PF
gain was found by poking the robot; the LQR gain is derived from the measured model and two stated
tolerances, so it survives design changes. Swap the flywheel mass, re-run system identification,
recompute — no re-tuning session. And when the mechanism grows a second state, the same recipe hands
you both gains at once, which hand-tuning genuinely cannot.
</Callout>

<Admonition type="warning" title="Hardware reality: the sample period is part of the design">

The derivation, widget, test, and FTC constant all use the same exact-ZOH **20 ms discrete design**. Measure
your loop distribution, reset timing state once at controller start, and stop or redesign when the loop
exceeds its validated bound. Never carry this gain to another period or plant without recomputing the
discrete model and Riccati solution — the same timing discipline as
[everywhere else on this site](../software-architecture/discretization.mdx).

</Admonition>
