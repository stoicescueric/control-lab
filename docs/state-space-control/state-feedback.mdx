# State-Space Models & State Feedback

<Difficulty level="Hard" />

<Abstract>
A state-space controller feeds back every state of the mechanism at once: $u = K(r - x)$. For a
two-state elevator that means one gain on position error and one on velocity error, working together
by design instead of by cascaded tuning. The model comes cheap — the $k_V$ and $k_A$ you measured in
system identification are the matrix entries. The open question this lesson leaves behind is how to
choose $K$, and the next lesson answers it.
</Abstract>

<Admonition type="note" title="Prerequisites">

Read the [state-space preface](../preface/why-math-matters/state-space.mdx) for the vocabulary and
[system identification](../control-theory/system-identification.mdx) for where $k_V$ and $k_A$ come
from. The [Kalman filter](../signal-processing/kalman.mdx) shows up at the end.

</Admonition>

<Problem>

## One error signal can't see two states

An elevator has a position and a velocity, and both matter. Feed a PID only the position error and it
is blind to how fast the carriage is moving until the error starts changing — the D term is a crude,
noisy estimate of a state you actually know. Teams patch this with cascaded loops (a position PID
commanding a velocity PID) and it works, but now there are two loops fighting through each other, six
gains, and no principled way to trade them off.

The state-space view removes the patchwork. The mechanism *is* a vector of states. Measure or estimate
all of them, feed all of them back, and design the response once.

</Problem>

<Theory>

## The model you already measured

### From kV, kA to a differential equation

The [feedforward model](../control-theory/feedforward.mdx) says the voltage a velocity mechanism needs
is $V = k_V v + k_A \dot{v}$. Solve that for the acceleration and you have a state equation:

$$
\dot{v} = -\frac{k_V}{k_A}\,v + \frac{1}{k_A}\,V
$$

That is $\dot{x} = Ax + Bu$ with one state. For a flywheel:

$$
A = -\frac{k_V}{k_A}, \qquad B = \frac{1}{k_A}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'A', meaning: 'how the state changes on its OWN, with zero input. Negative here means a coasting flywheel slows down, which is what you would expect'},
    {symbol: 'B', meaning: 'how much the input moves the state. Since k_A is volts per unit of acceleration, 1/k_A is acceleration per volt'},
    {symbol: 'the sign of A', meaning: 'a negative A is a system that settles by itself; a positive A is one that runs away. This single number decides which'},
  ]}
/>

$A$ says how the system moves on its own (back-EMF and friction bleed speed off, hence the minus
sign). $B$ says how hard the input pushes on the state. Both numbers came out of the
[system-identification](../control-theory/system-identification.mdx) fit — the model costs nothing new.

### Take one exact sampled step

The robot runs a loop, not a differential equation, so the model gets converted once into a
step-ahead form (the same move as everywhere in
[From Math to a Loop](../software-architecture/discretization.mdx)):

$$
\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = A_d\,\mathbf{x}_k + B_d\,\mathbf{u}_k
$$

WPILib does this conversion internally from your loop period. For one state,

$$
A_d=e^{A\Delta t}, \qquad
B_d=\begin{cases}
B\Delta t, & A=0 \\
\dfrac{e^{A\Delta t}-1}{A}B, & A\ne0
\end{cases}
$$

<EquationLegend
  title="Symbols in the discretization"
  items={[
    {symbol: 'A_d, B_d', meaning: 'the DISCRETE versions of A and B: the exact effect of holding the input constant for one whole \u0394t, rather than the instantaneous rate A and B describe'},
    {symbol: 'e^{A\u0394t}', meaning: 'the matrix exponential. For one state it is the ordinary exponential, and it is exact rather than an approximation of the step'},
    {symbol: 'the A = 0 case', meaning: 'when A is zero the state does not decay at all, so the input simply accumulates: B\u0394t. The general formula would divide by zero, hence the split'},
  ]}
/>

The $A=0$ branch is the finite limit, not a special physical plant. Change $v$, $V$, and measured
$\Delta t$ below; the first readout computes $v_{k+1}=A_dv_k+B_dV_k$. The second applies an illustrative
feedback gain and clips the voltage to $\pm12$ V before taking the same exact step.

**Change:** adjust $v$, $V$, and $\Delta t$, then enable the illustrative feedback command. **Observe:**

### State feedback: u = K(r − x)

The control law is one line:

$$
\mathbf{u} = K(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{x}) + u_{ff}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'r', meaning: 'the reference: the state you WANT, as a vector. r \u2212 x is therefore the state error'},
    {symbol: 'K', meaning: 'the feedback gain solved from the model. This is state-feedback K, not the Kalman gain from the estimation lessons'},
    {symbol: 'u_ff', meaning: 'feedforward added outside the loop, such as a gravity hold. The feedback then only has to correct what the model missed'},
  ]}
/>

For one state, $K$ is one gain. The $u_{ff}$ term is the plant-inversion feedforward you already know:
$k_Vr$ holds a flywheel reference, so feedback only cleans up the remaining error.

### Core state-feedback checkpoint

If you can calculate one sampled update, form `requestedVolts = K * (reference - state) + feedforward`,
and clamp it to the available voltage, you have the required artifact. The rest shows how the same idea
expands to multiple states and how designers interpret the resulting dynamics.

### Optional continuation: multiple states, poles, and observers

An elevator needs two states, position and velocity, so $A$ and $B$ grow into matrices:

$$
\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} p \\ v \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -k_V/k_A \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1/k_A \end{bmatrix}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'p', meaning: 'position, the first state of the elevator'},
    {symbol: 'the 1 in A', meaning: 'this row says "the rate of change of position IS velocity". It is not a tuned number, it is a definition'},
    {symbol: 'the 0 in B', meaning: 'voltage cannot change position directly, only velocity. Position moves only as a consequence'},
  ]}
/>

Read the rows: the first says $\dot{p}=v$; the second is the same motor equation as the flywheel. The
matrix form lets one input respond to both state errors. Now $K$ has a position gain and a velocity
gain; structurally, that resembles PD control.

Substitute the law into the model and the closed-loop system becomes

$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}}=(A-BK)\mathbf{x}+BK\mathbf{r}+B u_{ff}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'A \u2212 BK', meaning: 'the CLOSED-LOOP dynamics. Choosing K reshapes this matrix, and its eigenvalues decide whether the mechanism settles smoothly, oscillates, or diverges'},
  ]}
/>

For a constant reference, choose the equilibrium feedforward so $A\mathbf r+B u_{ff}=0$ and define
$\mathbf e=\mathbf x-\mathbf r$. Then $\dot{\mathbf e}=(A-BK)\mathbf e$. The eigenvalues of $A-BK$ are
the closed-loop *poles*: they describe convergence and overshoot. Picking them directly is pole placement;
the [next lesson](./lqr.mdx) instead derives $K$ from an error-versus-effort cost.

### When you can't measure a state

The elevator encoder gives position. Velocity has to be estimated, and differentiating the encoder
amplifies noise — the same problem the [filter module](../signal-processing/index.mdx) solved. The
clean answer is an observer: run the model forward as a prediction, correct it with the measurement,
weighted by trust. That is exactly a [Kalman filter](../signal-processing/kalman.mdx), now estimating
the states the controller needs. The full pattern is a loop within a loop: **predict** the state,
**correct** with the sensor, **control** with $K$.

</Theory>

<Deploy>

## Matrices in WPILib, arithmetic in FTC

FRC ships the whole stack. You hand WPILib the identified constants and it builds the model:

<JavaCode league="FRC" label="The model from kV/kA — WPILib builds the matrices">{`// Flywheel: one state (velocity). Units: volts per (rad/s), volts per (rad/s²).
LinearSystem<N1, N1, N1> flywheel =
    LinearSystemId.identifyVelocitySystem(kV, kA);

// Elevator: two states (position, velocity), from the same characterization.
LinearSystem<N2, N1, N1> elevator =
    LinearSystemId.identifyPositionSystem(kV, kA);`}</JavaCode>

FTC has no such library, and for one or two states you do not need it. Compute the gains offline (next
lesson), then the runtime controller is plain arithmetic:

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Two-state elevator feedback, gains precomputed offline">{`// K_POS and K_VEL are computed offline. velocity is a FILTERED encoder
// derivative, not a raw difference.
double update(double targetPosition, double position, double velocity) {
    double vbat = voltageSensor.getVoltage();
    if (!Double.isFinite(vbat) || vbat < MIN_VALID_BATTERY_VOLTS) return 0;

    double requested = K_POS * (targetPosition - position)
            + K_VEL * (0 - velocity)     // arrive STOPPED
            + kG;                        // gravity feedforward, always on

    double available = Math.min(12, vbat);
    double volts = clamp(requested, -available, +available);
    return clamp(volts / vbat, -1, +1);
}
`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="State feedback, not PD">

The velocity term is what makes this state feedback rather than a P controller with a D term bolted on.
Work out the difference: a PD controller differentiates the *error*, while this multiplies a measured
*state* by its own gain, chosen jointly with `K_POS` from the model. What is the reference velocity
here, and why is zero the right choice for a hold?

<Solution>

Zero, because a hold wants the elevator to arrive stopped — the desired velocity at the target *is*
nothing. So the velocity error is $0 - v$, and the term is pure damping proportional to how fast the
mechanism is currently moving.

The structural difference is where the two gains come from. A PD controller's `kD` is tuned
independently of `kP`; here `K_POS` and `K_VEL` are solved together from one model, so they are
guaranteed consistent with each other. Bolt a D term onto a P controller and nothing enforces that
relationship.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why kG sits outside the feedback">

Gravity is a known, constant disturbance. Work out what the feedback would have to do to cancel it on
its own, and what steady-state error would remain.

<Solution>

Feedback only produces output in response to error, so cancelling a constant load with `K_POS` alone
requires a permanent position error large enough that `K_POS * error` equals the holding voltage —
the elevator sits below its target forever, by exactly $k_G / K_{POS}$. Adding `kG` outside the loop
supplies the known part directly, leaving the feedback to handle only what the model did not predict.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why the velocity must be filtered">

Work out what "filtered encoder derivative" is buying you here, then go implement it from the
[low-pass lesson](../signal-processing/low-pass.mdx).

<Solution>

Differencing raw ticks over a 20 ms loop turns one tick of quantization into a large apparent velocity,
and `K_VEL` multiplies that straight into the motor command every loop. The mechanism buzzes and the
feedback gain you can safely run drops — so the filter is not cosmetic, it is what makes `K_VEL`
usable at all.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Callout type="idea" title="You have been doing scalar state feedback all along.">
A P controller on velocity with a kV feedforward is state feedback on a one-state system. The
state-space framing did not change the code; it changed where the numbers come from and how far the
same recipe stretches when the mechanism gets more states.
</Callout>

</Deploy>

<Admonition type="warning" title="Hardware reality: the model is the contract">

State feedback trusts the model with more authority than PID ever did. A wrong ticks-per-unit
conversion, a $k_A$ fitted from noisy data, or an unmodeled game element in the intake all corrupt the
gain design silently. Re-run [system identification](../control-theory/system-identification.mdx) when
the mechanism changes, and keep the feedback term doing cleanup work, never the bulk of the effort.

</Admonition>
