# PID Control

<Difficulty level="Medium" />

<Abstract>
PID adds up three reactions to the error: one to how big it is now, one to how much has built up over
time, and one to how fast it's changing. This lesson builds each term from a worked example, shows why
the derivative term behaves like a shock absorber, and ends with PDFL, a version popular in FTC that
replaces the integral with feedforward and a friction term.
</Abstract>

You'll use PID for arm position, flywheel speed, heading hold, and lift height, probably more than any
other controller. The name makes it sound complicated, but each of the three terms answers a simple
question about the error.

<Problem>

## Proportional control stops short

In [Control & Feedback](../preface/control-and-feedback.mdx) you met the proportional rule,
$u = k_P e$. Put it on an arm that has to hold itself out horizontally.

Say the arm needs 0.2 power to hold against gravity, and $k_P = 0.01$ power per degree. A proportional
controller only produces 0.2 power when the error is $0.2 / 0.01 = 20°$. So the arm settles 20° low and
stays there. Doubling $k_P$ halves the droop to 10°, but it also makes the arm more likely to overshoot
and oscillate, and the droop never reaches zero. A heavier game element or a sagging battery makes it
worse.

Proportional control only looks at the error right now. To do better, the controller also needs to
look at how the error has behaved over time and how fast it's changing.

</Problem>

<Theory>

## Three questions about the error

Follow one arm through a few loops. The target is 90°, and each loop is 0.02 s.

| Loop | Measured angle | Error $e$ | Error added up, $\sum e\,\Delta t$ | Change in angle per second |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 70° | 20° | 0.40 °·s | — |
| 2 | 72° | 18° | 0.76 °·s | +100 °/s |
| 3 | 73.5° | 16.5° | 1.09 °·s | +75 °/s |

Each column answers a different question.

**How far off is it now?** That's the error, 16.5° on loop 3. The **P** term pushes in proportion to
it: $u_P = k_P e$.

**How long has it been off?** Add up each loop's error times the loop time. That running total keeps
growing as long as the arm stays low, so a term built from it, $u_I = k_I \sum e\,\Delta t$, keeps
pushing harder until the error is actually gone. That's the **I** term, and it's what removes the 20°
droop from the example above. In calculus this running total is the integral, $\int e\,dt$.

**How fast is it moving?** The angle went from 72° to 73.5° in 0.02 s, which is 75 °/s. If the arm is
closing in fast, you want to ease off before it overshoots. The **D** term pushes against that motion:
$u_D = -k_D \times (\text{rate of change of the measurement})$. In calculus the rate of change is the
derivative.

Put together:

$$
u = k_P\,e + k_I \int e\,dt - k_D \frac{dm}{dt}
$$

where $m$ is the measurement. You'll also see the D term written as $+k_D\,de/dt$, the rate of change of
the *error*. For a target that isn't moving, the two are the same thing, since the error only changes
when the measurement does. They differ when the target jumps, which comes up below.

<Callout type="idea" title="A mass on a spring">
<ul>
<li><strong>P is a spring.</strong> It pulls toward the target harder the farther away you are. A
spring with no damping would bounce back and forth forever.</li>
<li><strong>D is a shock absorber.</strong> It pushes against motion, harder the faster you move, which
is why it's often called velocity feedback. With suitable gains it lets the arm arrive and settle
instead of bouncing. For an ideal mass $m$ the boundary between overshooting and not is
$k_D = 2\sqrt{m k_P}$. Less damping than that overshoots. More settles without overshoot, just more
slowly.</li>
<li><strong>I moves where the spring rests.</strong> It slowly builds up whatever constant push the job
needs, like holding an arm against gravity, so the spring doesn't have to stay stretched to provide
it.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

In the demo, the drone has to hover at the dashed line while gravity pulls it down, which is the same
droop problem as the arm. Drag the target, change the gains, and watch each term's contribution.

<Callout type="idea" title="Three things to try">
<ol>
<li><strong>P only.</strong> The drone settles <em>below</em> the target. Without an integral term, it
can only produce the thrust to fight gravity by staying low.</li>
<li><strong>PID (tuned).</strong> The integral builds up exactly the extra thrust gravity needs, and the

<li><strong>Too much kP,</strong> then a wind gust. It overshoots and oscillates. Adding kD calms it

## Tuning

A procedure that works for most mechanisms: raise $k_P$ until the mechanism just starts to oscillate,
then back off a bit. Add $k_D$ to damp the overshoot. Add $k_I$ last, and only if there's still a
steady error you can't fix with feedforward. Log each term and the final output while you do it.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Try |
|---|---|---|
| Slow to respond | `kP` too low | raise `kP` |
| Overshoots and oscillates | `kP` too high or `kD` too low | lower `kP`, raise `kD` |
| Stops short of the target | no `kI`, or missing feedforward | add feedforward, then a little `kI` |
| Slow, wide oscillation | `kI` too high | lower `kI` |
| Big overshoot after a long move at full power | integral windup | add anti-windup (next lesson) |
| Jittery, twitchy | `kD` too high for the noise | lower `kD`, filter the velocity |

</Theory>

## PID in a loop

On the robot, the running total is a sum with one rectangle per loop, and the rate of change is the
difference between two readings divided by the measured $\Delta t$, as in
[From Math to a Loop](../software-architecture/discretization.mdx):

$$
u_k = k_P e_k + k_I \sum_{j} e_j \Delta t_j
- k_D \frac{m_k - m_{k-1}}{\Delta t}
$$

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="PID for an arm, derivative on the measurement">{`private double integral, lastMeasurement;
private boolean firstLoop = true;

// target and measurement in encoder ticks; dt is the measured loop time.
double update(double target, double measurement, double dt) {
    if (!Double.isFinite(dt) || dt <= 0) return 0;

    double error = target - measurement;
    integral += error * dt;

    double rate = firstLoop ? 0 : (measurement - lastMeasurement) / dt;
    lastMeasurement = measurement;
    firstLoop = false;

    double power = kP * error + kI * integral - kD * rate;
    return clamp(power, -1, +1);
}`}</JavaCode>

The motor for this arm runs in `RUN_WITHOUT_ENCODER`, because this method is the controller and its
output is a motor power. Keep `kP`, `kI`, and `kD` somewhere you can change them live, like FTC
Dashboard, so tuning doesn't mean redeploying.

Two details in that block matter on real hardware.

**The derivative uses the measurement, not the error.** Suppose the target jumps from 0 to 500 ticks in
one 20 ms loop. The error jumps by 500 too, so its rate of change is $500 / 0.02 = 25{,}000$ ticks per
second for one loop, and the D term slams the motor to full power even though the arm hasn't moved.
This is called derivative kick. The measurement only changes when the arm actually moves, so taking the
rate of change of the measurement gives the same damping without the kick. The minus sign is there
because, for a fixed target, the error goes down exactly as fast as the measurement goes up.

**The integral needs protection when the output is maxed out.** While the motor sits at full power the
error keeps adding up, and all that stored-up push comes out as overshoot later. The fix is covered in
[Actuator Saturation & Anti-Windup](./actuator-saturation-anti-windup.mdx).

Libraries like FTCLib include a PID class, which is fine to use, but check how it computes the
derivative. FTCLib's `PIDController` takes the rate of change of the error, so it will kick when the
target jumps.

<Exercise title="What an output of 3.7 means">

The controller computes 3.7. What does that mean, and what does the SDK do with it? And why does it
matter that the motor is in `RUN_WITHOUT_ENCODER` rather than a mode that treats the command as a
velocity or position request?

<Solution>

It means the controller wants 3.7 times full power, which doesn't exist, so the output is clipped to 1.
While it sits there, anything the integral adds is windup.

This method outputs a motor power. `RUN_USING_ENCODER` treats the command as a velocity target and
`RUN_TO_POSITION` as a position target, so sending the same number through those modes would change
what it means and give behavior this controller wasn't tuned for. Stacking loops can be done on
purpose, with an outer position loop feeding an inner velocity loop, but that's a design you tune
inner loop first, not something that should happen by accident.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Callout type="tip" title="Filter the velocity, not the position">
The D term multiplies a rate of change, and differencing encoder readings over a short loop turns one
tick of jitter into a big spike. A light low-pass filter on that computed velocity helps a lot. A noisy
analog sensor, like a distance sensor, needs filtering before you use it at all.
</Callout>

## PDFL: replacing the integral

Look back at the arm. The steady push it needed was for gravity, and gravity is predictable. You can
just supply it, instead of waiting for an integral to discover it slowly and then having to protect
that integral from windup. That's the idea behind a controller the FTC community calls **PDFL**, after
the video linked below: proportional, derivative, feedforward, and a lower limit, with no integral.

The F and L terms are the same things you met in [feedforward](./feedforward.mdx), under different
names:

- **P**: proportional to the error, as before.
- **D**: the derivative of the measurement, passed through a low-pass filter so encoder jitter doesn't
  get amplified. (See [low-pass filters](../signal-processing/low-pass.mdx).)
- **F**: a constant that holds the mechanism against gravity. This is $k_G$ from the feedforward
  lesson. On a slide it really is one constant. On an arm the hold changes with $\cos\theta$, so for an
  arm that sweeps a wide range, use $k_G\cos\theta$ instead and keep the rest.
- **L**: a small push in the direction of the error, just enough to overcome friction. This is $k_S$.
  It's turned off inside a small dead zone around the target, because there the error's sign flips
  every loop and the push would make the mechanism buzz.

$$
u = k_P\,e - k_D\,\dot m_\text{filtered} + k_F + k_L\,\operatorname{sign}(e)
$$

with the $k_L$ term only applied outside the dead zone.

<Callout type="idea" title="Measuring F and L">
Slowly raise the power by hand until the mechanism just starts to move up. Call that $u_\text{up}$.
Then lower the power until it just starts to creep down, and call that $u_\text{down}$. Going up, the
motor fights gravity and friction, so $u_\text{up} = k_F + k_L$. Going down, gravity helps and friction
still resists, so $u_\text{down} = k_F - k_L$. Solving:
$$
k_F = \frac{u_\text{up} + u_\text{down}}{2}, \qquad k_L = \frac{u_\text{up} - u_\text{down}}{2}.
$$
For example, if it starts moving up at 0.26 and creeps down at 0.14, then $k_F = 0.20$ and
$k_L = 0.06$. There's no single power that "holds it still," because friction lets it hold anywhere
between those two readings.
</Callout>

<VideoEmbed title="PDFL: Improving Upon PID" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/EKSDbWL7tIY?start=6" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EKSDbWL7tIY">
A walkthrough of these changes: swapping the integral for feedforward, differentiating the
measurement, and filtering the derivative, and why they work better than textbook PID on FTC
mechanisms.
</VideoEmbed>

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="PDFL: P, filtered D, constant F, static-friction L">{`// No integral term at all: F and L do that job explicitly.
double run(double target, double measurement, double dt) {
    dt = Math.max(MIN_DT, dt);          // measured, never assumed
    double error = target - measurement;

    double p = kP * error;

    double rawD = firstLoop ? 0 : -(measurement - lastMeasurement) / dt;
    double alpha = 1 - Math.exp(-dt / TAU);
    filteredD += alpha * (rawD - filteredD);
    double d = kD * filteredD;

    lastMeasurement = measurement;
    firstLoop = false;

    double f = kF;                      // a constant that holds against gravity
    double l = kL * Math.signum(error); // a static-friction floor

    if (Math.abs(error) < DEADZONE) return p + d + f;   // drop L near target
    return p + d + f + l;
}`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="How much jitter the filter removes">

The encoder jitters by one tick. At `dt = 0.02 s`, what velocity does that one tick look like? Then
check what `alpha` does as `dt` gets much larger than `TAU`.

<Solution>

One tick in 20 ms is 50 ticks per second of velocity that isn't real, multiplied by `kD` and sent to the
motor every loop. The filter averages it out at the cost of a little lag. As `dt` grows past `TAU`,
$e^{-dt/\tau}$ shrinks toward zero and `alpha` goes to 1, which means "so much time has passed, just use
the new value."

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why L turns off near the target">

`Math.signum(error)` flips every time the arm crosses the target. Trace three loops with the arm
wobbling one tick either side. What does the mechanism sound like?

<Solution>

`l` alternates between `+kL` and `-kL`, so the command jumps by $2k_L$ at 50 Hz around a target the
arm has basically already reached. It buzzes and the gearbox takes the load for nothing. The dead zone
turns the friction push off once the arm is close enough that it doesn't matter.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

With $k_F$ holding the load and $k_L$ breaking friction, there's nothing to wind up.
