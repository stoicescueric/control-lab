# PID Control

<Difficulty level="Medium" />

<Abstract>
PID is the most widely used feedback controller: it sums three reactions to the error — proportional to its
present value, its accumulated past, and its rate of change — into a single command. This lesson builds
each term from intuition — including *why* the derivative term lands you on the target with zero velocity —
then upgrades the loop to the controller the FTC community calls "PDFL": a constant feedforward and a static-friction floor
that replace the integral entirely, plus a low-pass-filtered derivative.
</Abstract>

PID is the controller you will deploy most often: arm position, flywheel velocity, drivetrain
heading, lift height. It is three reactions to a single error signal, summed into one command. The
name sounds scary; the idea behind it is simple.

<Problem>

## Proportional-only control leaves a standing error

Consider an arm that must hold itself horizontal against gravity — the worst case, where the full
weight acts at the end of the moment arm. The intuitive controller is *proportional* —
command in proportion to the error, $u = K_p e$. It has a structural flaw: to produce the steady
holding torque gravity demands, it **requires a non-zero error to exist**. The arm settles a few
degrees low and stays there. Raising $K_p$ shrinks the gap but invites overshoot and oscillation,
and it still never reaches zero. Disturbances — a game element's weight, a sagging battery — make it
worse. We need terms that address the error's *history* and its *trend*, not just its present value.

</Problem>

<Theory>

## Three terms acting on present, past, and future

The error is the gap between target and measurement, $e(t) = \text{setpoint} - \text{measurement}$.
PID sums three responses to it:

- **P — proportional** · *the present.* $u_P = K_p\,e(t)$ — push in proportion to the current gap.
- **I — integral** · *the past.* $u_I = K_i \int_0^t e(\tau)\,d\tau$ — accumulate effort until a
  persistent gap is eliminated. This is what removes the standing error above.
- **D — derivative** · *the future.* $u_D = K_d \frac{de(t)}{dt}$ — anticipate: if the gap is
  closing quickly, ease off early to avoid overshoot.

$$
u(t) = K_p\,e(t) + K_i \int_0^t e(\tau)\,d\tau + K_d \frac{de(t)}{dt}
$$

<Callout type="idea" title="What the three terms really are: a mass on a spring.">
<ul>
<li><strong>P is the spring.</strong> It pulls toward the target with a force proportional to how far off
you are. A spring on its own overshoots and oscillates forever.</li>
<li><strong>D is the shock absorber.</strong> It opposes <em>motion</em>. Because the well-behaved version
differentiates the measurement, $u_D = -K_d\,\dot m$ is literally <strong>velocity feedback</strong>: the
faster you're moving, the harder it brakes. So D does more than "reduce overshoot" — it makes the system
<strong>arrive at the target with ≈ zero velocity</strong> instead of racing past it. P + D forms a
mass-spring-damper, but it is critically damped only for a particular gain-to-plant relationship. For an
ideal mass $m$, that boundary is $K_d = 2\sqrt{mK_p}$; less damping overshoots and more damping settles
without overshoot but more slowly.</li>
<li><strong>I shifts the rest position.</strong> It slowly builds whatever constant push the job needs
(holding an arm against gravity) so the spring no longer has to hold a standing error to make that
force.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

The gains $K_p, K_i, K_d$ are the tunable parameters. The drone below must hover at the dashed
target while gravity pulls it down — the classic "standing error" problem. Drag the target, tune
the gains, and read the live contribution of each term.

<Drone />

<Callout type="idea" title="Three experiments that isolate each term:">
<ol>
<li><strong>"P only"</strong> — the drone stabilises <em>below</em> target. With no integral term it
cannot generate the constant thrust that cancels gravity without holding an error. This is
steady-state error, made visible.</li>
<li><strong>"PID (tuned)"</strong> — the integral term accumulates exactly enough extra thrust to
cancel gravity; the drone settles on target. Watch the yellow I-meter climb.</li>
<li><strong>"Too much Kp"</strong> + a <strong>wind gust</strong> — overshoot and oscillation. The
derivative term is what damps it.</li>
</ol>
</Callout>

**Tuning** is the engineering. A reliable procedure: raise $K_p$ until the mechanism just begins to
oscillate, then back off; add $K_d$ to damp the overshoot; add $K_i$ last, only if a standing error
remains.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Slow to respond | `kP` too low | raise `kP` |
| Overshoots & oscillates | `kP` high / `kD` low | lower `kP`, raise `kD` |
| Never reaches target | no / low `kI` | add a little `kI` |
| Slow drift, sluggish wobble | `kI` too high | lower `kI` |
| Big overshoot after a long saturated move | integral windup | add anti-windup (next lesson) |
| Jittery, twitchy | `kD` too high (noise) | lower `kD`, filter the input |

### Core checkpoint: tune and log one feedback loop

Stop here only after you can define `error = target - measurement`, tune P then D, add I only for a
measured persistent bias, and log each term plus the saturated output. The deploy block turns that
artifact into measured-loop Java.

</Theory>

## A discrete PID loop, hardened for the Control Hub

The hub can't integrate or differentiate, so discretise: the integral becomes a running sum, the
derivative a difference over the measured $\Delta t$.

$$
u_k = K_p e_k + K_i \sum_{j} e_j \Delta t
- K_d \frac{m_k - m_{k-1}}{\Delta t}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'u_k, e_k', meaning: 'the command and the error at loop k'},
    {symbol: 'j', meaning: 'a counter over past loops for the running sum. Not a physical quantity'},
    {symbol: 'm_k, m_{k-1}', meaning: 'the MEASUREMENT this loop and last loop. The derivative is taken on m rather than on e, which is why a minus sign appears in front of K_d'},
    {symbol: 'the minus sign', meaning: 'this term is velocity feedback: it always opposes the direction the mechanism is moving, on either side of the target. Differentiating the error instead would spike every time the target moves'},
  ]}
/>

Here $m_k$ is the measurement. This is derivative-on-measurement, so a setpoint step does not create a
derivative kick.

Two refinements are mandatory on real hardware:

- **Derivative on measurement.** Differentiating the *error* spikes the output whenever the setpoint
  changes ("derivative kick"). Differentiate the *measurement* instead — $-K_d\,\dot{m}$ — for the
  same damping without the kick. (The drone above already does this with $-K_d\,\dot{y}$.)
- **Anti-windup.** When the actuator is saturated, stop accumulating the integral; otherwise it
  "winds up" and causes a large overshoot once the target is reached. The full implementation lives in
  [Actuator Saturation & Anti-Windup](./actuator-saturation-anti-windup.mdx).

FTCLib provides a convenient baseline controller, and FTC Dashboard tunes the gains live over Wi-Fi.
Treat the refinements above as production requirements: verify whether your library implements them
the way your mechanism needs, or use the explicit anti-windup controller in the next lesson.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="A baseline PID loop for an arm">{`// Expose the gains for live tuning, not as constants buried in the file.
public static double kP = 0.004, kI = 0, kD = 0.0002;

private PIDController pid;
private DcMotorEx arm;

void setup() {
    arm = hardwareMap.get(DcMotorEx.class, "arm");
    arm.setMode(RUN_WITHOUT_ENCODER);   // YOU are the controller now
    pid = new PIDController(kP, kI, kD);
}

void update(double targetTicks) {
    pid.setPID(kP, kI, kD);             // so a tuning edit lands this loop
    double power = pid.calculate(arm.getCurrentPosition(), targetTicks);
    arm.setPower(Math.max(-1, Math.min(1, power)));
}`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="The clamp is not decoration">

Work out what a PID output of 3.7 means, and what the SDK does with it. Then explain why this example
uses `RUN_WITHOUT_ENCODER`: the controller's output is motor power, so what would change if that command
were sent through a mode that interprets it as a velocity or position request?

<Solution>

3.7 means the controller wants 3.7 times full power, which does not exist — `setPower` clips it to 1.0
silently. The output is saturated, and everything the integrator accumulates while it sits there is
windup, which is the subject of the next lesson.

For the mode: this controller computes a direct motor-power command, so `RUN_WITHOUT_ENCODER` matches
the actuator model used by the lesson. `RUN_USING_ENCODER` regulates velocity and `RUN_TO_POSITION`
regulates position; sending the same output through those modes changes what the command means and can
produce behavior this outer loop did not model.

Nested loops are not inherently wrong. A deliberately designed cascade can use an outer position loop
to command an inner velocity loop. In that design, each loop has a defined measurement and output and
the faster inner loop is tuned before the slower outer loop. It is different from stacking two
position controllers or treating a power command as though it were a velocity request.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Callout type="tip" title="Filters and controllers are a team.">
The derivative term amplifies sensor noise. Production controllers run the measurement through a
low-pass filter first — a noisy encoder or distance sensor will ruin a D term. Clean the signal,
then control it.
</Callout>

### Production extension: the PDFL controller

Notice the arm problem never actually needed an *integral*. The steady push gravity demands is
*predictable*, so you can supply it directly and skip the slow, windup-prone I term. That is the idea
behind the controller the FTC community calls **PDFL** (from the video linked below) — **P**roportional,
**D**erivative, **F**eedforward, and an **L**ower-limit (static-friction) term, with **no integral at
all**. The name is community shorthand rather than standard control vocabulary; the pieces are the same
$k_G$ and $k_S$ you meet in [feedforward](./feedforward.mdx), rearranged to replace the integral:

- **P**: proportional to the error, exactly as before.
- **D**: the derivative, **low-pass filtered** (an exponential moving average) so encoder noise doesn't
  ruin it; the filter lives *inside* the D term (see [low-pass filters](../signal-processing/low-pass.mdx)).
  It is still the velocity feedback that brings you in at zero velocity.
- **F**: a **constant** feedforward: the steady power that holds the mechanism in place against gravity.
  It does the integral's old job instantly, with nothing to wind up. On a **slide** that really is one
  constant. On an **arm** the hold varies as $\cos\theta$, so a constant `kF` is only right near the angle
  you measured it at — for an arm that sweeps a wide range, swap it for the $k_G\cos\theta$ term from
  [feedforward](./feedforward.mdx) and keep the rest of PDFL unchanged.
- **L**: a **static-friction floor**: a fixed $k_L\,\operatorname{sign}(e)$ that shoves *just hard enough*
  to break stiction in the direction you need to move. It is applied only **outside a small dead zone**
  around the target — inside it the sign of the error flips every loop, so dropping L there stops the
  mechanism from buzzing.

$$
u = \underbrace{k_P\,e}_{P} - \underbrace{k_D\,\dot m_\text{filtered}}_{D}
  + \underbrace{k_F}_{F} + \underbrace{k_L\,\operatorname{sign}(e)}_{L\ \text{(outside the dead zone)}}
$$

    {symbol: 'k_L', meaning: 'a static-friction floor: a constant push in the direction of the error, applied only outside the dead zone'},

  ]}
/>

<Callout type="idea" title="How to actually find F and L.">
$k_F$ is the term you'd like to tune first, but it is hard to read off directly. The method: slowly raise the
command by hand until you find the power <strong>just as the mechanism starts to move up</strong>
($u_\text{up}$), then the power <strong>just as it starts to move down</strong> ($u_\text{down}$). Going
up you fight gravity <em>and</em> friction ($u_\text{up} = k_F + k_L$); going down gravity helps while
friction still resists ($u_\text{down} = k_F - k_L$). Solve the pair:
$$
k_F = \frac{u_\text{up} + u_\text{down}}{2}, \qquad k_L = \frac{u_\text{up} - u_\text{down}}{2}.
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'u_up', meaning: 'the smallest command that just moves the mechanism UP, found by experiment. Gravity and friction both oppose you here'},
    {symbol: 'u_down', meaning: 'the smallest command that just moves it DOWN. Now gravity helps while friction still resists, which is why the two differ'},
  ]}
/>
The <strong>average</strong> is the gravity hold; <strong>half the difference</strong> is the friction.
Two measurements give you both gains.
</Callout>

measurement, and low-pass-filtering the derivative — and why they beat a textbook PID on FTC mechanisms.
</VideoEmbed>

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="PDFL: P, filtered D, constant F, static-friction L">{`// No integral term at all: F and L do that job explicitly.
double run(double target, double measurement, double dt) {
    dt = Math.max(MIN_DT, dt);          // measured, never assumed
    double error = target - measurement;

    double p = kP * error;

    double rawD = firstLoop ? 0 : -(measurement - lastMeasurement) / dt;
    double alpha = 1 - Math.exp(-dt / TAU);
    filteredD += alpha * (rawD - filteredD);
    double d = kD * filteredD;

    lastMeasurement = measurement;
    firstLoop = false;

    double f = kF;                      // a constant that holds against gravity
    double l = kL * Math.signum(error); // a static-friction floor

    if (Math.abs(error) < DEADZONE) return p + d + f;   // drop L near target
    return p + d + f + l;
}`}</JavaCode>

Tune `kF` and `kL` by measurement rather than by guessing, using the two-reading method above: find the
smallest power that just moves the arm **up** ($u_\text{up}$), then lower the command until the arm just
starts to creep **down** — the largest power at which it still descends is $u_\text{down}$. Then `kF` is
their average and `kL` is half their difference. Note that there is no single "power that holds the arm
still" to read off: static friction holds it anywhere between those two readings. That band is
$2k_L$ wide, so `kL` is *half* of it — which is exactly the formula above.

<Exercise title="Two ideas hidden in the D term">

Both are worth deriving.

First, why the derivative is taken on the *measurement* and negated, rather than on the error. Step the
target from 0 to 500 in one loop and compute $d(\text{error})/dt$. What does that spike do to the motor?

Second, why `rawD` is filtered at all. Differencing a quantized encoder over a short `dt` amplifies
noise — work out the velocity implied by a one-tick jitter at `dt = 0.02 s`, and confirm that
$\alpha \to 1$ as `dt` grows past `TAU`.

<Solution>

A 500-tick step in one 20 ms loop makes $d(\text{error})/dt$ equal 25,000 ticks/s — a single enormous
spike from the *target* moving, not the arm. The motor slams to full power for one loop and the
mechanism jerks. Differentiating the measurement instead means the derivative only ever responds to
what the arm actually did; the negation is because $\frac{d}{dt}(\text{target} - x) = -\dot x$ when the
target is constant. Look up "derivative kick".

For the filter: one tick over 20 ms is 50 ticks/s of pure noise, multiplied by `kD` and fed straight to
the motor every loop. The low-pass averages it away at the cost of a little lag, and as `dt` grows past
`TAU` the exponential term collapses so $\alpha \to 1$ — meaning "so much time has passed, just take
the new reading."

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why L is dropped near the target">

`Math.signum(error)` flips every time the arm crosses the setpoint. Trace three loops with the arm
oscillating one tick either side of the target, and describe the noise the mechanism makes.

<Solution>

`l` flips between `+kL` and `-kL` on alternating loops, so the command jumps by $2k_L$ at 50 Hz around
a target the arm has effectively already reached. The mechanism buzzes audibly and the gearbox takes the
load for nothing. `DEADZONE` exists so the friction term switches off once the arm is close enough that
breaking friction no longer matters.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

This mirrors the widely shared FTC `PDFL` controller. There is no integral term at all: $k_F$ holds the
steady load and $k_L$ breaks friction, so there is nothing to wind up.

PID reacts only to error that has *already* happened. Pair it with the
[feedforward](./feedforward.mdx) — feedforward for the bulk of the command, PID for the residual it
can't predict — and you have the control stack behind nearly every fast, accurate FTC mechanism.

From here the module branches into the techniques that make that stack *good*. For a high-inertia
flywheel, a far simpler controller often beats PID — [**bang-bang**](./bang-bang.mdx) (and its
take-back-half upgrade). To
move a mechanism fast *without* overshoot, stop feeding it a step and feed it a
[**motion profile**](./motion-profiling.mdx) instead. And to keep every one of these behaving identically
from a full battery to a sagging one, wrap them all in [**voltage compensation**](./voltage-compensation.mdx).
