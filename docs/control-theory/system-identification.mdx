# System Identification

<Difficulty level="Hard" />

<Abstract>
System identification replaces guessed feedforward constants with measured ones. You drive the
mechanism with known voltages, log the speed and acceleration it reaches, and fit
$V = k_S\,\mathrm{sign}(v) + k_V v + k_A a$ to the data. A slow ramp gives $k_S$ and $k_V$ as the
intercept and slope of a line, and a second, faster test gives $k_A$. FRC's SysId tool automates the
same procedure.
</Abstract>

<Admonition type="note" title="Before you start">

Read [Feedforward](./feedforward.mdx) first, and [Signals & Noise](../signal-processing/signals.mdx) for
the difference between random noise and a steady bias. The math here is a line of best fit: a scatter
plot, its slope and intercept, and how far the points sit from the line. The derivation of the fitting
formulas is an optional exercise.

</Admonition>

The [feedforward](./feedforward.mdx) lesson left a question open: where do $k_S$, $k_V$, and $k_A$ come
from? A guess, or another team's numbers, gives a model that's wrong by some unknown amount, and then
feedback has to cover the difference, which is the problem feedforward was supposed to solve. The
reliable answer is to measure them on your own robot.

<Problem>

## You can't trust a guessed model

You can estimate $k_V \approx 12\,\text{V} \div v_\text{max}$ from the top speed, but real mechanisms
have friction, gearbox losses, and motors that don't match the datasheet. If the feedforward is 20% off,
feedback quietly makes up the difference. The mechanism still works, but it's sluggish and you won't
know why. You want the actual constants for this robot, and some idea of how much to trust them.

</Problem>

## Running the test safely

Set the experiment up before worrying about the math:

1. Test a drivetrain on the floor, in a clear lane. For a mechanism, secure the robot but leave the part
   you're measuring free to move. Don't characterize a drivetrain with its wheels in the air.
2. Set a voltage limit and keep a way to stop immediately.
3. Measure the battery voltage instead of assuming 12 V.
4. Pick one velocity unit and log time, applied volts, velocity, and acceleration every loop.
5. Throw out the samples from before the mechanism starts moving.
6. Fit the data afterwards, then check the constants on a separate run.

:::tip[Predict first]
Before pressing **Run a new test**, guess where the fitted intercept will land. Then set **Noise level**
to zero (which turns off both voltage and velocity noise) and run it. There's still a small error in the
intercept. It comes from how fast the ramp rises compared with how slowly the mechanism responds, not
from the demo running at 12× speed.
:::

<Theory>

## Fitting a line to the data

From [feedforward](./feedforward.mdx), the voltage to hold velocity $v$ while accelerating at $a$ is

$$
V = k_S\,\operatorname{sign}(v) + k_V\,v + k_A\,a
$$

The unknowns, $k_S$, $k_V$, and $k_A$, only appear multiplied by things you can measure, so you can fit
them with ordinary line fitting. The trick is designing tests that isolate them.

### The slow ramp gives kS and kV

Raise the voltage very slowly, so slowly that the mechanism is barely accelerating and $k_A a$ is close
to zero. Moving forward, the model becomes a straight line:

$$
V = k_S + k_V\,v
$$

Plot every logged sample with velocity across and voltage up. The points fall roughly on a line. Its
slope is $k_V$, the extra volts per unit of speed. Where it crosses $v = 0$ is $k_S$, the friction
voltage.

To see where the fitting formulas come from, take three samples: $(v, V) = (10, 2.4), (20, 3.9),
(30, 5.4)$. The average velocity is $\bar v = 20$ and the average voltage is $\bar V = 3.9$. Each sample's
distance from those averages is $(-10, -1.5)$, $(0, 0)$, $(10, 1.5)$. The slope is how much voltage
changes per unit of velocity across the samples:

$$
k_V = \frac{(-10)(-1.5) + (0)(0) + (10)(1.5)}{(-10)^2 + 0^2 + 10^2} = \frac{30}{200} = 0.15
$$

and the line has to pass through the average point, so
$k_S = \bar V - k_V \bar v = 3.9 - 0.15(20) = 0.9$ V. In general, with $n$ samples, that's the
**ordinary least squares** fit:

$$
k_V = \frac{\sum (v_i - \bar v)(V_i - \bar V)}{\sum (v_i - \bar v)^2},
\qquad
k_S = \bar V - k_V\,\bar v
$$

The bars mean averages over all the samples, and the subscript $i$ numbers the samples. It's the line
that makes the total of the squared vertical misses as small as possible.

In the demo, each blue dot is a logged sample, the amber line is the fit, and the green dot where it
crosses $v = 0$ is $k_S$. Turn the noise up and watch the estimate wobble. More samples help with
random noise but not with everything. Least squares assumes the velocity readings are accurate. This
demo adds noise to both axes, and noise in velocity tends to pull the slope toward zero. Turn noise off
to see the small bias that's left from the ramp, and always check a real fit on a separate run.

<SystemId />

### A step test gives kA

At a steady speed $k_A$ does nothing, so the ramp can't see it. Run a second test: jump to a fixed
voltage and log the acceleration as the mechanism speeds up. Rearranging the model,

$$
k_A = \frac{V - k_S - k_V\,v}{a}
$$

Don't compute that for each sample, though. Near the end of the step the acceleration gets close to zero
and the division blows up. Instead, fit all the accelerating samples at once, with columns
$[\operatorname{sign}(v_i), v_i, a_i]$ and target $V_i$. One least-squares solve with three unknowns
returns $k_S$, $k_V$, and $k_A$ together.

The sign column also lets you fit forward and reverse data together, since it flips $k_S$ with the
direction of travel. That assumes friction is the same both ways. If you're not sure it is, fit each
direction separately and compare. A big difference is worth knowing about.

<Callout type="idea" title="It's just line fitting">
For a motor, system identification is fitting a line, or a plane, to data. The math is the easy part.
The skill is in designing tests that isolate one term at a time.
</Callout>

</Theory>

<Deploy>

## Running the ramp and fitting it

The demo ramps at $0.10\ \mathrm{V/s}$ in real time. With a mechanism that takes about $0.35\ \mathrm{s}$
to respond, that adds at most about $0.035\ \mathrm{V}$ of error to the intercept. The display runs 12×
faster so you don't have to wait two minutes, but the simulated mechanism runs at normal speed.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Quasistatic ramp: collect (velocity, voltage) samples">{`// Raise the command slowly and log every loop.
void rampStep(double elapsedSeconds) {
    double vbat = batteryVoltage();
    double applied = Math.min(RAMP_VOLTS_PER_SEC * elapsedSeconds / vbat, 1.0);
    motor.setPower(applied);

    double v = motor.getVelocity() * UNITS_PER_TICK;   // real units, not ticks/s
    double volts = applied * vbat;                     // estimated applied voltage
    samples.add(new Sample(v, volts));
}`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="How slow is slow enough?">

The ramp has to be slow enough that acceleration hardly matters. How slow is that for your mechanism,
and what would the fit show if you ramped too fast?

<Solution>

Slow enough that $k_A a$ is tiny next to $k_V v$. In practice that means reaching top speed over tens of
seconds, not one. If you ramp too fast, the voltage spent on acceleration ends up in the fit, and it's
worth seeing where. A ramp rising at $c$ volts per second settles into a nearly constant acceleration
$a \approx c/k_V$, so the missing $k_A a$ term is a constant, and a constant shifts the line's
**intercept**, not its slope:

$$
V = \underbrace{\left(k_S + \frac{k_A c}{k_V}\right)}_{\text{intercept, too high by } c\,\tau} + k_V\,v
$$

So a too-fast ramp gives a $k_S$ that's too high, by about the ramp rate times the mechanism's time
constant $\tau = k_A/k_V$. That's the $0.035\ \mathrm{V}$ above. Every feedforward built on it then
sends more voltage than the mechanism needs. The slope barely changes, which is why this is easy to
miss: the number you'd think to check looks fine. If $k_S$ comes out suspiciously large, slow the ramp
down before blaming friction.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why log the applied voltage">

`volts` is the applied power times the measured battery voltage, not the voltage you asked for. Why are
those different?

<Solution>

Two reasons. The command is clamped at 1.0, so past that point you keep asking for more and the motor
keeps getting the same. And the battery sags under load, so the same power fraction gives different
voltages during the test. Fitting against the requested voltage fits a line to numbers the motor never
saw.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Ordinary least squares -> kS (intercept), kV (slope)">{`// Fit V = kS + kV * v to the logged samples.
double[] fitLine(List<Sample> samples) {
    if (samples.size() < 3) return null;

    double meanV = 0, meanVolts = 0;
    for (Sample s : samples) {
        meanV += s.v;
        meanVolts += s.volts;
    }
    meanV /= samples.size();
    meanVolts /= samples.size();

    double sXY = 0, sXX = 0;
    for (Sample s : samples) {
        sXY += (s.v - meanV) * (s.volts - meanVolts);
        sXX += (s.v - meanV) * (s.v - meanV);
    }
    if (!Double.isFinite(sXX) || sXX < MIN_SPREAD) return null;

    double kV = sXY / sXX;                 // the slope
    double kS = meanVolts - kV * meanV;    // the intercept
    return new double[] {kS, kV};
}`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="Where the formulas come from (optional, uses derivatives)">

Write the total squared miss $\sum (V - k_S - k_V v)^2$. Find the $k_S$ and $k_V$ that make it smallest
by taking its derivative with respect to each one and setting both to zero. Then say what $k_S$ and
$k_V$ mean physically, and what a fitted $k_S$ of zero would tell you.

<Solution>

Setting the $k_S$ derivative to zero gives $k_S = \bar V - k_V \bar v$ right away. Substituting that
into the $k_V$ equation leaves $k_V = S_{xy}/S_{xx}$, the two lines in the code.

$k_S$ is the friction voltage while moving, and $k_V$ is volts per unit of speed. A fitted $k_S$ near
zero could mean low friction, or noisy data, or a poor fit. It doesn't prove the mechanism starts
moving at zero volts. Look at how the points scatter around the line, and make sure the samples cover a
useful range of speeds.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why check the spread">

`sXX` measures how spread out the velocity samples are. What does a near-zero `sXX` do to the division
after it, and why is refusing to fit better than returning a number?

<Solution>

Zero spread means every sample had the same velocity, so there's no way to tell the slope from the
intercept. Dividing by zero gives a non-finite result that could end up as a motor command. Returning
`null` makes the failure obvious so the caller can reject it.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

In FRC, **SysId** runs the ramp and step tests, fits all three gains, and reports an $R^2$ that says how
well the model fits. In FTC, run a small ramp OpMode like the one above or use a library tuner. The math
is the same.

<Callout type="tip" title="Only fit the moving samples">
Throw away samples where the mechanism hasn't started moving yet, with velocity near zero while the
voltage climbs. They follow different physics and pull the intercept off. Start the fit from the first
sample with clearly nonzero velocity.
</Callout>

</Deploy>

<Admonition type="warning" title="Common ways a fit goes wrong">

Velocity that wasn't converted to real units, so $k_V$ ends up in volts per tick (see
[units](../software-architecture/discretization.mdx)). Forward and reverse samples mixed without the sign
column. And a sagging battery during the test, if you logged 12 V instead of `power × batteryVoltage`.
Even that is an estimate of the voltage at the motor: wiring losses and timing differences between the
voltage and velocity readings can make it a little off.

</Admonition>
