# System Identification

<Difficulty level="Hard" />

<Abstract>
System identification is how you replace guessed feedforward constants with measured ones. You drive the
mechanism through a set of known voltages, record the velocity and acceleration it actually reaches, and
fit the motor model $V = k_S\,\mathrm{sign}(v) + k_V v + k_A a$ to that data by ordinary least squares.
The intercept of the fit is $k_S$, the slope is $k_V$, and a separate accelerating test gives $k_A$ —
the same procedure FRC's SysId tool automates.
</Abstract>

The [feedforward](./feedforward.mdx) lesson left one thing on a promissory note: *where do $k_S$, $k_V$,
and $k_A$ come from?* Guessing them, or copying another team's, gives a model that is wrong by an unknown
amount — and a wrong model means feedback has to cover the gap, which was the whole problem feedforward
set out to solve. The honest answer is to **measure** them from your own robot.

<Problem>

## A model you guessed is a model you can't trust

You can hand-estimate $k_V \approx 12\,\text{V} \div v_\text{max}$ and call it a day, but real mechanisms
have friction you didn't account for, a gearbox with its own losses, and a motor whose constants drift
from the datasheet. If the feedforward prediction is 20% off, feedback silently makes up the difference —
so the mechanism still works, but it is sluggish and you have no idea why. We want the *actual* constants
for *this* robot, with a number that tells us how much to trust them.

</Problem>

## Safe acquisition workflow

Before the algebra, make the experiment safe and reproducible:

1. For a drivetrain, test on the floor in a clear, controlled lane. For a standalone mechanism, secure
   its fixture while leaving the intended output free to move. Do not characterize a drivetrain lifted.
2. Set a voltage limit and keep an immediate stop control available.
3. Measure battery voltage instead of assuming a nominal value.
4. Choose one physical velocity unit and log time, applied volts, velocity, and acceleration every loop.
5. Discard the stuck/static regime before fitting the moving model.
6. Fit all valid samples offline, then validate the constants on a separate run.

:::tip[Predict, run, inspect]
Before pressing **Run a new test**, predict where the fitted intercept should land. Set **Noise level**
to zero (which disables both voltage and velocity noise), run the accelerated display, then explain why the small residual intercept error is bounded by
the real-time ramp rate times the plant lag rather than by the 12× presentation speed.
:::

<Theory>

## Optional derivation: fit the motor model to measured data

Recall the model from [feedforward](./feedforward.mdx): to hold velocity $v$ and accelerate at $a$,

$$
V = k_S\,\operatorname{sign}(v) + k_V\,v + k_A\,a
$$

This is **linear in the unknowns** $k_S, k_V, k_A$ — which is exactly what makes it fittable. Run two
kinds of test and the constants fall out.

### The quasistatic ramp → kS and kV

Drive the mechanism with a *slowly* rising voltage, so slowly that acceleration voltage is negligible
($k_Aa \approx 0$). The model collapses to a straight line:

$$
V = k_S + k_V\,v \qquad (\text{moving forward, } a \approx 0)
$$

Log many $(v, V)$ pairs and fit the best line through them. With $n$ samples, **ordinary least squares**
gives the slope and intercept in closed form:

$$
k_V = \frac{\sum (v_i - \bar v)(V_i - \bar V)}{\sum (v_i - \bar v)^2},
\qquad
k_S = \bar V - k_V\,\bar v
$$

<EquationLegend
  title="Symbols in the least-squares fit"
  items={[
    {symbol: 'v_i, V_i', meaning: 'the i-th logged sample: a measured velocity and the voltage actually applied at that moment. The subscript numbers samples, not loops'},
    {symbol: 'v\u0304, V\u0304', meaning: 'the bar means the MEAN over all n samples. Both formulas are written as deviations from those means'},
    {symbol: 'n', meaning: 'how many samples you logged'},
    {symbol: 'the numerator', meaning: 'how much v and V vary TOGETHER; the denominator is how much v varies on its own. Their ratio is the slope of the best-fit line'},
  ]}
/>

The **intercept is $k_S$** (the voltage that produces motion at zero speed — pure friction) and the
**slope is $k_V$** (extra volts per unit speed — the back-EMF term). Below is the ramp test: each blue dot
is one logged sample, the amber line is the least-squares fit, and the green dot where it crosses $v = 0$
is $k_S$. Crank the noise up and watch the estimate wobble — then notice the fit still recovers the truth
on average, which is the point of using *many* samples instead of two.

<SystemId />

### The dynamic test → kA

$k_A$ never appears at steady speed, so the ramp can't see it. Run a second test: a **step** to a fixed
voltage and record the acceleration while the mechanism speeds up. Rearranging the model for the
accelerating phase,

$$
k_A = \frac{V - k_S - k_V\,v}{a}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'a', meaning: 'the measured acceleration during the step test. Everything else on the right is already known from the ramp test, so k_A is what is left over', unit: 'in/s\u00b2 or rad/s\u00b2'},
  ]}
/>

Do **not** divide each sample by $a$. Instead, fit every valid accelerating sample jointly with a design
row $[\operatorname{sign}(v_i),v_i,a_i]$ and response $V_i$; one multivariable least-squares solve returns
$k_S,k_V,k_A$ without exploding near $a=0$. A dynamic voltage step supplies acceleration that the
quasistatic ramp intentionally avoids. When combining forward and reverse data, the sign column keeps
static friction pointed correctly.

<Callout type="idea" title="This is just linear regression.">
"System identification" sounds exotic; for a motor it is fitting a line (or a plane) to data. The same
ordinary-least-squares machinery underlies the curve-fitting you may meet again in
[state estimation](../signal-processing/kalman.mdx). The art is in the *test design* — isolating one term
at a time — not the math.
</Callout>

</Theory>

<Deploy>

## Run the test, fit the line

The widget uses a real-time ramp of $0.10\ \mathrm{V/s}$ so its $0.35\ \mathrm{s}$ plant lag contributes
at most about $0.035\ \mathrm{V}$ of intercept bias. Simulation time is displayed 12× faster only so the
learner does not wait two minutes; the plant time constant and logged model remain unchanged.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Quasistatic ramp: collect (velocity, voltage) samples">{`// Raise the command slowly and log every loop.
void rampStep(double elapsedSeconds) {
    double vbat = batteryVoltage();
    double applied = Math.min(RAMP_VOLTS_PER_SEC * elapsedSeconds / vbat, 1.0);
    motor.setPower(applied);

    double v = motor.getVelocity() * UNITS_PER_TICK;   // real units, not ticks/s
    double volts = applied * vbat;                     // what was ACTUALLY applied
    samples.add(new Sample(v, volts));
}`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="How slow is quasistatic?">

"Quasistatic" means slow enough that acceleration is negligible, so the $k_A$ term drops out and what
is left is only $k_S$ and $k_V$. Work out how slow that has to be for your mechanism, and what the fit
would show if you ramped too fast.

<Solution>

Slow enough that $k_A a$ is small next to $k_V v$ — in practice, slow enough that the mechanism reaches
its top speed over tens of seconds rather than one. Ramp too fast and the extra volts spent on
acceleration never leave the fit, but watch *where* they land. Once the initial transient dies, a ramp
at $c$ volts per second accelerates at a nearly constant $a \approx c/k_V$, so the unmodeled $k_A a$
term is a **constant offset** — and a constant offset moves the line's **intercept**, not its slope:

$$
V = \underbrace{\left(k_S + \frac{k_A c}{k_V}\right)}_{\text{intercept, inflated by } c\,\tau} + k_V\,v
$$

So the symptom of a too-fast ramp is an inflated $k_S$, too large by roughly the ramp rate times the
mechanism's time constant — the same $0.035\ \mathrm{V}$ the widget above quantifies. Every downstream
feedforward then commands more voltage than the mechanism needs. The slope $k_V$ is barely affected,
which is exactly why this bug is easy to miss: the number you would think to check looks fine. If your
fitted $k_S$ comes out implausibly large, slow the ramp before you go hunting for friction.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Applied, not requested">

`volts` comes from the applied power times the measured battery, not from the voltage you asked for.
Work out why those two differ.

<Solution>

Two reasons, and both matter. The command is clamped at 1.0, so once the ramp passes full power you
keep asking for more and the motor keeps receiving the same thing. And the rail is not 12 V — it sags
under load, so the same duty cycle delivers different voltages over the test. Fitting against the
requested voltage fits a line to a number the motor never saw.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Ordinary least squares -> kS (intercept), kV (slope)">{`// Fit V = kS + kV * v to the logged samples.
double[] fitLine(List<Sample> samples) {
    if (samples.size() < 3) return null;

    double meanV = 0, meanVolts = 0;
    for (Sample s : samples) {
        meanV += s.v;
        meanVolts += s.volts;
    }
    meanV /= samples.size();
    meanVolts /= samples.size();

    double sXY = 0, sXX = 0;
    for (Sample s : samples) {
        sXY += (s.v - meanV) * (s.volts - meanVolts);
        sXX += (s.v - meanV) * (s.v - meanV);
    }
    if (!Double.isFinite(sXX) || sXX < MIN_SPREAD) return null;

    double kV = sXY / sXX;                 // the slope
    double kS = meanVolts - kV * meanV;    // the intercept
    return new double[] {kS, kV};
}`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="Derive the two lines">

Do not trust those formulas — derive them. Write the total squared error
$\sum (V - k_S - k_V v)^2$, differentiate with respect to $k_S$ and $k_V$, set both derivatives to
zero, and solve the pair. Then interpret the answers physically: what is $k_S$ in units, what is
$k_V$, and what would a fitted $k_S$ of zero say about your mechanism?

<Solution>

Setting $\partial/\partial k_S = 0$ gives $k_S = \bar V - k_V \bar v$ immediately; substituting that
into $\partial/\partial k_V = 0$ leaves $k_V = S_{xy}/S_{xx}$. Those are the normal equations, and
they are the two lines above.

$k_S$ is volts at zero speed — pure static friction, the voltage that buys you nothing but breaking the
mechanism loose. $k_V$ is volts per unit of speed, which is what back-EMF costs you. A fitted $k_S$ of
zero would mean the mechanism starts moving at any voltage above nothing, which no real gearbox does;
in practice it means you fitted samples from before the mechanism broke free.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why guard the spread">

`sXX` is the spread of the velocity samples. Work out what a near-zero `sXX` does to the division
below it, and why refusing to fit beats returning the number anyway.

<Solution>

Zero spread means every sample had the same velocity, and there is no line through a single point.
Dividing by it gives $\pm\infty$ or `NaN`, and `kS = meanVolts - kV * meanV` inherits it — so both
gains become `NaN`, the feedforward becomes `NaN`, and `setPower(NaN)` is a silent dead motor with no
error anywhere. A `null` return is a fit you can notice failed.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

In FRC this is one tool: **SysId** runs the quasistatic and dynamic tests, fits all three gains, and even
reports an $R^2$ so you know how well the model fits. FTC teams either run a small ramp OpMode like the
above or use a library tuner; the math underneath is identical.

<Callout type="tip" title="Only fit the moving data.">
Throw away samples where the mechanism hasn't broken static friction yet (velocity ≈ 0 while voltage
climbs) — they sit on a different regime and bias your intercept. Start the fit from the first sample with
clearly nonzero velocity.
</Callout>

</Deploy>

<Admonition type="warning" title="Hardware reality: units, direction, and a fresh battery">

Three ways a fit goes wrong: velocity not converted to real units (so $k_V$ is in nonsense per tick — see
[units](../software-architecture/discretization.mdx)); mixing forward and reverse samples (the
$k_S\,\mathrm{sign}(v)$ flips, so fit each direction separately); and a sagging battery during the test
(always log the voltage *actually applied*, `power × batteryVoltage`, not the nominal 12 V).

</Admonition>
