# Feedforward (kS, kV, kA, kG)

<Difficulty level="Medium" />

<Abstract>
Feedforward is the predictive half of a controller: instead of waiting for an error to appear, it uses
a model of the mechanism to command most of the required effort up front, leaving feedback to mop up
the rest. This lesson derives the kS, kV, and kA terms straight from the motor's physics and combines
them with PID, then extends the model to gravity-loaded FTC mechanisms: slides/elevators and arms.
</Abstract>

Feedback is reactive by construction. Feedforward is the predictive half: a model of the mechanism
that supplies most of the required command immediately, leaving feedback to correct only the small
residual. It is the difference between a sluggish velocity loop and a crisp one.

<Problem>

## A reactive controller must first fail to respond

A proportional-only controller needs an error to produce a command. On a velocity-controlled
mechanism — a flywheel or drivetrain at cruise — it therefore needs a steady error to supply the
holding voltage. Integral feedback can remove that steady error by retaining an accumulated command;
feedforward supplies the expected voltage immediately, reducing the correction feedback must learn.
Raising proportional gain alone reduces the error but can increase sensitivity to noise and overshoot.
The information to do better already exists: you *know* roughly what voltage a given speed requires.

<Analogy>
<strong>Cresting a hill.</strong> A skilled driver presses the accelerator <em>as</em> the hill
begins, anticipating the load. A reactive driver waits until the car has already slowed, then
over-corrects. Feedforward is the anticipation; feedback only cleans up the difference.
</Analogy>

</Problem>

<Theory>

## A physical model of the required voltage

To hold a target velocity $v$ (and optionally accelerate at $a$), the predicted voltage is:

$$
V = \underbrace{k_S\,\operatorname{sign}(v)}_{\text{break friction}}
  + \underbrace{k_V\,v}_{\text{hold the speed}}
  + \underbrace{k_A\,a}_{\text{accelerate}}
$$

<Formula>
  V = <Amber>kS·sign(v)</Amber> + <Teal>kV·v</Teal> + <Blue>kA·a</Blue>
</Formula>

These constants are not fudge factors — they are the [motor's own physics](./how-a-motor-works.mdx).
A DC motor obeys $V = IR + k_e\omega$: the back-EMF term $k_e\omega$ rises with speed (that is
$k_V v$), while the
current term supplies torque to overcome static friction (a constant, $k_S$) and to accelerate the
inertia, $\tau = J\alpha$ (that is $k_A a$).

| Term | Counteracts | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **kS**: static | friction / stiction | the minimum voltage to start motion |
| **kV**: velocity | back-EMF / drag | volts per unit speed — the bulk of the command |
| **kA**: acceleration | inertia (mass) | extra volts to change speed; only while the target's velocity is changing — zero during cruise |

The usual $k_S$ model combines friction into one offset. A fit from moving samples estimates
effective moving friction; actual breakaway stiction can be larger and direction-dependent.

## Core checkpoint: calculate one velocity command

Given identified $k_S$, $k_V$, and $k_A$, calculate the voltage for one target velocity and acceleration,
then clamp it to the available battery voltage. That model-plus-limit is the reusable core; gravity terms
below extend it for mechanisms whose load changes with pose.

## Production extension: add mechanism gravity

The flywheel model is missing one thing many FTC mechanisms care about: **weight**. A flywheel does not
need voltage to hold still against gravity. A vertical slide does. An arm definitely does, and the amount
changes with angle.

<MechanismFeedforward />

### Slides and elevators: gravity is mostly constant

For a vertical slide, define:

- $h$ as slide height.
- $v = \dot h$ as slide velocity, positive upward.
- $a = \ddot h$ as slide acceleration.
- $k_G$ as the voltage needed to hold the slide still against gravity.

The ideal vertical-slide feedforward is:

$$
\boxed{
V_{slide} =
k_S\,\operatorname{sign}(v)
+ k_G
+ k_V\,v
+ k_A\,a
}
$$

The important detail is that $k_G$ does **not** flip sign when the slide moves downward. Static friction
depends on direction, so $k_S\operatorname{sign}(v)$ changes. Gravity still pulls down, so the motor still
needs an upward holding term.

Real FTC slides are not always ideal elevators. String wrap diameter, cable routing, elastic tension,
carriage friction, arm coupling, or changing extension geometry can make the required hold voltage drift
with height. When one constant $k_G$ is not enough, measure the hold voltage at several heights and
interpolate:

$$
V_{slide} =
k_S\,\operatorname{sign}(v)
+ k_G(h)
+ k_V\,v
+ k_A\,a
$$

Between two calibration points $(h_i, k_{G,i})$ and $(h_{i+1}, k_{G,i+1})$:

$$
\boxed{
k_G(h)
=
k_{G,i}
+
\frac{h - h_i}{h_{i+1} - h_i}
\left(k_{G,i+1} - k_{G,i}\right)
}
$$

<EquationLegend
  title="Symbols in the gravity interpolation"
  items={[
    {symbol: 'h', meaning: 'the slide height you are asking about right now', unit: 'in'},
    {symbol: 'h_i, h_{i+1}', meaning: 'the two measured heights that BRACKET h. The subscript numbers a calibration point, not a loop'},
    {symbol: 'k_G,i', meaning: 'the gravity hold at height h_i: the AVERAGE of the up-break and down-break commands, not the smallest command that holds. Stiction makes "holds still" a band, and its bottom edge is short by k_S', unit: 'V'},
    {symbol: '(h \u2212 h_i)/(h_{i+1} \u2212 h_i)', meaning: 'how far between the two calibration points h sits, as a fraction from 0 to 1. This is ordinary linear interpolation'},
  ]}
/>

where $h$ is the current or target slide height, $h_i$ and $h_{i+1}$ are the two measured heights around
it, and $k_{G,i}$ / $k_{G,i+1}$ are the measured hold voltages at those heights.

### Arms: gravity changes with angle

For an arm, define:

- $\theta$ as the arm angle measured from **horizontal**.
- $\omega = \dot\theta$ as angular velocity.
- $\alpha = \ddot\theta$ as angular acceleration.
- $k_G$ as the voltage needed to hold the arm when it is horizontal.

Gravity torque is largest when the arm is horizontal and nearly zero when the arm is vertical. That gives
the arm feedforward:

$$
\boxed{
V_{arm} =
k_S\,\operatorname{sign}(\omega)
+ k_G\cos(\theta)
+ k_V\,\omega
+ k_A\,\alpha
}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: '\u03b8', meaning: 'the arm angle measured FROM HORIZONTAL, so cos\u03b8 is 1 when the arm sticks straight out and 0 when it points straight up'},
    {symbol: '\u03c9, \u03b1', meaning: 'the arm\u2019s angular velocity and angular acceleration. For a rotating joint these replace the linear v and a', unit: 'rad/s, rad/s\u00b2'},
    {symbol: 'k_G cos\u03b8', meaning: 'only the component of the arm\u2019s weight PERPENDICULAR to the link produces torque about the pivot, and that component scales with cos\u03b8'},
  ]}
/>

This convention matters. If your encoder reads zero somewhere else, convert it first:

$$
\theta_{horizontal} = \theta_{encoder} + \theta_{offset}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: '\u03b8_encoder', meaning: 'what the sensor reads, zeroed wherever the mechanism happened to be'},
    {symbol: '\u03b8_offset', meaning: 'the fixed constant that moves that zero to horizontal. Measure it once and it never changes unless the arm is re-mounted'},
  ]}
/>

Then use $\cos(\theta_{horizontal})$. If the sign is wrong, do not randomly negate the whole controller;
fix the angle convention and motor direction until a horizontal arm receives the expected upward holding
voltage.

The deployable pattern is **feedforward + feedback**: the model does ~90 % of the work instantly,
[PID](./pid.mdx) corrects the residual and rejects disturbances.

<Formula>
  output = <Teal>feedforward(goal)</Teal> + <Rose>PID(measurement, goal)</Rose>
</Formula>

In the demo, the motor must reach the dashed target speed. Toggle feedforward on and off with
identical gains; the bars show how the command splits between the
<span style={{ color: "#5ce08a" }}>feedforward prediction</span> and the
<span style={{ color: "#ffc24d" }}>feedback correction</span>.

<Flywheel />

<Callout type="idea" title="Three experiments:">
<ol>
<li>Feedforward <strong>ON</strong>, tiny kP — it reaches and holds the target; the green prediction
bar carries the load.</li>
<li>Feedforward <strong>OFF</strong>, same kP — it settles below target (steady-state error).
Raising kP to compensate makes it overshoot and twitch.</li>
<li>Mistune <strong>kV</strong> low — the prediction under-shoots and feedback must cover the gap;
the main benefit is lost. Accurate constants matter.</li>
</ol>
</Callout>

</Theory>

## Volts on a battery that sags

FTC motors take power in `-1..1`, but the model is in volts. Dividing by the *measured* battery
voltage keeps behaviour constant whether the pack is at 13 V or 11 V — see
[voltage compensation](./voltage-compensation.mdx).

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Feedforward + feedback, battery-compensated">{`void setup() {
    flywheel = hardwareMap.get(DcMotorEx.class, "flywheel");
    flywheel.setMode(RUN_WITHOUT_ENCODER);   // this class closes its own loop
}

// goalVelocity and goalAccel are in real units, e.g. metres/second.
void run(double goalVelocity, double goalAccel) {
    double measured = flywheel.getVelocity() * UNITS_PER_TICK;

    double predict = kS * Math.signum(goalVelocity)
            + kV * goalVelocity
            + kA * goalAccel;
    double correct = kP * (goalVelocity - measured);

    flywheel.setPower((predict + correct) / batteryVoltage());
}`}</JavaCode>

Read those two lines against each other. `predict` is computed entirely from the goal — it never looks
at the sensor, so it can act the instant you ask for a new speed. `correct` is computed from the error,
so by definition it can only act once the mechanism is already wrong.

<Exercise title="What each term does, by deleting it">

Work out each term's job on paper before tuning any of them:

- no `kS` — the flywheel never quite starts from rest. Why?
- no `kV` — the command does not scale with the speed you asked for.
- no `kA` — it tracks steady speeds fine but lags whenever the goal changes.

Then the consequence for tuning: with a good feedforward, what is left for `kP` to do, and why does
that let you run a *smaller* `kP` than you could with feedback alone?

<Solution>

`kS` is the voltage that buys nothing but breaking static friction, so without it every command is
short by exactly the amount needed to start moving. `kV` is the back-EMF cost, which scales with speed —
drop it and the model is a constant. `kA` covers the extra torque a *change* in speed demands, so
without it the model is only correct in steady state.

With all three, `kP` is left correcting model error and disturbance — a few percent, not the whole
command. A small `kP` is what you want: `kP` amplifies sensor noise and can only react after the fact,
so the less work it has to do, the less noise reaches the motor and the less it can overshoot.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Arm feedforward with a gravity term">{`// Angle convention: 0 means the arm is HORIZONTAL. If your encoder zero is
// elsewhere, add the offset before calling this. Output is volts.
double calculate(double angle, double velocity, double acceleration) {
    double staticVolts = Math.abs(velocity) < EPS
            ? 0
            : kS * Math.signum(velocity);

    return staticVolts
            + kG * Math.cos(angle)      // <- the gravity term
            + kV * velocity
            + kA * acceleration;
}`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="Derive the cosine">

The `cos(angle)` is the whole difference from a flywheel's feedforward. Derive it: gravity pulls
straight down with force $mg$, but only the component *perpendicular* to the arm produces torque about
the pivot. Draw the arm at some angle from horizontal, resolve the weight into components along and
across the arm, and read off which one turns the joint. Then check the formula at three angles you can
verify by eye — horizontal, straight up, and hanging straight down.

<Solution>

The component along the arm is carried by the pivot bearing and does no work; the perpendicular
component is $mg\cos\theta$, at a moment arm of the arm's length, so the holding torque goes as
$\cos\theta$.

Horizontal ($\theta = 0$): $\cos = 1$, maximum hold voltage — correct, this is the worst case.
Straight up ($90°$): $\cos = 0$, no hold voltage — correct, the weight is straight down through the
pivot. Hanging down ($-90°$): $\cos = 0$ again, for the same reason. The two zeros are physically very
different situations that happen to need the same voltage: straight up is an **unstable** equilibrium —
nudge it and the gravity torque grows and pulls the arm over — while hanging down is the **stable** one,
where gravity restores it. The feedforward is blind to that difference. Only feedback is not.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why zero the static term near rest">

`staticVolts` is forced to zero at near-zero velocity rather than just using `Math.signum(velocity)`.
What is `Math.signum(0)`, and what would a stationary arm do with that term flickering?

<Solution>

`Math.signum(0)` is 0, so the exact-zero case is already handled — the problem is the case *around*
zero. A stationary arm still has encoder velocity jittering between small positive and negative values,
so `signum` flips sign every few loops and the command jumps by $2k_S$ at loop rate. The mechanism
buzzes. The `EPS` band decides that "not really moving" means "do not apply a friction-breaking term at
all."

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Slide feedforward with interpolated gravity">{`// For a vertical slide where ONE constant kG is not accurate across the
// travel: rigging friction and stage engagement change with height.
private double[] heights;     // the heights you tested at
private double[] holdVolts;   // the voltage that held the slide at each one

double calculate(double height, double velocity, double acceleration) {
    double staticVolts = Math.abs(velocity) < EPS
            ? 0
            : kS * Math.signum(velocity);

    return staticVolts
            + interpolateHoldVolts(height)   // instead of a constant kG
            + kV * velocity
            + kA * acceleration;
}

double interpolateHoldVolts(double height) {
    if (heights.length < 2) return 0;
    if (height <= heights[0]) return holdVolts[0];

    for (int i = 0; i < heights.length - 1; i++) {
        if (height <= heights[i + 1]) {
            double t = (height - heights[i]) / (heights[i + 1] - heights[i]);
            return holdVolts[i] + t * (holdVolts[i + 1] - holdVolts[i]);
        }
    }
    return holdVolts[holdVolts.length - 1];   // clamp above the last sample
}`}</JavaCode>

Collect the table by measurement rather than derivation: drive the slide to each height and take **two**
readings — the smallest voltage that just moves it up, and the largest at which it still creeps down.
The **average** is $k_{G,i}$; half the difference is $k_S$ at that height. Do not use "the smallest
voltage that holds it stationary": stiction means it holds anywhere in a band, and the bottom edge of
that band is $k_G - k_S$, so the whole table comes out low and the separate $k_S$ term then
double-counts. This is the same two-reading method the [PID lesson](./pid.mdx) uses for `kF` and `kL`.
Five or six heights across the travel is plenty.

<Exercise title="Why clamp at both ends">

That is linear interpolation, and the clamping at both ends is deliberate. Work out what extrapolating
past your measured range would command, and why holding the endpoint value is the safer wrong answer.

<Solution>

Extrapolation continues the last segment's slope forever, so a height a little past the top of your
table produces a voltage a little past the last one — and a sensor glitch reading far past it produces
an arbitrarily large voltage, from a model that has no evidence out there at all. Clamping caps the
command at the hold voltage of the nearest measured height. This prevents unbounded extrapolation;
it does **not** bound the physical modeling error outside the calibrated range. Extend the calibration
before relying on feedforward there, and enforce mechanism travel and voltage limits separately.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Is this worth the complexity?">

Measure the hold voltage at the bottom, middle and top of *your* slide.

<Solution>

If the three agree within a few percent, a single `kG` is fine and this whole class is
over-engineering — you have added a table, an interpolator, and a tuning session to reproduce a
constant. The table earns its place when the numbers diverge noticeably, which typically means
cascading rigging where stages engage at different heights.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

The identical recipe in FRC is one line, because WPILib bundles it:

<JavaCode league="FRC" label="WPILib equivalent">{`SimpleMotorFeedforward ff = new SimpleMotorFeedforward(kS, kV, kA);
PIDController pid = new PIDController(kP, 0, 0);
motor.setVoltage(ff.calculate(goalVel) + pid.calculate(measVel, goalVel));`}</JavaCode>

The constants come from **measurement**, not guesswork. For velocity mechanisms, drive the mechanism
through known voltages and fit the steady speeds. For gravity mechanisms, measure the hold voltage at the
important positions: a slide at several heights, or an arm at known angles. A useful first estimate is
$k_V \approx 12\,\text{V} \div v_{\max}$, but to get them right you measure them:
[**system identification**](./system-identification.mdx) recovers $k_S$, $k_V$, and $k_A$ by
least-squares fit, exactly as FRC's SysId does.

<Callout type="warn" title="Feedforward is only as good as its units.">
<code>kV</code> in "volts per m/s" is meaningless unless <code>getVelocity()</code> is truly
converted to m/s. A wrong ticks→units factor produces a wrong model — exactly the failure
[Discretization &amp; Units](../software-architecture/discretization.mdx) guards against.
</Callout>

Feedforward is only ever half a controller, though — it is open-loop, blind to anything it didn't
model: a jammed mechanism, a worn gear, a battery having a bad day. Those gains are also only as good as
your measurement of them — so pair feedforward with [**system identification**](./system-identification.mdx)
to measure $k_S, k_V, k_A$ from data, measure $k_G$ on the mechanism itself, and use [**PID**](./pid.mdx)
for the feedback half that catches everything the model missed.
