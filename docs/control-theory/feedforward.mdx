# Feedforward (kS, kV, kA, kG)

<Difficulty level="Medium" />

<Abstract>
Feedback waits for an error before it does anything. Feedforward uses a model of the mechanism to
send most of the needed voltage right away, and leaves feedback to clean up what's left. This lesson
builds the kS, kV, and kA terms from the motor model, pairs them with PID, and adds gravity terms for
slides and arms.
</Abstract>

A feedback controller only acts once something is already wrong. But for most mechanisms you know
roughly what voltage a given speed needs before you ask for it. Feedforward sends that voltage up
front, and feedback only has to handle the difference. It's usually what separates a sluggish velocity
loop from a crisp one.

<Problem>

## Proportional control needs an error to hold a speed

Take a flywheel you want at 300 rad/s. Holding that speed takes about 6 V. A proportional controller
only outputs voltage when there's an error. With $k_P = 0.1$ V per rad/s, the flywheel settles where the
error supplies just enough voltage for the speed it actually reached: about 250 rad/s, which needs 5 V,
supplied by a 50 rad/s error. Raising $k_P$ shrinks the gap, but it also
amplifies sensor noise and makes overshoot worse. An integral term can build up the missing voltage
over time, but it's slow to do it.

You already know the flywheel needs about 6 V at that speed, so you can just send it.

<Analogy>
<strong>Driving up a hill.</strong> A good driver presses the accelerator as the road starts to climb.
A purely reactive driver waits until the car has slowed down, then overcorrects. Feedforward is
pressing early. Feedback is the small adjustment after.
</Analogy>

</Problem>

<Theory>

## Where the voltage goes

The voltage to hold a velocity $v$ while accelerating at $a$ splits into three parts:

$$
V = \underbrace{k_S\,\operatorname{sign}(v)}_{\text{break friction}}
  + \underbrace{k_V\,v}_{\text{hold the speed}}
  + \underbrace{k_A\,a}_{\text{accelerate}}
$$

These come straight out of the [motor model](./how-a-motor-works.mdx), $V = IR + k_e\omega$. The
back-EMF part grows with speed, which is the $k_V v$ term. The current part supplies torque, some of it
a roughly constant amount to overcome friction ($k_S$) and some of it to accelerate the mechanism's
inertia ($k_A a$).

| Term | Pays for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **kS**: static | friction | the minimum voltage to keep it moving; the sign follows the direction of travel |
| **kV**: velocity | back-EMF and drag | volts per unit of speed; usually most of the command |
| **kA**: acceleration | inertia | only nonzero while the target speed is changing |

The $k_S$ you get from a fit is the friction while moving. The voltage needed to break a stationary
mechanism loose can be a bit higher, and it can differ between directions.

## Working out one command

Say system identification gave a drivetrain $k_S = 0.9$ V, $k_V = 0.15$ V per in/s, and $k_A = 0.03$ V
per in/s². You want 40 in/s while still speeding up at 20 in/s²:

$$
V = 0.9 + 0.15(40) + 0.03(20) = 0.9 + 6.0 + 0.6 = 7.5\ \text{V}
$$

Once it reaches 40 in/s and stops accelerating, the $k_A$ term drops out and the command falls to
6.9 V. Before sending either number, clamp it to what the battery can supply.

In the demo the motor has to reach the dashed target speed. Turn feedforward on and off with the same
gains. The bars show how the command splits between the
<span style={{ color: "#5ce08a" }}>feedforward prediction</span> and the
<span style={{ color: "#ffc24d" }}>feedback correction</span>.

<li>Feedforward <strong>off</strong> with the same kP. It settles below the target. Raising kP to make

## Adding gravity

A flywheel doesn't need any voltage to stay still. A vertical slide does, and an arm needs an amount
that changes with its angle.

<MechanismFeedforward />

### Slides: gravity is roughly constant

For a vertical slide at height $h$, with velocity $v$ (positive up) and acceleration $a$, add a constant
$k_G$, the voltage that holds the slide still:

$$
\boxed{
V_{slide} =
k_S\,\operatorname{sign}(v)
+ k_G
+ k_V\,v
+ k_A\,a
}
$$

$k_G$ doesn't change sign when the slide moves down. Friction always opposes the motion, so
$k_S\operatorname{sign}(v)$ flips, but gravity always pulls down, so the motor always needs an upward
holding term.

Real slides aren't always that tidy. Cable routing, stage engagement, elastic tension, and carriage
friction can make the holding voltage change with height. If one $k_G$ isn't enough, measure it at
several heights and interpolate between them. For a height $h$ between two measured heights $h_i$ and
$h_{i+1}$:

$$
\boxed{
k_G(h)
=
k_{G,i}
+
\frac{h - h_i}{h_{i+1} - h_i}
\left(k_{G,i+1} - k_{G,i}\right)
}
$$

The fraction is how far $h$ sits between the two measured heights, from 0 to 1. For example, with
$k_G = 1.2$ V at 10 in and $1.6$ V at 20 in, a height of 12.5 in is a quarter of the way along, so
$k_G = 1.2 + 0.25(0.4) = 1.3$ V.

### Arms: gravity changes with angle

Measure the arm angle $\theta$ from **horizontal**, with $\omega$ its angular velocity and $\alpha$
its angular acceleration. Gravity pulls hardest on a horizontal arm and not at all on a vertical one,
and the factor that captures that is $\cos\theta$:

$$
\boxed{
V_{arm} =
k_S\,\operatorname{sign}(\omega)
+ k_G\cos(\theta)
+ k_V\,\omega
+ k_A\,\alpha
}
$$

Here $k_G$ is the voltage that holds the arm horizontal. At $\theta = 0$, $\cos\theta = 1$ and you get
all of it. Pointing straight up, $\cos 90° = 0$ and you get none.

If your encoder reads zero somewhere other than horizontal, convert it first:

$$
\theta_{horizontal} = \theta_{encoder} + \theta_{offset}
$$

where $\theta_{offset}$ is a constant you measure once. If the sign comes out wrong, don't flip the
whole controller. Fix the angle convention and motor direction until a horizontal arm gets an upward
holding voltage.

In practice, feedforward and feedback run together: the model supplies most of the command right away,
and [PID](./pid.mdx) corrects what's left and rejects disturbances.

<Formula>
  output = <Teal>feedforward(goal)</Teal> + <Rose>PID(measurement, goal)</Rose>
</Formula>

</Theory>

## Feedforward in code, in volts

FTC motors take a power from −1 to 1, but the model is in volts. Divide by the measured battery voltage,
as in [voltage compensation](./voltage-compensation.mdx), so the behavior is the same at 13 V and 11 V.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Feedforward + feedback, battery-compensated">{`// goalVelocity and goalAccel are in real units, e.g. metres/second.
// The motor runs in RUN_WITHOUT_ENCODER: this method closes its own loop.
void run(double goalVelocity, double goalAccel) {
    double measured = flywheel.getVelocity() * UNITS_PER_TICK;

    double predict = kS * Math.signum(goalVelocity)
            + kV * goalVelocity
            + kA * goalAccel;
    double correct = kP * (goalVelocity - measured);

    flywheel.setPower((predict + correct) / batteryVoltage());
}`}</JavaCode>

Compare the two lines. `predict` only uses the goal. It never looks at the sensor, so it can act the
moment you ask for a new speed. `correct` uses the error, so it can only act once the mechanism is
already off.

<Exercise title="Delete one term at a time">

What happens without each term?

- no `kS`: the flywheel never quite gets going from rest.
- no `kV`: the command doesn't grow with the speed you ask for.
- no `kA`: steady speeds are fine, but it lags whenever the goal changes.

Once all three are in, what's left for `kP` to do, and why does that let you use a *smaller* `kP` than
with feedback alone?

<Solution>

`kS` pays for friction, so without it every command comes up short by exactly that much. `kV` pays for
back-EMF, which grows with speed, so without it the model is just a constant. `kA` pays for the extra
torque it takes to change speed, so without it the model is only right when nothing is changing.

With all three, `kP` only has to correct model error and disturbances, a few percent of the command.
That's good, because `kP` amplifies sensor noise and can only react after the fact. The less it has to
do, the less noise reaches the motor and the less it overshoots.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Arm feedforward with a gravity term">{`// Angle convention: 0 means the arm is HORIZONTAL. If your encoder zero is
// elsewhere, add the offset before calling this. Output is volts.
double calculate(double angle, double velocity, double acceleration) {
    double staticVolts = Math.abs(velocity) < EPS
            ? 0
            : kS * Math.signum(velocity);

    return staticVolts
            + kG * Math.cos(angle)      // <- the gravity term
            + kV * velocity
            + kA * acceleration;
}`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="Where the cosine comes from">

Gravity pulls straight down with force $mg$, but only the part of that force perpendicular to the arm
turns it around the pivot. Draw the arm at an angle $\theta$ above horizontal, split the weight into a
part along the arm and a part across it, and find the one that makes torque. Then check your answer at
horizontal, straight up, and hanging straight down.

<Solution>

The part along the arm just pushes on the pivot bearing. The part across the arm is $mg\cos\theta$, and
it acts at the arm's length from the pivot, so the holding torque goes as $\cos\theta$.

Horizontal ($\theta = 0$): $\cos = 1$, the most holding voltage, which is the worst case. Straight up
($90°$): $\cos = 0$, because the weight points straight through the pivot. Hanging down ($-90°$):
$\cos = 0$ again, for the same reason. Those two zeros are very different situations, though. Straight
up is unstable: nudge it and gravity pulls it over. Hanging down is stable: gravity pulls it back.
Feedforward can't tell them apart. Feedback can.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why the static term is zero near rest">

`staticVolts` is forced to zero at very low speeds instead of just using `Math.signum(velocity)`. What
is `Math.signum(0)`, and what would a stationary arm do if this term kept flickering?

<Solution>

`Math.signum(0)` is 0, so exactly zero is already handled. The problem is speeds *near* zero. A
stationary arm's measured velocity jitters slightly positive and negative, so `signum` flips every few
loops and the command jumps by $2k_S$. The arm buzzes. The `EPS` band treats "barely moving" as "not
moving" and leaves the friction term off.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Slide feedforward with interpolated gravity">{`// For a vertical slide where ONE constant kG is not accurate across the
// travel: rigging friction and stage engagement change with height.
private double[] heights;     // the heights you tested at
private double[] holdVolts;   // the voltage that held the slide at each one

double calculate(double height, double velocity, double acceleration) {
    double staticVolts = Math.abs(velocity) < EPS
            ? 0
            : kS * Math.signum(velocity);

    return staticVolts
            + interpolateHoldVolts(height)   // instead of a constant kG
            + kV * velocity
            + kA * acceleration;
}

double interpolateHoldVolts(double height) {
    if (heights.length < 2) return 0;
    if (height <= heights[0]) return holdVolts[0];

    for (int i = 0; i < heights.length - 1; i++) {
        if (height <= heights[i + 1]) {
            double t = (height - heights[i]) / (heights[i + 1] - heights[i]);
            return holdVolts[i] + t * (holdVolts[i + 1] - holdVolts[i]);
        }
    }
    return holdVolts[holdVolts.length - 1];   // clamp above the last sample
}`}</JavaCode>

Build the table by measuring. At each height, take **two** readings: the smallest voltage that just
moves the slide up, and the largest at which it still creeps down. Their **average** is $k_G$ at that
height, and half the difference is $k_S$. Don't use "the smallest voltage that holds it still." Friction
lets it hold anywhere in a band, and the bottom of that band is $k_G - k_S$, so every entry would come
out low and the separate $k_S$ term would count friction twice. The [PID lesson](./pid.mdx) uses the
same two readings. Five or six heights across the travel is plenty.

<Exercise title="Why clamp at both ends">

What would extrapolating past the ends of the table command, and why is holding the last measured value
the safer choice?

<Solution>

Extrapolating carries the last segment's slope on forever. A height slightly past the top gives a
voltage slightly past the last one, and a sensor glitch reading far past the top gives an enormous
voltage, from a model with no data out there at all. Clamping caps the command at the nearest measured
value. It stops the runaway, but it doesn't make the model right outside the measured range. If you
need feedforward out there, measure out there, and enforce travel and voltage limits separately.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Do you need the table?">

Measure the holding voltage at the bottom, middle, and top of your slide. What would tell you the
table isn't worth it?

<Solution>

If the three agree within a few percent, one `kG` is fine, and the table just adds tuning work to
reproduce a constant. It's worth it when the numbers differ noticeably, which usually means cascade
rigging where stages engage at different heights.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

In FRC, WPILib bundles the same recipe:

<JavaCode league="FRC" label="WPILib equivalent">{`SimpleMotorFeedforward ff = new SimpleMotorFeedforward(kS, kV, kA);
PIDController pid = new PIDController(kP, 0, 0);
motor.setVoltage(ff.calculate(goalVel) + pid.calculate(measVel, goalVel));`}</JavaCode>

The constants come from measurement. A rough first guess is $k_V \approx 12\,\text{V} \div v_{\max}$, but
[system identification](./system-identification.mdx) measures $k_S$, $k_V$, and $k_A$ properly with a
least-squares fit, the same way FRC's SysId does. For gravity, measure $k_G$ on the mechanism at the
positions that matter.

<Callout type="warn" title="Check your units">
<code>kV</code> in volts per m/s only means something if <code>getVelocity()</code> has really been
converted to m/s. A wrong ticks-to-units factor gives a wrong model. See
[From Math to a Loop](../software-architecture/discretization.mdx).
</Callout>

Feedforward on its own is open loop, so it can't see anything the model left out: a jam, a worn gear, a
tired battery. Pair it with [PID](./pid.mdx) to catch the rest.

</Deploy>
