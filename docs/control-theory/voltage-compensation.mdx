# Voltage Compensation

<Difficulty level="Easy" />

<Abstract>
A motor power command in $[-1, 1]$ is a fraction of whatever the battery reads right now, not a fixed
voltage. As the battery sags during a match, the same command does less. Voltage compensation divides
the voltage you want by the measured battery voltage, so the motor gets the same voltage all match (as
long as the battery can supply it) and your feedforward gains stay correct.
</Abstract>

This is a short lesson about one division. It's what makes [feedforward](./feedforward.mdx),
[PID](./pid.mdx), and [motion profiles](./motion-profiling.mdx) behave the same on a fresh battery and a
tired one.

<Problem>

## The same command does less as the battery drains

`motor.setPower(0.6)` doesn't apply a fixed voltage. It applies 60% of whatever the battery reads at that
moment. A freshly charged 12 V battery usually reads around 13 V and can sag to 11 V or lower under
heavy load. (The exact numbers depend on the battery's age, charge, and current draw.) So `0.6` means
$0.6 \times 13 = 7.8$ V early in the match and $0.6 \times 11 = 6.6$ V late in it, for the same line of
code. An auto tuned on a full battery starts coming up short, and a flywheel that was on speed falls
behind. Your feedforward gains are also measured in volts, and they're being applied to a command that
no longer means a fixed number of volts.

</Problem>

<Theory>

## Divide by what you actually have

Suppose you want 6 V at the motor. On a 13 V battery that's a power of $6/13 = 0.46$. On an 11 V battery
it's $6/11 = 0.55$. In general, divide the voltage you want by the voltage you have:

$$
\text{power} = \frac{V_\text{cmd}}{V_\text{battery}}
$$

The motor then gets $\text{power} \times V_\text{battery} = V_\text{cmd}$, whatever the battery is doing.
The one limit is that you can't ask for more than the battery has. Once $V_\text{cmd} > V_\text{battery}$
the command hits full power and the output drops with the battery.

In the demo, the rose line is a raw power command, so the delivered voltage drifts as the battery
changes. The green line is compensated and stays flat at the target until the battery can't reach it.
Drag the battery from full to sagging and compare them.

<VoltageComp />

<Callout type="idea" title="Gains that stay correct">
With the command in real volts, the feedforward constants you
[measured](./system-identification.mdx) stay correct all match, and they carry over better between two
robots with the same mechanism. Without it, every gain is really "correct at whatever voltage the
battery had when I tuned," which is never the voltage you compete on.
</Callout>

</Theory>

<Deploy>

## One division, or one SDK call

Do it last, after the controller has worked out a voltage. Read the battery once per loop, since each
read takes time, and clamp the result to $[-1, 1]$.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Voltage-compensated output">{`// A telemetry sanity floor, NOT a battery cutoff.
static final double MIN_VALID_BATTERY_VOLTS = 6.0;

void loop() {
    double vbat = voltageSensor.getVoltage();   // ONCE per loop, shared below
    setVoltage(leftMotor,  leftDesiredVolts(),  vbat);
    setVoltage(rightMotor, rightDesiredVolts(), vbat);
}

void setVoltage(DcMotor motor, double desiredVolts, double vbat) {
    if (!Double.isFinite(vbat) || !Double.isFinite(desiredVolts)
            || vbat < MIN_VALID_BATTERY_VOLTS) {
        motor.setPower(0);          // reject a disconnected sensor
        return;
    }

    double power = desiredVolts / vbat;         // volts -> the SDK's [-1, 1]
    motor.setPower(Math.max(-1, Math.min(1, power)));
}`}</JavaCode>

Every mechanism works out what it wants in volts, and this is the only place volts become motor power.
All of them use the same `vbat` reading.

<Exercise title="Why one reading, shared">

Each motor could read the battery itself. What goes wrong when two drive motors read a sagging battery
a few milliseconds apart and are both asked for the same voltage?

<Solution>

They divide by slightly different numbers, so the same 6 V request turns into two different powers. On a
drivetrain that's a steering error nobody asked for, and it only shows up when the battery is sagging,
which is when you're pushing hardest. With one reading per loop, the whole robot agrees on the battery
voltage.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="The sign of the effect">

You want 6 V. On a fresh 13.0 V battery the power is 0.46, and on a sagging 11.0 V battery it's 0.55. Why
is a bigger number the right answer for a weaker battery?

<Solution>

`setPower` is a fraction of the battery voltage, not a voltage. A weaker battery needs a bigger fraction
to give the motor the same voltage. Without the division, `0.46` gives 6.0 V on a fresh battery and
5.06 V on a sagging one, and every gain you tuned drifts over the match.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

In FRC you don't write the division yourself. `setVoltage(...)` divides by the measured bus voltage, and
newer vendor APIs like Phoenix 6's `VoltageOut` take a voltage directly. Use either the roboRIO-side
`setVoltage` or the motor controller's own `enableVoltageCompensation(nominalVolts)`, not both. If you
use both, `setVoltage` sends a percent $V_\text{cmd}/V_\text{bus}$, the controller scales that percent
by $V_\text{nom}$, and the mechanism receives

$$
V_\text{delivered} = V_\text{cmd}\,\frac{V_\text{nom}}{V_\text{bus}}
$$

On a sagging battery that asks for too much: request 6 V with $V_\text{bus} = 11$ V and 6.55 V arrives,
9% high, in exactly the situation compensation is meant for. On a fresh battery above nominal it asks for
too little.

<Callout type="tip" title="It doesn't fix brownouts">
Holding the voltage steady while the battery sags means drawing more current, which sags the battery
further. Compensation keeps behavior consistent within what the battery can do. If you're browning
out, look at the [motor model](./how-a-motor-works.mdx): something is probably running too close to
stall.
</Callout>

</Deploy>

<Admonition type="warning" title="Check the voltage reading">

Battery voltage dips sharply under a sudden load, like the drivetrain going to full power, so one
reading can come back very low and make compensation ask for too much. Throw out readings that aren't
finite or aren't physically possible, as the code does. If the reading is noisy, a very light
[low-pass filter](../signal-processing/low-pass.mdx) is fine, but don't smooth heavily: real sag is
exactly what the command needs to respond to.

</Admonition>
