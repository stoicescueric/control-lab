# RST Pole Placement

<Difficulty level="Hard" />

<Abstract>
An RST controller is discrete pole placement written as three polynomials, $R$, $S$ and $T$, acting on
the measurement, the past command, and the reference. Instead of three gains with no physical meaning
it asks for a description of the plant, which [system identification](./system-identification.mdx)
already measured, and a description of the response you want, expressed as a time constant in seconds,
then solves for its own coefficients from those.
</Abstract>

[System identification](./system-identification.mdx) ends with $k_S$, $k_V$ and $k_A$ measured from
your own robot. A PID loop then throws most of that away: it takes the error and asks you for three
more numbers, tuned by feel, whose units nobody states. The model you just measured is a complete
description of the mechanism, and a controller can be solved for rather than tuned.

<Problem>

## A tuned gain is a number that only works where it was tuned

Tune a velocity PID on a drivetrain at a 20 ms loop and it behaves differently at 45 ms, because the
integral is a running sum and the derivative a difference over a $\Delta t$ that changed. Tune it on a
fresh battery and it softens as the pack sags, because a duty cycle is not a unit of force. Neither
failure announces itself. The loop keeps working, slightly worse, and nothing in the three numbers
tells you which of them to move.

The symptom on a real robot is a mechanism that was tuned crisply on Friday, feels sluggish in the
third match on Saturday, and rings when a teammate adds a vision pipeline to the loop.

</Problem>

## Build the intuition

Pole placement means choosing the closed-loop response instead of discovering it. The plant has one
time constant, $\tau$, which is how long it takes to reach 63% of a new speed on its own. The
controller is asked to make the closed loop have a different time constant, $\tau_{cl}$, and the
design produces whatever coefficients that requires.

<RstPolePlacement />

The reference alternates every 1.6 s, because the interesting behaviour is on a **change** and not on
the first move from rest. Drag `tauClosedLoop` and watch the readout: the green trace reaches 63% of
each change in about the time you asked for, and the number is measured off the simulation rather than
asserted. That is the whole appeal. `tauClosedLoop = 0.1` is a claim about the robot that a graph can
confirm or refute, in a way that `kP = 0.004` is not.

Two things are worth doing before reading the derivation:

- **Drag `tauClosedLoop` down.** The lower plot is the price. For a reference change $\Delta v$, the
  first-loop reference increment is

  $$
  \Delta u_{ref} = \frac{1 - e^{-\Delta t / \tau_{cl}}}{k_{dc}(1 - e^{-\Delta t / \tau})}\,\Delta v
  $$

  When $\Delta t$ is small relative to both time constants, that approaches
  $(\tau/\tau_{cl})(\Delta v/k_{dc})$, so halving $\tau_{cl}$ roughly doubles the increment. The
  readout uses the exact discrete coefficients, including the four-loop floor. The total command also
  includes the voltage already holding the previous speed. Once it saturates, the response stops
  matching the request no matter what the slider says.
- **Compare the two traces.** They are the same poles on the same plant, differing only in how the
  reference enters. Press **Slow the integral pole** and watch the pink one fall behind while the green
  one does not move.

<Theory>

## Solve the controller from the plant and the response you want

### The plant a feedforward already describes

The feedforward model says the voltage to hold velocity $v$ while accelerating at $a$ is
$u = k_S\operatorname{sgn}(v) + k_V v + k_A a$. Set the static term aside for a moment, since it is
not linear and is handled separately below, and rearrange what is left:

$$
\frac{k_A}{k_V}\,\dot v + v = \frac{1}{k_V}\,u
$$

That is a first-order lag, so the same measurement serves both purposes:

$$
\tau = \frac{k_A}{k_V}, \qquad k_{dc} = \frac{1}{k_V}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: '\\tau', meaning: 'the plant time constant, how long the mechanism takes to reach 63% of a new speed under a fixed voltage', unit: 's'},
    {symbol: 'k_{dc}', meaning: 'the steady-state gain, the speed one volt eventually buys', unit: 'velocity per volt'},
    {symbol: 'u', meaning: 'applied voltage, not motor power', unit: 'V'},
    {symbol: 'v', meaning: 'measured velocity in whatever unit the identification used'},
  ]}
/>

Nothing new was measured here. A feedforward and a first-order plant are the same three numbers
written two ways.

### Discretise it once, in the loop

The Control Hub runs a loop, not an integral, so the design happens in discrete time. For a
first-order lag under a held voltage the discretisation is exact rather than approximate:

$$
v_k = a\,v_{k-1} + b\,u_{k-1},
\qquad a = e^{-\Delta t/\tau},
\qquad b = k_{dc}\,(1 - a)
$$

Written with $q^{-1}$ as a one-loop delay, that is $A(q^{-1})\,v = B(q^{-1})\,u$ with
$A = 1 - a q^{-1}$ and $B = b q^{-1}$. See
[Discretization](../software-architecture/discretization.mdx) for why the exponential appears where a
naive Euler step would put $1 - \Delta t/\tau$.

### The three polynomials

An RST controller is one equation:

$$
S(q^{-1})\,u = T(q^{-1})\,v^{*} - R(q^{-1})\,v
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'v^{*}', meaning: 'the reference velocity, what the profile is asking for'},
    {symbol: 'R', meaning: 'acts on the MEASUREMENT. This is the feedback path'},
    {symbol: 'S', meaning: 'acts on the controller OUTPUT. Choosing it to contain the factor (1 - q^{-1}) is what gives integral action'},
    {symbol: 'T', meaning: 'acts on the REFERENCE. It is free, and setting it well is the difference between a loop that tracks at the speed it was asked for and one that does not'},
  ]}
/>

Two degrees of freedom, which a PID does not have: $R$ decides how disturbances are rejected and $T$
decides how the reference is followed, separately. Substituting the plant into the controller gives
the closed loop:

$$
\bigl(A S + B R\bigr)\,v = B\,T\,v^{*}
$$

So $A S + B R$ is the closed-loop denominator. Pole placement is choosing what it should be and then
solving for $R$ and $S$, which is a Diophantine equation.

### Placing two real poles

Take $S = 1 - q^{-1}$ for integral action, and ask for two real closed-loop poles:

$$
A_{cl} = (1 - p_1 q^{-1})(1 - p_2 q^{-1}) = 1 + \alpha_1 q^{-1} + \alpha_2 q^{-2}
$$

with $p_1 = e^{-\Delta t/\tau_{cl}}$ the tracking pole and $p_2 = e^{-\Delta t/\tau_{i}}$ the integral
pole, $\alpha_1 = -(p_1 + p_2)$ and $\alpha_2 = p_1 p_2$. Real poles rather than complex ones because
a drivetrain that overshoots its velocity reference on a path is worse than one that arrives slightly
late.

Expanding $A S = 1 - (1 + a) q^{-1} + a q^{-2}$ and taking $R = r_0 + r_1 q^{-1}$, matching
coefficients of $q^{-1}$ and $q^{-2}$ gives the whole controller in closed form:

$$
r_0 = \frac{\alpha_1 + 1 + a}{b},
\qquad
r_1 = \frac{\alpha_2 - a}{b}
$$

There is no iteration and nothing to search. Two time constants in, two coefficients out.

### Why T is not just a gain

$T$ is unconstrained by the pole placement, so the obvious choice is the scalar that makes the steady
state come out right, $t_0 = A_{cl}(1)/B(1)$. That choice is wrong in a way that is easy to miss,
because the closed-loop reference response is

$$
\frac{v}{v^{*}} = \frac{B\,T}{A_{cl}}
$$

and with a scalar $T$ both poles remain in it. The integral pole is deliberately slow, so it dominates.
On the plant in the simulation above, a 0.08 s closed loop against a 0.4 s integral pole answers a
change of reference in about 0.48 s, six times slower than asked, and the tests in
`src/lib/domain/rst.test.ts` pin that ratio. The loop is not broken and disturbance rejection is fine.
It simply does not track at the speed it was asked to, and the number you set does not describe the
behaviour you get.

Choosing $T$ to cancel the integral pole from the reference path fixes it:

$$
T = \frac{1 - p_1}{b}\,\bigl(1 - p_2 q^{-1}\bigr)
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\frac{v}{v^{*}} = \frac{(1 - p_1)\,q^{-1}}{1 - p_1 q^{-1}}
$$

Tracking is now first order with exactly the time constant asked for, while $A_{cl}$ still contains
$p_2$, so the integral pole keeps doing the job it was there for: rejecting the steady offsets a model
cannot know about. For a held reference the two versions of $T$ agree exactly, since $t_0(1 - p_2)$ is the scalar
choice, which is why the error hides until the reference starts moving. A step response measured from
rest cannot see it at all.

<Exercise title="What the slow pole costs a scalar T">

At $\Delta t = 0.02$ s, with $\tau_{cl} = 0.08$ s and $\tau_i = 0.4$ s, compute $p_1$ and $p_2$. Which
is the slow mode, and roughly how long does a reference step take to settle if that mode is present in
the tracking response?

<Solution>

$p_1 = e^{-0.25} = 0.779$ and $p_2 = e^{-0.05} = 0.951$. The pole nearer 1 is the slow one, so the
response is governed by $\tau_i = 0.4$ s rather than the 0.08 s requested. The simulation above
measures 0.48 s against 0.08 s, a factor of six.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

### The loop, as a recurrence

Because $S = 1 - q^{-1}$, the controller equation is $u_k - u_{k-1} = \ldots$, which rearranges into
an incremental form:

$$
u_k = u_{k-1} + t_0\bigl(v^{*}_k - p_2 v^{*}_{k-1}\bigr) - r_0 v_k - r_1 v_{k-1}
$$

The previous output *is* the integrator state. That single observation is worth more than it looks,
because it makes [anti-windup](./actuator-saturation-anti-windup.mdx) free: clamp the value before
storing it and a saturated axis cannot accumulate a command it will later have to unwind. There is no
separate integral term to freeze.

</Theory>

## One method, recomputed from the measured loop time

Every coefficient above depends on $\Delta t$, so all of them are computed inside the loop from the
$\Delta t$ that actually elapsed. A fixed-coefficient discrete controller quietly retunes itself when
the loop jitters, which is the failure mode a robot with a vision pipeline runs into first.

<JavaCode label="One RST loop, coefficients solved from the measured dt">{`private double previousOutput;
private double previousMeasurement;
private double previousReference;
private boolean primed;

double calculate(double reference, double measurement, double dt) {
    if (!Double.isFinite(dt) || dt <= 0 || dt > MAX_DT) dt = NOMINAL_DT;
    if (!primed) {
        previousMeasurement = measurement;
        previousReference = reference;
        primed = true;
    }

    double a = Math.exp(-dt / Math.max(tau, 1e-3));
    double b = kDc * (1 - a);
    if (Math.abs(b) < 1e-9) return previousOutput;

    double p1 = Math.exp(-dt / Math.max(tauClosedLoop, 4 * dt));
    double p2 = Math.exp(-dt / Math.max(tauIntegral, 1e-3));
    double alpha1 = -(p1 + p2);
    double alpha2 = p1 * p2;

    double r0 = (alpha1 + 1 + a) / b;
    double r1 = (alpha2 - a) / b;
    double t0 = (1 - p1) / b;

    double output = previousOutput
            + t0 * (reference - p2 * previousReference)
            - r0 * measurement - r1 * previousMeasurement;

    output = clamp(output, -availableVolts, availableVolts);

    previousOutput = output;
    previousMeasurement = measurement;
    previousReference = reference;
    return output;
}
`}</JavaCode>

Three of those lines are guards rather than algebra, and each one is a failure mode. The $b$ check is
the interesting one: $b = k_{dc}(1 - a)$ goes to zero as $\Delta t$ does, so on an implausibly short
loop, or with a mistyped $k_V$, every coefficient divides by something near zero. Returning the
previous command is a better answer than an infinite gain. The `4 * dt` floor on $\tau_{cl}$ is the
same idea applied to ambition: no pole placement usefully beats a handful of loop periods, however
good the model.

Storing the clamped `output` is the anti-windup, and it is the only anti-windup this controller needs.

<Exercise title="How aggressive tauClosedLoop can be">

A drivetrain measures $k_V = 0.15$ V per in/s and $k_A = 0.03$ V per in/s², so $\tau = 0.2$ s and
$k_{dc} = 6.7$ in/s per volt. The loop runs every $\Delta t = 0.02$ s, and $k_S = 1.4$ V. For
$\tau_{cl} = 0.1$ s, calculate the exact reference-step voltage increment for a settled step from 0 to
40 in/s. Does the total command fit on a 12 V battery after adding the static-friction voltage?

<Solution>

$a = e^{-0.02/0.2} = 0.905$, $p_1 = e^{-0.02/0.1} = 0.819$, and
$b = 6.7(1-a) = 0.634$. Therefore

$$
\Delta u_{ref} = \frac{1-p_1}{b}(40) = 11.43\ \text{V}
$$

Adding $k_S$ asks for about 12.83 V, so the command saturates. Solving
$k_S + \Delta u_{ref} \le 12$ gives $\tau_{cl} \gtrsim 0.109$ s before leaving any extra margin. The
continuous approximation gives a 12 V reference increment at $\tau_{cl} = 0.1$ s, but the discrete
calculation is the value the implemented controller actually uses.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

### Volts are the output, power is the boundary

The controller returns volts, because volts are what $k_V$ and $k_S$ were measured in. Dividing by
the battery is the last thing that happens, at the hardware boundary, for the reason
[Voltage Compensation](./voltage-compensation.mdx) gives: a controller working in duty cycle silently
retunes itself as the pack sags.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Volts to wheel power, scaled rather than clipped">{`void applyVolts(double forwardVolts, double turnVolts) {
    double battery = voltageSensor.getVoltage();
    if (!Double.isFinite(battery) || battery < 6) return;

    double f = forwardVolts / battery;
    double t = turnVolts / battery;
    double peak = Math.max(1, Math.max(Math.abs(f + t), Math.abs(f - t)));
    setWheelPowers((f + t) / peak, (f - t) / peak);
}
`}</JavaCode>

The `peak` division matters more than it looks. Clamping each wheel on its own changes the ratios
between them, and the ratios are the direction: a robot asked for a fast diagonal answers with a
command that is no longer diagonal, and the harder it is pushed the further off course the correction
points. Scaling gives up speed and keeps the direction, which is the trade a follower can reason
about.

:::warning[Static friction is not part of this plant]
The model solved above is linear, and $k_S$ is not: it is a fixed voltage that must be paid before
anything moves at all, and no choice of poles produces it. The controller can only discover it by
integrating error, which means waiting. Feed $k_S$ forward ahead of the loop and leave the rest to
the design; the integrator absorbs whatever the estimate got wrong.

On a holonomic drive, apply it as **one vector along the direction of travel**, not as
$k_S\operatorname{sgn}(v)$ per axis. A robot driving down a path holds a small lateral correction that
changes sign as it wanders across the line, and an independent lateral term answers every one of those
crossings with the full lateral $k_S$. The correction that should have been an ounce of voltage becomes
a shove, and the robot weaves at the frequency of its own sign changes.
:::

:::tip[Log the reference against the measurement]
The one plot worth having is the velocity reference and the measured velocity on the same axes. A gap
that persists means the loop cannot keep up, so either the constants are wrong or $\tau_{cl}$ is
asking for more than the pack can supply. A gap that oscillates means $\tau_{cl}$ is too small for the
loop rate. Nothing else distinguishes those two, and the three coefficients will not tell you.
:::

:::warning[The measurement sets a floor of its own]
A velocity that comes from differencing encoder counts over a window lags by roughly half that window.
That delay adds to the loop period when deciding how fast a pole can usefully be placed, so a 25 ms
differencing window on a 20 ms loop leaves less headroom than the loop rate alone suggests. See
[Low-Pass Filtering](../signal-processing/low-pass.mdx) for the trade between that lag and noise.
:::

</Deploy>

## Check your understanding

- $b = k_{dc}(1 - a)$ appears in the denominator of every coefficient. What happens to $r_0$, $r_1$
  and $t_0$ as $\Delta t$ becomes small, and which guard in the block covers it?
- Suppose you store the unclamped output and clamp only what you send to the motors. Describe what the
  first command after a long saturated stretch looks like, and why the mechanism does not move at
  first.
- $\tau_i$ is deliberately slower than $\tau_{cl}$. What goes wrong if you set them equal, given that
  $A_{cl}$ would then have a repeated pole and $T$ would cancel the only one you have?
- Your velocity comes from a 25 ms differencing window and your loop runs at 20 ms. Argue for a
  $\tau_{cl}$ and say what evidence would change your mind.
