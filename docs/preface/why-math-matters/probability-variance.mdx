# Probability, Variance & Gaussians

<Difficulty level="Medium" />

<Abstract>
A sensor that reads slightly differently while the robot is still is not choosing a random answer on
purpose. Its readings form a spread around a best estimate. This bridge turns that spread into the
mean, variance, covariance, and Gaussian vocabulary used by a Kalman filter.
</Abstract>

## Repeated readings describe uncertainty

Hold a mechanism still and collect many encoder or distance-sensor readings. Their **mean** is the
average reading: the best single center for that set. Their **variance** measures how far the readings
typically spread from that center. Standard deviation is simply the square root of variance, so it has
the same unit as the measurement.

If a distance sensor reports values in centimetres, standard deviation is in centimetres and variance is
in square centimetres. A small variance means a tightly repeated measurement; a large variance means the
measurement should carry less weight when it disagrees with a trustworthy model.

Read [Signals & Noise](../../signal-processing/signals.mdx) for the measured, loop-safe procedure that
computes this spread on a real robot.

## A Gaussian is a useful bell-shaped model

A **Gaussian** (or normal distribution) is the familiar bell curve. It is not a claim that every sensor
reading is perfect. It is a convenient model for many small, independent disturbances: vibration,
quantization, and electrical noise tend to produce many readings near the mean and fewer farther away.

A linear Kalman filter uses a Gaussian model because mean and variance are enough to carry its estimate
and uncertainty forward. Real sensors can violate that assumption through bias, outliers, or changing
conditions. Those problems need calibration, gating, or an explicit bias state; calling the noise
Gaussian does not remove them.

## Covariance: when two errors move together

**Covariance** is the multi-signal version of spread. It asks whether two errors tend to be high or low
together. Positive covariance means they tend to move in the same direction; negative covariance means
they tend to move oppositely; near-zero covariance means no consistent linear relationship was measured.

A Kalman filter stores all variances and covariances in a covariance matrix $P$. The diagonal entries are
each state's uncertainty. The off-diagonal entries record which state errors move together. You can use
the scalar Kalman loop before deriving that matrix form.
