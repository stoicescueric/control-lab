# Linear Algebra

<Difficulty level="Medium" />

<Abstract>
Push the stick forward on a robot that happens to be facing sideways and, with no rotation applied, it strafes instead. That bug and the four wheel powers a mecanum chassis needs come out of the same operation: a grid of numbers turning one driver intent into commands the hardware can execute.
This page builds that grid, starting from a rotation and ending at the wheel-mixing matrix and what to do when it asks a motor for more than the motor has.
</Abstract>

Linear algebra starts with a simple idea: some quantities only make sense as a package. A robot pose is
not just $x$, not just $y$, and not just heading. It is all of them together.

$$
\mathbf{x} =
\begin{bmatrix}
x \\
y \\
\theta
\end{bmatrix}
$$

That package is a **vector**. Here, $x$ and $y$ are field position coordinates, and $\theta$ is the
robot's heading angle.

## When field-centric drive feels rotated

Your driver pushes the joystick away from the alliance wall and expects the robot to move upfield. But
the robot is turned 90 degrees, so robot-forward is now sideways. Without coordinate transforms, the
same joystick command means different things depending on heading.

The driver says, "field-centric is broken." Linear algebra says, "you forgot to rotate a vector."

## Arrows, and the grids that move them

Vectors are arrows; matrices are the transformations that rotate or stretch those arrows.

## Vectors, matrices, and one rotation

A vector stores related components:

$$
\mathbf{v} =
\begin{bmatrix}
v_x \\
v_y
\end{bmatrix}
$$

A matrix maps one vector into another:

$$
\mathbf{y} = A\mathbf{x}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'A', meaning: 'a MATRIX: a grid of numbers that turns one vector into another. Every matrix on this page is a recipe for "given these inputs, produce these outputs"'},
    {symbol: 'Ax', meaning: 'matrix times vector. Each row of A produces one number of the answer, by multiplying that row against x and adding the results'},
  ]}
/>

Here, $\mathbf{x}$ is the input vector, $A$ is the matrix doing the transformation, and $\mathbf{y}$ is
the output vector after the transformation.

For a robot, the most important early matrix is rotation. If the robot heading is $\theta$, a field
velocity can be converted into the robot frame by rotating by $-\theta$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
v_x^\text{robot} \\
v_y^\text{robot}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\theta & \sin\theta \\
-\sin\theta & \cos\theta
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
v_x^\text{field} \\
v_y^\text{field}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

That is field-centric drive in one equation. Here, $v_x^\text{field}$ and $v_y^\text{field}$ are the
driver's desired field-relative velocity components, while $v_x^\text{robot}$ and $v_y^\text{robot}$
are the commands after converting them into the robot's own forward/strafe frame.

## Mecanum wheel mixing

In FTC, mecanum drive is usually the first place where linear algebra stops feeling optional. A driver
moves two joysticks and a trigger: forward/back, strafe, and turn. The robot has to convert that one
driver intent into four motor commands every loop.

Before the motors are involved, the driver command is a chassis velocity:

$$
\mathbf{u} =
\begin{bmatrix}
v_x \\
v_y \\
\omega
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Here, $\mathbf{u}$ is the chassis command vector, $v_x$ is robot-forward velocity, $v_y$ is sideways
velocity, and $\omega$ is angular velocity, or turn rate.

The result is a wheel-speed vector:

$$
\mathbf{w} =
\begin{bmatrix}
w_{FL} \\
w_{FR} \\
w_{BL} \\
w_{BR}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Here, $\mathbf{w}$ stores the four wheel speeds: front-left, front-right, back-left, and back-right.

For this convention, positive $v_x$ means drive forward, positive $v_y$ means strafe left, and positive
$\omega$ means rotate counterclockwise. The wheel-mixing equation is:

$$
\mathbf{w} =
\frac{1}{r}
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & -1 & -(L + W) \\
1 & 1 & (L + W) \\
1 & 1 & -(L + W) \\
1 & -1 & (L + W)
\end{bmatrix}
\mathbf{u}
$$

<EquationLegend
  title="Symbols in the wheel-mixing matrix"
  items={[
    {symbol: 'w_FL, w_FR, w_BL, w_BR', meaning: 'the four wheel speeds: front-left, front-right, back-left, back-right. The subscript names the corner of the robot'},
    {symbol: 'r', meaning: 'the wheel RADIUS. Dividing by it converts a chassis speed in metres per second into a wheel speed in radians per second', unit: 'm'},
    {symbol: 'L', meaning: 'half the wheelbase: the front-to-back distance from the robot\u2019s centre to a wheel', unit: 'm'},
    {symbol: 'W', meaning: 'half the track width: the side-to-side distance from centre to a wheel', unit: 'm'},
    {symbol: 'L + W', meaning: 'how much leverage a wheel has for turning. A wheel further from the centre must spin faster to produce the same rotation, which is why this appears only in the \u03c9 column'},
  ]}
/>

Here, $r$ is wheel radius. $L$ is the distance from the robot center to the front/back wheel line, and
$W$ is the distance from the robot center to the left/right wheel line. The term $L + W$ describes how
strongly robot rotation contributes to wheel speed. The signs depend on wheel order, roller direction,
and your chosen coordinate convention.

That matrix is exactly the FTC software problem: combine forward, strafe, and turn without writing four
unrelated motor formulas by hand. A clean implementation computes the four wheel speeds, finds the
largest magnitude, and scales all four together if any command is outside the legal motor range:

$$
\mathbf{w}_\text{normalized}
=
\frac{\mathbf{w}}{\max\!\left(1,\ \dfrac{|w_{FL}|}{\omega_\text{max}},\ \dfrac{|w_{FR}|}{\omega_\text{max}},\ \dfrac{|w_{BL}|}{\omega_\text{max}},\ \dfrac{|w_{BR}|}{\omega_\text{max}}\right)}
$$

Here $\omega_\text{max}$ is the wheel's free-speed rate in rad/s, and dividing by it is what makes each
term a *fraction of what the motor can do* — a plain number — so it can be compared against $1$. The
denominator is at least $1$, so small commands are not amplified, but large commands are compressed
while preserving the requested drive direction.

:::warning[Divide by the maximum, not by 1]
The bare form $\mathbf{w}/\max(1, |w_i|)$ appears in a lot of sample code, and it is only correct when
$\mathbf{w}$ is *already* a normalized motor power in $[-1, 1]$. The matrix above is the physical one —
the $\tfrac1r$ factor means every $w_i$ is in rad/s, which cannot be compared to a dimensionless $1$.
With a typical $r = 0.048$ m wheel, a gentle $v_x = 0.1$ m/s creep gives $w_i = 2.08$ rad/s on all four
wheels, and the bare form would scale that to full power. The
[mecanum kinematics](../../path-following/mecanum-kinematics.mdx) lesson takes the other route: it drops
$\tfrac1r$ and $(L+W)$ entirely and mixes in normalized power from the start, which is why it can
compare against $1$ directly.
:::

For a visual sanity check, open this Desmos graph: [Mecanum wheel-mixing visual](https://www.desmos.com/calculator/x39el6adbw).
Use its chassis and wheel vectors to connect the abstract vector $\mathbf{u}$ to the way an FTC robot
actually translates, strafes, and rotates; pose estimation is outside this lesson's scope.

Different teams use different sign conventions depending on wheel order and coordinate choices, but
the engineering idea is stable: wheel mixing is a linear transformation.

<Admonition type="warning" title="Hardware reality: matrices do not remove saturation">

The matrix may output one wheel speed larger than the motor can physically produce. Production code
must normalize or desaturate the wheel commands while preserving the direction of the requested chassis
motion as much as possible.

</Admonition>

<Admonition type="tip" title="FTC intuition">

If the robot drives diagonally when it should drive straight, check coordinate frames. If one wheel
clips before the others, check the vector after the matrix multiply. Linear algebra gives you places to
look.

</Admonition>
