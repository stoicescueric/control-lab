# State Space

<Difficulty level="Hard" />

<Abstract>
Your odometry says the robot is at (24, 48) and the AprilTag camera says (26, 47). Neither number is the robot's position; both are evidence about it, and something has to hold one estimate and decide how much of each reading to believe.
State space is that something: the list of variables that describe the robot right now, together with the rules that update them every loop.
</Abstract>

State space is the moment the previous lessons snap together. Calculus gives rates. Linear algebra
packages variables into vectors and matrices. Differential equations describe evolution. State space
puts all of that into a model you can run every loop.

## The loop you are about to write

The picture worth holding is a loop: state goes into the model, input changes it, sensors correct it,
and the next state becomes the starting point for the next loop.

## One sensor reading is not enough

An encoder gives a wheel position. An IMU gives heading. A distance sensor gives range. None of those
alone tells you "where the robot is" or "what the mechanism is doing."

The robot needs an estimate of the whole situation:

$$
\mathbf{x} =
\begin{bmatrix}
x \\
y \\
\theta \\
v \\
\omega
\end{bmatrix}
$$

That vector is the **state**. Here, $x$ and $y$ are field position, $\theta$ is heading, $v$ is linear
velocity, and $\omega$ is angular velocity.

## States, inputs, outputs, and the update rule

A state-space model has four pieces:

| Symbol | Meaning | FTC example |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbf{x}$ | state | pose, velocity, heading, lift height |
| $\mathbf{u}$ | input | motor voltages or drive commands |
| $\mathbf{y}$ | output | encoder, IMU, camera, distance sensor readings |
| $t$ or $k$ | time | continuous time or loop index |

In continuous time:

$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\mathbf{x} + B\mathbf{u}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'x\u0307 (x with a dot)', meaning: 'the rate of change of the whole state vector: how fast each entry of x is changing right now'},
    {symbol: 'A', meaning: 'how the state changes on its OWN, with no command. A coasting robot slowing down lives here'},
    {symbol: 'B', meaning: 'how your command pushes the state'},
    {symbol: 'u', meaning: 'the input you get to choose: the motor commands'},
  ]}
/>

Here, $\dot{\mathbf{x}}$ means "the rate of change of the state." $A$ describes how the current state
affects future motion, $B$ describes how inputs affect the state, and $\mathbf{u}$ is the input vector.

<StateSpaceIllustration />

In a robot loop, the discrete version is more directly useful:

$$
\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = A_d\mathbf{x}_k + B_d\mathbf{u}_k
$$

Here, $k$ is the current robot loop and $k+1$ is the next loop. The equation says: use the current
state and current input to predict the next state.

:::warning[$A_d$ is not $A$]
The subscript is doing real work. $A$ and $B$ describe *rates* — how fast the state is changing right
now — while $A_d$ and $B_d$ describe a *jump* across one whole loop period. They are different numbers,
and converting the first pair into the second is called **discretization**: for a loop period $\Delta t$
it is $A_d = e^{A\Delta t}$, not $A$ itself. Dropping the subscript is one of the most common beginner
errors in state-space code, and it is dimensionally impossible to miss once you look: $A$ carries units
of $1/\text{s}$, while $A_d$ is a pure number. See
[From Math to a Loop](../../software-architecture/discretization.mdx) and
[State Feedback](../../state-space-control/state-feedback.mdx) for how the conversion is done.
:::

Sensors read some part of the state:

$$
\mathbf{y}_k = C\mathbf{x}_k
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'y_k', meaning: 'what the sensors report at loop k, which is usually less than the whole state'},
    {symbol: 'C', meaning: 'which parts of the state a sensor can actually SEE. An encoder measures position but not heading, and C is where that fact is written down'},
  ]}
/>

Here, $\mathbf{y}_k$ is the sensor output at loop $k$, and $C$ maps the hidden full state into the
specific quantities the sensors can observe.

Those three matrices say:

- $A$ describes how the system evolves by itself,
- $B$ describes how inputs affect it,
- $C$ describes what the sensors can see.

## Odometry plus correction

Odometry is a state update. Every loop, wheel motion advances the pose:

$$
\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = f(\mathbf{x}_k, \Delta s, \Delta\theta)
$$

<EquationLegend
  title="Reading this equation"
  items={[
    {symbol: 'x_{k+1}', meaning: 'the pose at the NEXT loop: what we are solving for'},
    {symbol: 'x_k', meaning: 'the pose at THIS loop: where we currently believe we are'},
    {symbol: 'f(\u00b7)', meaning: 'some function. Naming it f rather than writing it out says "the details are elsewhere; what matters here is what goes in and what comes out"'},
    {symbol: '\u0394s', meaning: 'how far the robot travelled during this one loop, from the wheel encoders', unit: 'm'},
    {symbol: '\u0394\u03b8', meaning: 'how much it turned during that same loop', unit: 'rad'},
    {symbol: 'the whole line', meaning: 'where we will be next equals where we are now, combined with how far we moved and turned in between. Odometry is that sentence, repeated fifty times a second'},
  ]}
/>

But odometry drifts. A camera or AprilTag observation gives a measurement:

$$
\mathbf{z}_k = h(\mathbf{x}_k) + \text{noise}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'z_k', meaning: 'a MEASUREMENT at loop k, such as a pose computed from an AprilTag. Distinct from y above only by convention: z is the reading a filter will correct against'},
    {symbol: 'h(\u00b7)', meaning: 'the measurement model: what the camera SHOULD report if the state really were x_k'},
    {symbol: 'noise', meaning: 'the sensor\u2019s error, written explicitly because a filter\u2019s whole job is deciding how much of z to believe'},
  ]}
/>

A Kalman filter is state-space thinking with uncertainty attached. It predicts the next state from the
model, compares the predicted sensor reading to the real one, and corrects the state estimate by the
amount justified by the uncertainty.

<Admonition type="warning" title="Hardware reality: the state is only as good as the sensors">

If wheel slip corrupts odometry, if an IMU is noisy, or if a camera frame arrives late, the state vector
can look precise while being wrong. A state-space model earns its keep here by forcing you to write down
how much you trust each source, so a bad sensor shows up as a number you chose rather than a silent
assumption.

</Admonition>

<Admonition type="tip" title="FTC intuition">

When a later lesson says "pose estimate," "Kalman filter," or "state estimator," translate it as: a
state vector plus rules for prediction and correction.

</Admonition>
