# Complementary Filter

<Difficulty level="Medium" />

<Abstract>
This is where the module switches from filtering to fusion. A filter cleans one sensor; fusion
combines two sensors whose failures point in opposite directions. The complementary filter is the
simplest fusion there is — one blending constant — shown here on a real FTC pairing: a motor encoder
that is fast but slips away from the truth, and an absolute encoder that stays honest forever but is
noisy and slow.
</Abstract>

The previous filters cleaned a *single* sensor, and they all paid the same price: smoothing fights
jitter but does nothing about drift. No amount of averaging fixes a sensor whose *center* is walking
away from the truth. For drift you need a second sensor — one that never walks — and a rule for
combining the two.

<Problem title="The motor encoder is fast but lies slowly; the absolute encoder is honest but rough">

Put an arm on a robot and you usually have two ways to measure its angle:

- **Motor encoder** — counts ticks at the motor, fresh every loop, fine resolution. But it measures
  *the motor*, not the joint. Every bit of belt slip, chain skip, and gear backlash between the two
  accumulates, so the angle you compute from ticks drifts away from the true arm angle over a match.
  Excellent short-term, wrong in the long run.
- **Absolute encoder on the joint** — reads the joint angle itself, so it *cannot* drift. But the
  reading is noisy, and it often updates slower than your loop runs. True in the long run, rough
  right now.

Run on the motor encoder alone and the arm slowly forgets where it is. Run on the absolute encoder
alone and your control loop chews on noise. The two failures are opposites — *complementary* — which
is the whole trick.

</Problem>

<Theory title="High-pass the fast sensor, low-pass the honest one, add them">

Pass the motor encoder through a *high-pass* (keep its fast, accurate motion, block its slow drift)
and the absolute encoder through a *low-pass* (keep its slow, honest average, reject its jitter).
The two frequency responses are complements that sum to one, so nothing of the true signal is lost.
In code, all of that collapses into a single recursive line:

$$
\hat{\theta}_k = \alpha\,(\hat{\theta}_{k-1} + \Delta\theta_{\text{motor}}) + (1 - \alpha)\,\theta_{\text{abs}}
$$

<Formula>
  angle = <Amber>α</Amber>·(angle + <Teal>Δmotor</Teal>) + (1 − <Amber>α</Amber>)·<Rose>absolute</Rose>
</Formula>

The first term advances the estimate by however far the motor encoder moved this loop — smooth and
immediate. The second term gently pulls the estimate toward the absolute reading, bleeding off
whatever drift has crept in. $\alpha$ sits close to 1 (≈ 0.98): mostly ride the motor encoder, sip
just enough of the absolute sensor to stay anchored.

<ComplementarySplitExplorer />

This Desmos-style graph is the "complements" claim, drawn. The rose curve is the absolute encoder's
**low-pass** — it passes the slow, honest angle and rejects the fast noise. The teal curve is the
motor encoder's **high-pass** — it passes fast motion and blocks the slow drift. They cross at the
cutoff $f_c = 1/(2\pi\tau)$, where $\tau$ comes directly from $\alpha$ and the loop time, and at
*every* frequency the two responses **sum to exactly one** (the dashed line): nothing of the true
signal is lost, it is only reassigned to whichever sensor reports that band honestly. Push $\alpha$
toward 1 and the crossover slides left — you trust the motor encoder deeper into the slow band and
sip even less of the absolute sensor; the cost is that slip takes longer to cancel.

The demo below runs the filter on a moving arm: a motor-encoder-only estimate (smooth but sliding off
the truth), an absolute-only estimate (anchored but noisy and stepped — it updates slower than the
loop), and the fusion. Crank the slip drift to see why neither raw sensor is acceptable.

<Callout type="tip" title="Two wrongs make a right.">
Let it run: the motor-encoder line slides off the truth while the absolute line is fuzzy — yet the
fusion stays smooth <em>and</em> locked on. The compute cost is one multiply-add per loop.
</Callout>

</Theory>

<Deploy title="One line per loop">

The fast source enters as a *delta* (ticks moved since last loop), the honest source as an *absolute*
reading. Seed the estimate from the absolute sensor so the filter starts true:

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Fuse a motor encoder with an absolute encoder">{`double angle = absEncoder.getAngleDeg();      // seed from the honest sensor
final double ALPHA = 0.98;                    // motor-encoder trust
int lastTicks = motor.getCurrentPosition();

while (opModeIsActive()) {
    int ticks = motor.getCurrentPosition();
    double deltaDeg = (ticks - lastTicks) * DEG_PER_TICK;
    lastTicks = ticks;

    double absDeg = absEncoder.getAngleDeg();  // honest but noisy

    angle = ALPHA * (angle + deltaDeg) + (1 - ALPHA) * absDeg;
}`}</JavaCode>

:::note[The drone-textbook version]
Most textbooks teach this filter on a gyro + accelerometer pair: integrate the gyro's rate (fast,
drifts), anchor with the accelerometer's gravity direction (honest, shaky). Same math, different
sensors — and on FTC your IMU already does that fusion internally. The pattern is worth recognizing
in general: *any* fast-relative source plus *any* slow-absolute source fits, like a gyro heading
anchored by an occasional vision bearing.
:::

The complementary filter hand-picks a fixed blend $\alpha$. The Kalman filter computes the
*optimal* blend automatically, every step, from the actual uncertainties — the subject of the next
page.

</Deploy>
