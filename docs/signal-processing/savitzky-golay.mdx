# Savitzky-Golay Filter

<Difficulty level="Medium" />

<Abstract>
A moving average is a polynomial fit of the dumbest possible kind: it fits a flat line to the window,
which is why it flattens every peak it touches. Fitting a parabola instead costs nothing at runtime,
keeps the shape of the signal, and hands you a clean velocity estimate as a side effect.
</Abstract>

<Admonition type="note" title="Prerequisites and a starting path">

Start with [Moving Average](./moving-average.mdx) and [Low-Pass Filtering](./low-pass.mdx).
**Math used:** a small polynomial, least-squares fitting, and a derivative read from that fit. The
five-point implementation is already derived below; you do not need to solve normal equations or invert
matrices before using it.

</Admonition>

Your robot is closing on an AprilTag. The vision pose jitters a few centimetres frame to frame, so you
smooth it. The jitter goes away, and so does something you wanted.

<Problem>

## The average of a curve is not a point on the curve

Forget noise for a moment. Take a signal with no noise at all, sampled five times, that happens to
follow $y = 10 - x^2$ around the sample you care about:

| offset $x$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| reading $y$ | 6 | 9 | **10** | 9 | 6 |

The middle reading is exactly 10, and it is exactly right, because there is no noise. Now average the
five readings the way a moving average would:

$$
\frac{6 + 9 + 10 + 9 + 6}{5} = \frac{40}{5} = 8
$$

The filter reports 8 where the truth is 10. Not because a sensor lied, and not because the window was
too long. It is wrong by 2 on perfectly clean data, and making the readings cleaner will not move that
number at all.

This is the flaw worth understanding before reaching for a longer window. Averaging asks "what single
flat value best explains these five readings?", and for data that curves, the honest answer to that
question sits below the peak. The average is not failing at its job. Its job is the wrong job.

<Analogy>
<strong>You have seen this.</strong> Blur a photo to hide the grain and the edges go soft too. The
blur cannot tell noise from a real edge, because both are places where neighbouring values disagree.
</Analogy>

</Problem>

<Theory>

## Fit the shape the signal actually has

### A moving average is a least-squares fit

You already fit a line to data in [System Identification](../control-theory/system-identification.mdx):
you logged voltage and velocity, then picked the $k_S$ and $k_V$ that made the total squared error as
small as possible. Run that same machinery on the simplest model there is, a single constant $a$, and
ask for the $a$ that minimises

$$
E(a) = \sum_{i} \left(y_i - a\right)^2
$$

Differentiate, set to zero: $-2\sum_i (y_i - a) = 0$, so $\sum_i y_i = na$, so $a = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i y_i$.

That is the mean. **The moving average is not merely similar to a least-squares fit, it is one**: the
best fit of a horizontal line to the window. The 8 from the opening was not a bug, it was the correct
answer to a question about flat lines.

So if the model is what went wrong, change the model.

### Fit a parabola instead

Keep the window, keep least squares, and let the fitted shape curve. Number the samples by their
offset from the centre, $x = -2, -1, 0, 1, 2$, and fit

$$
y = a + bx + cx^2
$$

The value we want is the fitted curve at the centre of the window, and at $x = 0$ that is just $a$.
Minimising $\sum_i (y_i - a - bx_i - cx_i^2)^2$ means setting the derivative with respect to each of
$a$, $b$ and $c$ to zero, which gives three equations. Symmetric offsets make them collapse, because
$\sum x_i = 0$ and $\sum x_i^3 = 0$ kill every odd power:

$$
\begin{aligned}
5a + 10c &= S_0 \\
10b &= S_1 \\
10a + 34c &= S_2
\end{aligned}
$$

    {symbol: 'S_1', meaning: 'the sum weighted by offset, -2y(-2) - y(-1) + y(1) + 2y(2). It leans on how tilted the window is'},
    {symbol: 'S_2', meaning: 'the sum weighted by offset squared, 4y(-2) + y(-1) + y(1) + 4y(2). It leans on the outer samples, which is where curvature shows'},
    {symbol: 'a', meaning: 'the fitted value at the window centre, and the number the filter reports'},
    {symbol: 'b', meaning: 'the fitted slope at the centre, in units per sample. Free velocity, see below'},
    {symbol: 'c', meaning: 'the fitted curvature. Never reported, but its presence is what stops the peak being flattened'},
  ]}
/>

The middle equation stands alone and hands you $b = S_1/10$. The first and third are two equations in
$a$ and $c$. Double the first and subtract the third to eliminate $a$, then back-substitute:

$$
a = \frac{17S_0 - 5S_2}{35}
$$

### The fit collapses into five fixed numbers

Now expand that. $S_0$ and $S_2$ are both just sums of the five readings with known multipliers, so
group by reading:

$$
a = \frac{-3y_{-2} + 12y_{-1} + 17y_{0} + 12y_{1} - 3y_{2}}{35}
$$

Look at what survived. **No $y$ values appear in the coefficients.** The fit depends on the data, but
the *recipe* does not: it is always $(-3, 12, 17, 12, -3)$ divided by 35. Do the least-squares algebra
once, on paper, and at runtime a Savitzky-Golay filter is five multiplies and an add. It costs what a
moving average costs.

Two checks you can do by eye. The coefficients sum to $-3+12+17+12-3 = 35$, so a constant signal comes
through unchanged, exactly as it must. And on the five readings from the opening:

$$
\frac{-3(6) + 12(9) + 17(10) + 12(9) - 3(6)}{35} = \frac{-18 + 108 + 170 + 108 - 18}{35} = \frac{350}{35} = 10
$$

Exactly 10, the value the moving average missed by 2. The parabola through those five points *is*
$y = 10 - x^2$, and a fit that can represent the true shape reproduces it perfectly.

### Where the moving average went

Set the degree to 0 and the whole derivation shrinks back: with no $b$ or $c$ term the single equation
is $5a = S_0$, the weights become $(1, 1, 1, 1, 1)/5$, and you have the moving average again. It is
not a rival filter, it is the bottom rung of the same ladder. Degree 1 fits a straight tilted line and,
for a symmetric window, produces those same equal weights. Degree 2 is the first setting that can
represent a bend.

Drag the degree slider from 0 upward and watch the weight bars change shape. At degree 0 they are a
flat block. At degree 2 the outer two go negative, and those negative weights are exactly what lets
the output climb above every reading in the window when the signal is peaking.

<SavitzkyGolay />

<Callout type="warn" title="Negative weights cut both ways.">
A moving average can never leave the range of its window; it is an average, so it sits between the
smallest and largest reading. A Savitzky-Golay filter can overshoot past every sample it saw, because
two of its weights are negative. On a real peak that is the feature. On a single wild outlier it is a
liability: the filter treats the spike as evidence of curvature and leans further into it than an
average would. Gate outliers out before this filter: reject any sample that jumps further than the
mechanism physically can in one loop, and the spike never reaches the fit.
</Callout>

### Velocity, already paid for

The $b$ term has been sitting there unused. It is the fitted slope at the window centre, and the same
grouping trick turns $b = S_1/10$ into fixed weights:

$$
b = \frac{-2y_{-2} - y_{-1} + 0y_{0} + y_{1} + 2y_{2}}{10}
$$

That is a velocity estimate in units per sample; divide by the loop period for units per second. It
matters because the obvious way to get velocity from a noisy position signal, subtracting consecutive
readings, is the worst thing you can do to noise. Differencing two samples doubles the noise variance
while the signal change between them is tiny. This estimate uses all five samples and the same fit
that produced the smoothed position, so it costs one more dot product and nothing else.

</Theory>

<Deploy>

## Precompute the weights, then it is a dot product

Nothing about the derivation happens on the robot. The weights are constants, so the Control Hub only
ever does the multiply-and-add.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Five-point quadratic smoothing">{`private static final double[] SG_SMOOTH = {-3.0 / 35, 12.0 / 35, 17.0 / 35, 12.0 / 35, -3.0 / 35};

private final double[] window = new double[SG_SMOOTH.length];
private int filled;

double smooth(double reading) {
    System.arraycopy(window, 1, window, 0, window.length - 1);
    window[window.length - 1] = reading;
    if (filled < window.length) {
        filled++;
        return reading;              // no full window yet, so pass the raw value through
    }

    double total = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < window.length; i++) {
        total += window[i] * SG_SMOOTH[i];
    }
    return total;
}`}</JavaCode>

The returned number describes the *centre* of the window, which is two samples behind the reading you
just fed in. That is the honest price of a symmetric fit and it is the same price a symmetric moving
average pays. If two loops of delay is too much, refit the polynomial to be evaluated at the newest
sample instead of the middle. The demo's second button does exactly that, and the weights it shows
change accordingly: the fit is then extrapolating to the window edge rather than interpolating to its
centre, which is more responsive and noticeably noisier.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Centre velocity from the same window">{`private static final double[] SG_SLOPE = {-0.2, -0.1, 0.0, 0.1, 0.2};

double centreVelocity(double loopSeconds) {
    double perSample = 0;
    for (int i = 0; i < window.length; i++) {
        perSample += window[i] * SG_SLOPE[i];
    }
    return perSample / loopSeconds;
}`}</JavaCode>

When you tune this, log the raw readings alongside both outputs. The failure you are looking for is
the smoothed line clipping the tops of real peaks, which means the degree is too low for the shape or
the window is too long for how fast the signal moves.

<Exercise title="Compute both filters by hand">

Take the readings `4, 10, 12, 10, 5`, which are noise-free samples of a signal peaking at the centre.
Compute the five-point moving average, then the five-point quadratic Savitzky-Golay value using
$(-3, 12, 17, 12, -3)/35$. Which one recovers the peak, and by how much does the other miss?

Then answer the part that matters: if you doubled the window to nine samples, would the moving
average's error get better or worse, and why?

<Solution>

The average is $(4+10+12+10+5)/5 = 41/5 = 8.2$. The fit is
$(-3(4) + 12(10) + 17(12) + 12(10) - 3(5))/35 = (-12 + 120 + 204 + 120 - 15)/35 = 417/35 = 11.91$.

The true peak is 12. The average misses by 3.8, the fit by 0.09. The fit is not exact here because
these readings are not exactly a parabola, which is the realistic case: the fit is close, not magic.

Widening the window makes the average **worse**, and this is the important half of the question. A
longer window reaches further down both sides of the peak, so the values being averaged are further
below the centre and the result sinks lower. For a curved signal, the moving average's error grows
with window length. That is the opposite of the intuition that a longer window is a better filter, and
it is why "just average more samples" eventually stops helping.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why the outer weights are negative">

The weights $(-3, 12, 17, 12, -3)/35$ do not merely average. Explain, in terms of the fitted parabola,
what the two negative weights accomplish. It may help to ask what the filter should output when the
outer readings are far *below* the inner ones.

<Solution>

When the outer samples sit well below the inner ones, the window is curving downward, and a parabola
through those points peaks *above* the middle reading. To report that peak the filter has to push its
output up, and the only way to push up using the outer samples is to subtract them. The negative
weights are the filter measuring curvature: the further the edges fall below the centre, the more it
lifts the answer. Set the readings to a straight line and the curvature contribution cancels exactly,
which is why the filter leaves lines and constants untouched.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

You are filtering AprilTag range at 40 Hz while driving at roughly 1 m/s. Vision noise is about 2 cm
one-sigma. Sketch how you would choose between a 5-sample quadratic fit and a 21-sample quadratic fit,
in terms of how far the robot travels inside the window and how much smoothing you need.

</Exercise>

:::warning[Hardware reality]
Everything here assumes samples arrive at a fixed period, because the offsets $-2, -1, 0, 1, 2$ are
sample indices, not timestamps. Vision frames dropped by the pipeline break that assumption silently:
the filter keeps producing numbers, but the window now spans more time than you think and the velocity
estimate is scaled wrong. Timestamp your frames and either discard incomplete windows or refit the
polynomial against the real timestamps.
:::

Both this filter and the moving average still work on one sensor and can only fight jitter. Neither
can tell you that your vision pose has drifted a steady 10 cm to the left, because a smooth wrong
answer is still wrong. The rest of the module brings in a second sensor, starting with the
[complementary filter](./complementary.mdx).
