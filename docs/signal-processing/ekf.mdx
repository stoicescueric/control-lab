# Extended Kalman Filter

<Difficulty level="Hard" />

<Abstract>
The Kalman filter is optimal only for linear systems, but real localisation — camera projection,
headings, and landmark geometry — is full of trigonometry and division by depth. This lesson extends
the filter to nonlinear models by linearising them at each step with a Jacobian, then uses an
FTC AprilTag-style vision update as the concrete example.
</Abstract>

The Kalman filter is optimal — for *linear* systems. Robot localisation is not linear: the same
field landmark appears in different image locations and apparent sizes depending on robot position,
heading, and distance from the target. The Extended Kalman Filter is the adaptation that makes the
Kalman machinery work on the curved models real robots actually have.

This lesson uses Kou and Haggenmiller's FTC paper,
[*Extended Kalman Filter State Estimation for Autonomous Competition Robots*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04459),
as its applied anchor. They frame the FTC localisation problem as fusing three imperfect sources:
frequent motion measurements from a source verified against the
[Pose Estimation Contract](../path-following/pose-estimation-contract.mdx), less frequent computer-vision landmark position
measurements, and a physics-model prediction. This lesson's AprilTag model is an FTC-specific
landmark example built from the same EKF idea: each source is useful, but none of them is trustworthy alone.

<Admonition type="note" title="Readiness and concept stop">

Before implementing an EKF, be comfortable with partial derivatives/Jacobians, the matrix Kalman
predict-correct equations, a calibrated camera model, and an external source satisfying the
[Pose Estimation Contract](../path-following/pose-estimation-contract.mdx). The builder-level endpoint is
smaller: explain **linearize near the current estimate, reject an impossible measurement, then reuse
predict/correct**. You may stop there and use a maintained pose-estimator library; the camera derivation
below is optional audit depth.

</Admonition>

<Problem title="Drift and nonlinear vision corrupt field coordinates">

Standard wheel-encoder odometry drifts over time — most aggressively during wheel slip and hard
turns — steadily corrupting the robot's absolute field coordinates. The natural fix is to correct it
with an absolute sensor, but AprilTag vision is not a linear measurement of pose. The camera sees a
square in the image; that square shifts, stretches, and shrinks according to the robot's position,
heading, and depth from the tag.

A plain Kalman filter assumes a fixed linear measurement matrix $H$. It cannot directly express
"if the robot turns a little, the tag moves left in the image" or "if the robot moves closer, the
tag gets larger." Worse, the nonlinearity is uneven: one pixel of image error means a small
field-position error near the tag, but a much larger error when the tag is far away.

</Problem>

<TagPoseUncertainty />

This demo asks the filter's real question, in reverse: *the tag detection jitters by a pixel or so —
where could the robot be?* The robot is squared to the wall with its heading held by the IMU, so the
tag's horizontal offset $u$ and apparent size $s$ pin down its field position. Every frame, the
detection jitters (the amber square in the camera view), gets inverted back into a field position,
and lands as a green dot. The dot cloud is the truth about what pixel noise does to your pose. The
amber ellipse drawn over it is the EKF's answer: the pixel covariance $R$ converted into field
coordinates through the Jacobian.

Drag the robot around and two things stand out. Near the tag, the cloud is centimeters tight; far
away it balloons — and it balloons *along the line of sight*. The bearing to the tag (from $u$) stays
precise, while the range (from apparent size) degrades with the square of the distance; the readout
under the demo prices it in cm per pixel. One pixel is worth millimeters in one direction and
centimeters in the other, and that exchange rate — different at every pose, different in every
direction — is exactly what the Jacobian carries into the filter. It is why an EKF automatically
trusts a distant tag's bearing more than its range, with no special case written anywhere.

<Theory title="Optional derivation: linearise about the current mean">

The EKF keeps the Kalman predict-update heartbeat but, at each step, **linearises the nonlinear
motion and measurement models around the current mean estimate** using their *Jacobians* — matrices
of partial derivatives that give the local slope. A curve, viewed close to your current estimate, is
approximately its tangent line; on that local linearisation the ordinary Kalman update applies.

<EkfLinearization />

The demo puts that bet on trial. The gray bump on the depth axis is the filter's uncertainty; every
green particle is one sample of "the robot might actually be here," drawn from it. Each particle
passes through the real camera curve — apparent tag size $s = fL/X$, straight from this lesson's own
model — and lands on the left axis, piling up into the *true* output distribution. The amber outline
over the pile is what the EKF claims comes out: a Gaussian centered at $h(\hat{x})$ with a spread set
by the tangent's slope, which *is* the Jacobian.

With the estimate far from the tag, the curve is nearly straight across the whole uncertainty window,
so the pile and the amber Gaussian agree — the EKF is essentially exact. Now drag $\hat{x}$ close and
widen $\sigma$. The pile grows a long upper tail the Gaussian refuses to admit, and its center slides
away from $h(\hat{x})$. Nothing about the filter changed; the curve simply stopped being straight
where the filter is standing. That gap is exactly the covariance error the warning below describes,
and the reason the Jacobian is re-evaluated at every step, at the current estimate, rather than
computed once. The rule of thumb the picture leaves you with: linearisation is safe whenever the
curve looks straight across your $\pm2\sigma$ window — shrink the uncertainty (more frequent
corrections, better odometry) and even a harsh curve becomes locally linear.

A derivative is "how fast one output changes when one input moves." A Jacobian is the same idea
when the input and output are vectors. For a simplified AprilTag camera model, let the robot state
be:

$$
\mathbf{x} =
\begin{bmatrix}
x_R \\
y_R \\
\theta
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Here $x_R$ and $y_R$ are the robot's field position, and $\theta$ is the robot heading. Let the tag
be fixed at field position $(x_T, y_T)$. First convert the tag from field coordinates into camera
coordinates:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
X_c \\
Y_c
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\theta & \sin\theta \\
-\sin\theta & \cos\theta
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
x_T - x_R \\
y_T - y_R
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Here $X_c$ is forward depth in the camera frame: bigger $X_c$ means the tag is farther in front of
the camera. $Y_c$ is sideways offset in the camera frame: positive or negative depending on whether
the tag appears to one side. A simplified pinhole camera then predicts two visual measurements:

Before evaluating either division, require a finite calibrated detection and $X_c>X_{\min}>0$. A tag
behind the camera or too close to the pinhole singularity is a **rejected measurement**, not an extreme
correction. The same validity gate must run before the $1/X_c^2$ Jacobian terms.

$$
h(\mathbf{x}) =
\begin{bmatrix}
u \\
s
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
f\dfrac{Y_c}{X_c} \\
f\dfrac{L}{X_c}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

    {symbol: 's', meaning: 'the tag\u2019s apparent SIZE in the image, in pixels. A tag looks smaller the further away it is'},

    {symbol: 'dividing by X_c', meaning: 'the pinhole rule: apparent size falls off with depth. It is also why X_c must be gated away from zero before this is ever evaluated'},

Here $u$ is the tag's horizontal image offset, $s$ is its apparent image size, $f$ is focal length in
pixel-like units, and $L$ is the physical tag width. This is a simplified model, not a replacement
for the full camera calibration and pose-solve used by real AprilTag pipelines, but it captures the
important EKF fact: the measurement is not a linear combination of robot pose. It divides by depth
$X_c$, so moving near a tag changes the image much more aggressively than moving far from it.

The Jacobian asks six small questions:

$$
H =
\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x_R} & \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y_R} & \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial \theta} \\
\dfrac{\partial s}{\partial x_R} & \dfrac{\partial s}{\partial y_R} & \dfrac{\partial s}{\partial \theta}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The first row says how the tag's image center moves when the robot pose changes. The second row
says how the tag's apparent size changes when the robot pose changes. This matters because
covariance lives in robot-state space, while camera noise lives in image-measurement space; the
Jacobian is the local converter between them. The demo above is this same matrix with the heading
column pinned by the IMU — a 2×2 slice you can invert exactly, which is how each jittered detection
became one green dot on the field.

The paper replaces the linear Kalman model with two nonlinear functions:

$$
x_{a,t} = f(x_{a,t-1}, u_t) + q_t, \qquad z_t = h(x_{a,t}) + r_t
$$

<EquationLegend
  title="Symbols in the general nonlinear model"
  items={[
    {symbol: 'subscript t', meaning: 'the time step, playing the same role k does elsewhere on the site. This equation follows the source paper\u2019s notation'},
    {symbol: 'f(\u00b7)', meaning: 'the MOTION model: where the state goes next, given the input'},
    {symbol: 'h(\u00b7)', meaning: 'the MEASUREMENT model: what a sensor should report from a given state'},
    {symbol: 'u_t', meaning: 'the control input applied over this step, such as the wheel deltas'},
    {symbol: 'q_t', meaning: 'process noise: everything the motion model failed to predict, like wheel slip'},
    {symbol: 'r_t', meaning: 'measurement noise: the sensor\u2019s own error. Q and R are the covariances of these two'},
  ]}
/>

Here $x_{a,t}$ is the actual robot state at time step $t$; $u_t$ is the commanded motion or control
input; $f$ is the nonlinear state-transition function; $z_t$ is the measurement vector; $h$ maps a
state into the measurement the sensors should see; $q_t$ is process noise with covariance $Q$; and
$r_t$ is measurement noise with covariance $R$. The subscript $t-1$ means "previous loop" and $t$
means "current loop."

For the AprilTag projection model above, the cleanest way to see the Jacobian is as a chain rule.
The first matrix explains how image measurements change when camera-relative coordinates change:

$$
\frac{\partial h}{\partial (X_c,Y_c)}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
-fY_c/X_c^2 & f/X_c \\
-fL/X_c^2 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The second matrix explains how camera-relative coordinates change when the robot state changes:

$$
\frac{\partial (X_c,Y_c)}{\partial (x_R,y_R,\theta)}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
-\cos\theta & -\sin\theta & Y_c \\
\sin\theta & -\cos\theta & -X_c
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Multiplying them gives the measurement Jacobian:

$$
H =
\frac{\partial h}{\partial (X_c,Y_c)}
\frac{\partial (X_c,Y_c)}{\partial (x_R,y_R,\theta)}
$$

This is why AprilTag EKF updates feel subtle. The detector may report a simple image-space error,
but the filter must ask: at this distance and heading, how should that image error push the field
pose estimate? The Jacobian is the answer.

The EKF update then uses the *true* nonlinear $h$ for the residual while using the local Jacobian
$H$ for the covariance:

$$
H = \frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\bigg|_{\hat{x}}, \qquad
K = P^- H^\top (H P^- H^\top + R)^{-1}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'the superscript minus', meaning: 'a PRIOR: the value after prediction but before any measurement has corrected it. P\u207b is the covariance we carry into the update'},
    {symbol: 'the bar with \u0125', meaning: '"evaluated at the current estimate". A Jacobian is only valid near the point it was linearized about, and this says which point that is'},
    {symbol: 'S = HP\u207bH\u1d40 + R', meaning: 'the denominator: how uncertain the PREDICTED measurement is, model doubt and sensor noise added together'},
  ]}
/>

$$
\hat{x} = \hat{x}^- + K\,\big(z - h(\hat{x}^-)\big)
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'z \u2212 h(x\u0302\u207b)', meaning: 'the INNOVATION: what the sensor reported minus what the model expected. Note it uses the true nonlinear h, not the linear H, because this is an honest prediction rather than a covariance approximation'},
  ]}
/>

In that equation, $H$ is the measurement Jacobian, $\hat{x}^-$ is the predicted state before the
sensor update, $K$ is the Kalman gain, $P^-$ is predicted covariance, and $R$ is measurement-noise
covariance. Kou and Haggenmiller emphasize the important implementation detail: the Jacobians for
the nonlinear functions must be recalculated at each time step because the local slope changes as
the robot moves.

<Callout type="warn" title="The approximation has limits.">
Linearisation is only valid if the model is gently curved near the estimate. If the nonlinearity is
severe or the estimate is far off, the tangent misleads the filter — which is why the Unscented
Kalman Filter and particle filters exist. For typical robot localisation, the EKF is the standard choice.
The FTC paper reports the same limitation from another angle: when the loop interval $\Delta t$
gets large, Jacobian approximations and odometry accuracy both degrade.
</Callout>

</Theory>

<Deploy title="Where this runs on a robot">

This estimator assumes an external motion/pose source; first verify its frame, units, cadence, and
freshness with the [Pose Estimation Contract](../path-following/pose-estimation-contract.mdx).

This predict → linearise → update loop is the pattern behind fusing drifting odometry with intermittent
vision landmarks. In the AprilTag-style example here, odometry advances the state (predict), a detected
tag provides a nonlinear camera measurement (update), and the Jacobian maps pixel-level uncertainty into
pose-level uncertainty. In Kou and Haggenmiller's mecanum simulation, the state includes field position,
heading, and body velocity, while the measurement function combines landmark pose information with
odometry-derived encoder measurements. The matrix algebra is the same `linalg` used in the site demos.

Below, `Mat` is any small dense matrix type — the site demos use `src/lib/domain/linalg.js`.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Predict: every loop, tag or no tag">{`private Mat x;    // the state
private Mat P;    // its covariance

void predict(Mat wheelDeltas) {
    // F is the derivative at the point you are leaving, so it is evaluated
    // before x advances, not after.
    Mat F = motionJacobian(x, wheelDeltas);
    x = motionModel(x, wheelDeltas);
    P = F.times(P).times(F.transpose()).plus(Q);
}`}</JavaCode>

The correction is where a tag can destroy the estimate, so the validity gate runs before any arithmetic
that could divide by a bad depth:

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Correct with a tag, gated before the division">{`boolean correctWithTag(TagDetection tag, Mat z) {
    Mat camera = tagInCameraFrame(tag);    // finds xc and yc, divides by neither
    double xc = camera.get(0);
    double yc = camera.get(1);

    boolean valid = tag.isValid()
            && calibration.isValid()
            && z.isFinite()
            && Double.isFinite(xc) && Double.isFinite(yc)
            && xc > MIN_DEPTH;             // strictly in FRONT of the camera
    if (!valid) return false;              // never evaluate 1/xc or 1/(xc*xc)

    Mat H = measurementJacobian(x);        // partials of h, evaluated at x
    Mat y = z.minus(h(x));                 // residual, via the TRUE nonlinear h
    Mat S = H.times(P).times(H.transpose()).plus(R);
    Mat K = P.times(H.transpose()).times(S.inverse());

    x = x.plus(K.times(y));
    P = I.minus(K.times(H)).times(P);
    return true;
}`}</JavaCode>

Those last five lines are the same five ideas as the scalar filter you already implemented: gain from
the covariances, correct by gain times innovation, shrink the covariance. Everything else is
bookkeeping for the fact that the state is a vector and the measurement model is curved.

<Exercise title="Why F is evaluated first">

`predict` computes `F` from the current `x` and only then advances `x`. Work out why that order is not
interchangeable, in terms of what a Jacobian actually is.

<Solution>

`F` is the derivative of the motion model *at a point*, and the linearization is meant to describe the
step you are about to take — so the point is the one you are leaving. Advancing `x` first would
linearize about the destination and propagate `P` through a slope the robot never travelled. Over one
loop the error is small; over a long blind stretch it compounds into a covariance that no longer
describes the estimate.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="The gate is the point of the block">

The measurement Jacobian divides by `xc` and by `xc` squared. Work out what those become for a tag
detected at `xc = 0.001` m, and again for a negative `xc`. Then work out what a single such update does
to `P`, and how many good frames it takes to recover.

<Solution>

At 1 mm the partials scale by $10^3$ and $10^6$, so `H` is enormous and `K` hands the tag almost total
authority over the state. A negative `xc` is a tag behind the camera: the projection flips sign and the
correction is applied backwards. Either way `P` collapses to a tiny, confidently wrong covariance —
and because `P` only grows by `Q` per loop, recovery takes as many frames as it takes `Q` to re-inflate
it. That can be seconds. Rejecting the frame costs one loop; accepting it costs the match.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Where the linear H is not used">

The residual `y` uses the true nonlinear `h(x)`, while `H` appears only in `S`, `K`, and the covariance
update. Work out what breaks if you compute the residual as `z.minus(H.times(x))` instead.

<Solution>

`H` is the *slope* of `h` near `x`, not a substitute for `h` itself. The linearization is
$h(x) \approx h(\hat x^-) + H(x - \hat x^-)$, so at the linearization point the correct prediction is
exactly $h(\hat x^-)$. Writing `H * x` silently drops the constant term $h(\hat x^-) - H\hat x^-$ — and
that is not a small approximation error, it is a large offset that even depends on where you happened to
put the field origin ($h$ is translation-invariant; $H\hat x^-$ is not). Move the origin and the residual
changes. It stops meaning "how surprised was the camera" and the filter diverges on the first update.

</Solution>

</Exercise>
