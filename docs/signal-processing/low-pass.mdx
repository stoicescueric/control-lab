# Low-Pass Filter

<Difficulty level="Medium" />

<Abstract>
A low-pass filter (the exponential moving average) smooths a noisy signal by blending each new reading
with a fraction of the running estimate — one multiply, one add, one stored number. This lesson derives
that update, makes it correct across a varying loop time, and shows the direct trade-off between
smoothness and lag.
</Abstract>

The low-pass filter is the most valuable line of code in embedded control: it removes most
sensor jitter, costs one multiply and one add, and stores a single number. If a reading is too noisy
to act on, this is the first thing to try.

<Problem title="Fast noise rides on top of a slow signal">

Real signals usually change slowly; noise changes quickly. A raw distance-sensor reading or an
AprilTag bearing carries both, and the fast component is what ruins downstream logic — most painfully a PID
**derivative** term, which differentiates and therefore *amplifies* high-frequency jitter into motor
chatter. We need to admit the slow, real component and reject the fast, noisy one — to let **low**
frequencies **pass**.

<Analogy>
<strong>A bucket with a small hole.</strong> Pour noisy readings in; the water level cannot jump
instantly — it rises and falls gradually, ignoring quick splashes but tracking the overall trend.
The water level is the filtered output.
</Analogy>

</Problem>

<Theory title="One step toward each new reading">

Each loop, move the estimate a fraction $\alpha \in (0, 1)$ of the way from its current value toward
the new measurement:

$$
\hat{x}_k = \hat{x}_{k-1} + \alpha\,(z_k - \hat{x}_{k-1})
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'x\u0302_k', meaning: 'the filtered ESTIMATE this loop. The hat marks it as something computed, never something measured'},
    {symbol: 'z_k', meaning: 'the raw sensor reading this loop'},
    {symbol: '\u03b1', meaning: 'the blend factor between 0 and 1: what fraction of the way to move toward the new reading. Near 0 is heavy smoothing and slow response; near 1 barely filters at all'},
  ]}
/>

The term $(z_k - \hat{x}_{k-1})$ is the *innovation* — how surprising the new reading is. You accept
a fraction $\alpha$ of each surprise. This is the **exponential moving average**: past readings fade
geometrically, so it remembers everything while storing only one number.

| α | Behaviour | Character |
|---|---|---|
| near 0 | tiny steps → very smooth, slow, laggy | a heavy flywheel |
| near 1 | big steps → snappy, barely filters | a twitchy needle |

The name "exponential" is literal. Unroll the update a few steps and the filter turns out to hold a
weighted average of *every* past reading, with geometrically fading weights:

$$
\hat{x}_k = \alpha\,z_k + \alpha(1-\alpha)\,z_{k-1} + \cdots
+ (1-\alpha)^{k+1}\hat{x}_{-1}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: '(1\u2212\u03b1) powers', meaning: 'the weight each older reading still carries. Because it is a fraction raised to a power, influence decays geometrically, which is what makes the filter "exponential"'},
    {symbol: 'x\u0302_{-1}', meaning: 'the value the filter was seeded with. Its weight also fades, which is why a bad seed eventually washes out but a good one saves you the wait'},
  ]}
/>

The final term carries the actual initial estimate. On hardware, seed $\hat{x}_{-1}$ from the first valid
measurement so the filter does not invent a transient from zero.

### Optional derivation: connect $\alpha$ to a time constant

That fade sets the filter's **time constant** $\tau$ — how long it takes to absorb a change. Matching
the sampled filter exactly to an exponential response gives

$$
\alpha = 1 - e^{-\Delta t/\tau},
\qquad
\tau = -\frac{\Delta t}{\ln(1-\alpha)}.
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: '\u03c4 (tau)', meaning: 'the time constant, in SECONDS: roughly how long the filter takes to absorb 63% of a step change. Unlike \u03b1 it does not change meaning when the loop rate does', unit: 's'},
    {symbol: 'the exponential', meaning: 'recomputing \u03b1 from the measured \u0394t each loop is what keeps the filter\u2019s real-world speed fixed at \u03c4 even when the loop rate wobbles'},
  ]}
/>

The endpoints are limits, not divide-by-zero cases: $\alpha=0$ gives $\tau=\infty$ and accepts no new
sample weight, while $\alpha=1$ gives $\tau=0$ and copies the current measurement directly.

The common formula $\alpha = \Delta t/(\tau+\Delta t)$ is the backward-Euler approximation. It is
stable and often adequate when $\Delta t \ll \tau$, but it is not the exact step-response relation.
Either way, the same hard-coded $\alpha$ represents a different $\tau$ at a different loop rate, so
the deployable code computes $\alpha$ from a target $\tau$ and the measured $\Delta t$.

<EmaWeightsExplorer />

This Desmos-style graph is the whole filter in two pictures. The **left panel is the memory**: the
weight each past reading still carries, $\alpha(1-\alpha)^k$ — never exactly zero, always fading, and
storing all of it costs one number. The **right panel is the price**: fed a sudden step, the output
climbs to 63 % of the change after exactly $\tau$, and ~95 % after $3\tau$. Slide $\alpha$ down and
watch both at once — the memory stretches deeper into the past (smoother) while the step response
lags further behind (slower). That is the trade-off, quantified. Then change $\Delta t$ and notice
$\tau$ move with it at fixed $\alpha$: proof that $\alpha$ only means something *relative to your
loop rate*.

Sweep $\alpha$ from heavy smoothing to nearly raw, and use the step test to feel the same 63 % lag
you just measured.

<Callout type="math" title="The fundamental trade-off.">
More smoothing always means more delay. Low <code>α</code> rejects noise but lags; high <code>α</code>
is responsive but leaks jitter. You are choosing how much to trust the latest reading versus the
accumulated history — there is no setting that wins both.
</Callout>

</Theory>

<Deploy title="One line in the loop, tuned to dt">

The implementation is trivial; the engineering is choosing $\alpha$ from a target time constant and
the measured loop time, so behaviour is independent of how fast the loop happens to run.

<Pseudocode label="Low-pass a noisy sensor before using it">{`LOW-PASS FILTER      TAU is the time constant, in seconds

  state:  filtered, and a flag for whether we have seeded it yet

  each loop:
      dt  <- measured seconds since the last loop
      raw <- read the sensor

      if raw is not finite:
          take your sensor-fault path
          continue          # do NOT seed the filter with garbage

      if not seeded:
          filtered <- raw   # first good sample becomes the state outright
          seeded   <- true  # otherwise the filter spends a second crawling
                            # up from a fake zero

      else if dt is finite and > 0:
          alpha    <- 1 - exp(-dt / TAU)
          filtered <- filtered + alpha * (raw - filtered)     # the whole filter

      hand filtered to the controller, never raw

# The single line that matters is the update: move a fraction alpha of the
# way from where you are to what you just measured. Work it out by hand with
# alpha = 0.2, starting at 0, with a constant reading of 10. Tabulate ten
# loops. How many loops to pass 9? That number IS the response time.
#
# Now the alpha formula. Naively you would pick a constant like 0.2 - but
# then the filter's real-world speed changes whenever your loop time does.
# Work out what alpha = 1 - exp(-dt/TAU) gives at dt = TAU (about 0.63) and
# as dt grows large (approaching 1, meaning "trust the new reading"). That
# is why TAU is in seconds and alpha is recomputed every loop.
# Look up: "exponential moving average", "RC filter time constant".
#
# Work out the cost, because there always is one: the filtered value is
# always slightly BEHIND the truth. Estimate the lag for TAU = 0.08 s, and
# decide whether a controller can tolerate it.
`}</Pseudocode>

<Callout type="tip" title="Free high-pass filter.">
Subtract the low-pass output from the raw signal — <code>raw − filtered</code> — and you get a
<em>high-pass</em> filter that keeps only fast changes: useful for detecting taps, impacts, or edges.
</Callout>

The low-pass weighs all history with a single fading factor. The next filter instead averages a
fixed, equally-weighted window — a different trade-off worth understanding.

</Deploy>
