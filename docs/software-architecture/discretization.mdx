# From Math to a Loop

<Difficulty level="Medium" />

<Abstract>
Control theory is written in continuous time, but a robot runs it as a repeating loop over discrete
samples, so derivatives become differences, integrals become running sums, and raw encoder counts
become real-world units. This lesson bridges the math to the loop: the measured time step, unit
conversion, and the Nyquist limit on how fast you must sample.
</Abstract>

Every formula in this curriculum is written in continuous time: integrals, derivatives, smooth
curves. The hardware does none of that. This page is the bridge between the analysis and the
embedded reality, and skipping it is the single most common reason "correct" control code
misbehaves on a real robot.

<Problem title="The hub runs discretely, in the wrong units, at a variable rate">

Three hardware facts break the clean continuous math:

- **It loops.** A Control Hub executes *read -> compute -> actuate* perhaps 20-50 times per second.
  It cannot integrate or differentiate; it can only work with discrete samples.
- **It counts ticks.** A motor encoder reports dimensionless counts, not meters or radians. Nothing
  downstream, including PID gains, feedforward, and odometry, is meaningful until those counts are converted.
- **The loop time wobbles.** Vision frames, logging, and garbage collection make each iteration take
  a slightly different amount of time. Assuming a fixed period silently corrupts every time-based term.

Get these three things right and the rest of the site's math drops onto hardware unchanged. Get
them wrong and even a perfect controller drifts, lags, or oscillates.

</Problem>

<Theory title="Discretization, units, and the sampling limit">

**Time step.** Replace "an instant" with "one loop." A derivative becomes a difference; an integral
becomes a running sum; a state is advanced with forward Euler:

$$
\frac{dx}{dt} \approx \frac{x_k - x_{k-1}}{\Delta t}, \qquad
\int x\,dt \approx \sum_k x_k\,\Delta t, \qquad
x_{k+1} = x_k + \dot{x}_k\,\Delta t
$$

Every term carries a $\Delta t$, so $\Delta t$ must be the *measured* elapsed time, never a constant.

**Units.** The most important conversion in the codebase turns encoder ticks into real distance:

$$
\text{meters} = \text{ticks} \times \frac{2\pi r}{N_{\text{out}}}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'r', meaning: 'the wheel RADIUS here, not a setpoint. So 2\u03c0r is the distance covered by one full wheel revolution', unit: 'm'},
    {symbol: 'N_out', meaning: 'encoder ticks per revolution of the OUTPUT shaft: the one that actually turns the wheel'},
  ]}
/>

where $r$ is the wheel radius and $N_{\text{out}}$ is encoder counts per output-shaft revolution.
If your datasheet gives encoder counts per motor revolution instead, multiply by the gearbox ratio
first:

$$
N_{\text{out}} = N_{\text{motor}}G
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'N_motor', meaning: 'ticks per revolution of the MOTOR shaft, which is what a datasheet usually quotes'},
    {symbol: 'G', meaning: 'the gearbox reduction. The motor turns G times for one turn of the output, so the output shaft sees G times as many ticks'},
  ]}
/>

where $N_{\text{motor}}$ is counts per motor revolution and this lesson defines
$G$ as **motor turns per one output-shaft turn**. For a 20:1 reduction, $G=20$, so the output needs
20 motor revolutions' worth of ticks. If a vendor publishes the inverse convention, invert it once here.

<Callout type="math" title="Worked example (motor configuration)">
Suppose your selected motor configuration or datasheet gives <strong>537.7 encoder counts per output revolution</strong>
and the wheel diameter is <strong>96 mm</strong>, so $r = 0.048\ \text{m}$. One wheel revolution covers
<code>2*pi*0.048 ~= 0.3016 m</code>, so
<code>METERS_PER_TICK = 0.3016 / 537.7 ~= 0.000561 m/tick</code>.
</Callout>

**Sampling rate.** Between loops the robot is blind: it acts on the last sample until the next
arrives. Increase **dt** below and watch the smooth signal degrade into a coarse staircase and,
past the limit, alias into nonsense.

<Callout type="math" title="The Nyquist limit.">
To capture a motion that repeats <code>f</code> times per second, you must sample faster than
<code>2f</code>. Below that threshold the motion <em>aliases</em>: it appears slower than reality
or disappears entirely. A loop that is too slow cannot control a fast, springy mechanism regardless
of how well its gains are tuned.
</Callout>

</Theory>

<Deploy title="Measure dt; define the unit conversion once">

Read the true loop time every iteration, and centralize the tick conversion so every downstream
controller speaks real units.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Units, defined once">{`// Never recomputed inline, never retyped in a subsystem.
static final double WHEEL_R = 0.048;                 // meters
static final double TICKS_PER_OUTPUT_REV = 537.7;
static final double METERS_PER_TICK =
        2 * Math.PI * WHEEL_R / TICKS_PER_OUTPUT_REV;
static final double MAX_VALID_DT = 0.25;      // s; beyond this, something stalled`}</JavaCode>

Then measure the loop time every iteration, and seed before differentiating anything:

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Real loop time, with a seed and a dt guard">{`private final ElapsedTime timer = new ElapsedTime();
private boolean havePreviousSample;
private double lastMeters, integral, distanceFromVelocity, derivative;

void update(double error) {
    double meters = motor.getCurrentPosition() * METERS_PER_TICK;

    if (!havePreviousSample) {
        lastMeters = meters;
        timer.reset();
        havePreviousSample = true;
        return;                        // no dt yet, so no derivative yet
    }

    double dt = timer.seconds();
    timer.reset();

    if (!Double.isFinite(dt) || dt <= 0 || dt > MAX_VALID_DT) {
        lastMeters = meters;           // RE-SEED, do not just skip
        derivative = 0;
        return;
    }

    derivative = (meters - lastMeters) / dt;   // m/s over THIS interval
    integral += error * dt;                    // this rectangle's own dt
    distanceFromVelocity += derivative * dt;
    lastMeters = meters;
}`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="What the seed prevents">

Delete the `havePreviousSample` branch. `lastMeters` starts at 0, and the encoder might already read
4000 ticks when the OpMode starts. Work out the velocity that implies on the first loop, and what the
controller does with it.

<Solution>

4000 ticks is 2.24 m, and the first `dt` is about 20 ms, so the derivative comes out around
**112 m/s** — roughly 250 mph. A D term sees that once and slams the output to its limit before the
mechanism has moved at all. The seed costs one loop and removes the entire failure.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why re-seed instead of skip">

A debugger pause, a slow vision frame, or a stalled loop produces a `dt` far outside the valid band.
Suppose the loop stalls for 2 seconds while a mechanism keeps moving at 1 m/s. Work out what happens
on the *next* loop if the guard skips without re-seeding `lastMeters`.

<Solution>

The stall itself is survivable — 2 m of travel divided by 2 s is the right answer. The damage comes
one loop later: `lastMeters` is still from before the stall, so the next update divides 2 m of travel
by a 20 ms `dt` and reports **100 m/s** instead of 1 m/s. Re-seeding throws away one derivative;
skipping without re-seeding poisons the one after it.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why the integral cannot use an average dt">

Every `dt` above is measured. It is tempting to log the loop times, average them at the end, and
multiply once. Work out why that is not the same number.

<Solution>

The integral is $\sum e_i \Delta t_i$, and replacing each $\Delta t_i$ with the mean gives
$\bar{\Delta t} \sum e_i$. Those agree only if the error is uncorrelated with the loop time — and it
is not: loops run longest exactly when the robot is doing the most work, which is when the error is
largest. The bias is systematic, not noise. The practical objection is simpler still: the controller
needs the integral *this* loop, not after the match.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Callout type="warn" title="Hard-coding dt is a classic, invisible bug.">
If you assume <code>dt = 0.02</code> but the loop actually runs at 35 ms, the two time-dependent terms
break by the same factor of 1.75 — in <strong>opposite directions</strong>. The integral accumulates
about 1.75 times too <em>slowly</em>, because you add 0.02 s of area every 0.035 s of real time. The
derivative reads about 1.75 times too <em>large</em>, because you divide a 35 ms change by a 20 ms
interval. Neither produces an error message, and the pair is easy to misread as integral windup when it
is the opposite. Measuring <code>dt</code> costs almost nothing.
</Callout>
