# Splines

<Difficulty level="Hard" />

<Abstract>
This lesson is about *describing* a smooth path. Three families do it: a Bézier curve, shaped by dragging
control points that pull the curve toward them; a quintic Hermite spline, pinned to waypoints with specified
velocity and acceleration; and a natural cubic spline, which threads every point and solves for the slopes
itself. Béziers are intuitive to author by feel; quintic and natural-cubic splines pass exactly through your
points and, when their tangent stays nonzero, can provide continuous curvature. Here we build the curves; *following* them at speed (resampling,
speed planning, centripetal feedforward) is covered in [Pure Pursuit](./pure-pursuit.mdx).
</Abstract>

<Admonition type="note" title="Prerequisites and a starting path">

Start with [PID to a Point](./pid-to-point.mdx) to see why a route needs a continuous target, then
review [Linear Algebra](../preface/why-math-matters/linear-algebra.mdx) for vectors and
[Calculus](../preface/why-math-matters/calculus.mdx) for derivatives. You can use the Bézier viewer
without deriving a quintic polynomial; the derivative notation is the advanced layer.

</Admonition>

PID-to-point and predictive deceleration drive to *one* target. Real autonomous routines thread through
many waypoints, and the path *between* them has to be drivable — smooth not just in position but in how
sharply it bends. That bending is curvature, and curvature is where simple paths quietly fail.

<Problem>

## The robot jerks at every waypoint

Chain simple segments between waypoints and the path *looks* smooth: no kinks, continuous heading. But
run a follower along it and the robot snaps its steering at each join. The reason is invisible in the
drawing — **curvature** (how tightly the path turns, the second derivative) is *discontinuous* at the
waypoints. The follower must change steering angle instantly, which means infinite angular acceleration,
which the robot answers with a jerk and a tracking error exactly where the segments meet.

The cure is a curve description that controls curvature, not just position. Two families do it, from two
opposite philosophies.

</Problem>

<Theory>

## Continuity: how smooth is smooth enough

"Smoothness" is graded by how many derivatives match across a join:

| Class | Matches across the join | What stays continuous | The drivetrain feels |
|---|---|---|---|
| C⁰ | position | the path connects | a corner — a kink |
| C¹ | + first derivative | heading (tangent direction) | a sudden curvature change — a jerk |
| C² | + second derivative | second derivative; curvature on a regular curve | smooth, followable steering |

C² means that position, first derivative, and second derivative match across each join. If the path is
**regular** there (its tangent is nonzero), the curvature formula has a nonzero denominator and curvature
is continuous, so the follower never has to change steering instantly. The path families below offer
different authoring trade-offs.

### Core checkpoint: choose a regular quintic Hermite path

For a first follower, use the **quintic Hermite** representation below: each segment is pinned to its
endpoint poses and shares first and second derivatives at joins. Verify that its tangent never becomes
zero, then resample it for the follower. Bézier authoring and the natural-cubic solve are useful optional
comparisons, not prerequisites for that artifact.

### Optional comparison: Bézier control-point paths

A cubic Bézier is built from four **control points** $P_0, P_1, P_2, P_3$ and a weighted blend called
the Bernstein basis:

$$
\mathbf{B}(t) = (1-t)^3\,P_0 + 3(1-t)^2 t\,P_1 + 3(1-t)t^2\,P_2 + t^3\,P_3,
\qquad t \in [0,1]
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'P_0 \u2026 P_3', meaning: 'the four control points. Here the subscript numbers a control point, not a loop or a path sample'},
    {symbol: 't', meaning: 'the curve parameter, running 0 to 1 along ONE segment. It is not time, and it is not distance along the curve'},
    {symbol: 'B(t)', meaning: 'the point on the curve at parameter t'},
    {symbol: '(1\u2212t) and t powers', meaning: 'the Bernstein weights. At any t the four of them are non-negative and sum to exactly 1, which together are what keep the curve inside its control points'},
  ]}
/>

The weights are never negative and always sum to $1$, so the curve stays inside the control polygon's
convex hull. It passes through
**only the endpoints** $P_0$ and $P_3$; the interior points $P_1, P_2$ are *handles* that pull the curve
without touching it. They set the endpoint tangents directly:

$$
\mathbf{B}'(0) = 3(P_1 - P_0), \qquad \mathbf{B}'(1) = 3(P_3 - P_2)
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'B\u2032(0), B\u2032(1)', meaning: 'the derivative of the curve with respect to t at each end: the tangent direction there, scaled by 3'},
  ]}
/>

That is why Béziers feel good to author — drag a handle and the curve leans that way. The point on the
curve at any $t$ is found by **de Casteljau's algorithm**: repeatedly interpolate along the control
polygon. Scrub $t$ in the demo to watch it, and drag the handles to feel how $P_1, P_2$ steer the ends:

<BezierExplorer />

The problem shows up when you chain Béziers. Matching the tangent across a join (C¹) forces the next
segment's first handle to mirror the previous one, $Q_1 - Q_0 = P_3 - P_2$; demanding C² pins the second
handle too ($Q_2 = P_1 - 4P_2 + 4P_3$). So in a Bézier *chain*, continuity steals exactly the handles
that made Béziers convenient — you no longer get to place them freely.

### Core representation: Quintic Hermite waypoints and derivatives

The other philosophy: instead of points that pull the curve, specify the curve's own state at each
**waypoint**: position, velocity, and acceleration — and let one polynomial satisfy all of it. Because
you pin the values, the curve passes *through* every waypoint. This is the difference in one picture —
the same four points, used as Bézier control points versus Hermite waypoints:

<SplineCompare />

The green curve hits all four points; the blue one only the ends. A drivetrain routine usually wants the
green behavior: "go through these poses," not "go near them."

How many degrees of freedom does it take? Matching position, velocity, and acceleration at *both* ends of
a segment is six conditions, so you need a degree-5 — **quintic** — polynomial per coordinate:

$$
x(t) = c_5 t^5 + c_4 t^4 + c_3 t^3 + c_2 t^2 + c_1 t + c_0
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'c_0 \u2026 c_5', meaning: 'the six polynomial coefficients being solved for, one per power of t. Six unknowns is exactly what six constraints can pin down'},
    {symbol: 'x(t)', meaning: 'ONE coordinate of the curve. A 2-D path runs this whole construction twice, once for x and once for y'},
  ]}
/>

Evaluating it and its derivatives at $t = 0$ fixes the low coefficients straight from the start
conditions:

$$
c_0 = x(0), \qquad c_1 = x'(0), \qquad c_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}x''(0)
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'x(0)', meaning: 'the position at the start of the segment'},
    {symbol: 'x\u2032(0)', meaning: 'the velocity there: the first derivative with respect to t'},
    {symbol: 'x\u2032\u2032(0)', meaning: 'the acceleration there. The one-half appears because differentiating c\u2082t\u00b2 twice gives 2c\u2082'},
  ]}
/>

and the endpoint-at-$t{=}1$ conditions give the top three:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} c_3 \\ c_4 \\ c_5 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\phantom{-}10 & -4 & \phantom{-}\tfrac{1}{2} \\
-15 & \phantom{-}7 & -1 \\
\phantom{-}6 & -3 & \phantom{-}\tfrac{1}{2}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
x(1) - c_0 - c_1 - c_2 \\
x'(1) - c_1 - 2c_2 \\
x''(1) - 2c_2
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Now the C² join is automatic: set each interior waypoint's ending acceleration equal to the next
segment's starting acceleration, $x''_\text{seg}|_{t=1} = x''_\text{next}|_{t=0}$, and the curvature is
continuous through every regular join — no handles sacrificed. Apply the same matching of position,
first derivative, and second derivative to both $x(t)$ and $y(t)$.

### Optional alternative: natural cubic interpolation

Quintic Hermite asks *you* for the velocity and acceleration at each waypoint. What if you only have the
points and want the smoothest curve through them? A **natural cubic spline** keeps one cubic per interval
but, instead of being handed the slopes, *solves for them* by demanding curvature match everywhere: C⁰ at
the endpoints, and C¹ **and** C² across every interior knot. That is two conditions short of unique, and the
**natural** boundary closes the gap by setting the component second derivatives to zero at the two ends
($S''(x_0) = S''(x_n) = 0$)
— the spline leaves the path straight, like a drafting spline released at its tips.

Demanding C² everywhere collapses into a single **tridiagonal** system in the knots' second derivatives,
which the **Thomas algorithm** solves in $O(n)$ — no general matrix inversion.

Drag the waypoints below: the spline always threads through every point. Where the 2-D tangent remains
nonzero, its C² joins make the curvature comb continuous, and zero component second derivatives make the
comb approach zero at the ends.

<NaturalCubicSpline />

The trade against quintic Hermite: a natural cubic is **already C²**. On regular portions, curvature is
continuous across every interior knot — with the *fewest* degrees of freedom and no slope input required.
What you give up is control of the ends: the natural boundary pins component second derivatives to zero
(and therefore zero endpoint curvature when the tangent is nonzero) rather than letting you
choose it. A quintic's two extra coefficients per segment let you **prescribe** velocity and acceleration
(hence curvature) at every waypoint. Both are C²; the quintic just gives you the boundary controls. (And since a spline written as a
function $S(x)$ needs $x$ strictly increasing, a path that doubles back is fit as **two** splines
$x(t)$ and $y(t)$, each against the parameter $t$.)

<SplineContinuityIllustration />

Whichever family you use, the quantity a [pure-pursuit](./pure-pursuit.mdx) or feedforward follower
actually consumes is the **curvature** at each sample:

$$
\kappa(t) = \frac{x'y'' - y'x''}{\left(x'^2 + y'^2\right)^{3/2}}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: '\u03ba(t)', meaning: 'curvature at parameter t: 1 divided by the radius of the circle that best fits the curve there', unit: '1/in'},
    {symbol: 'x\u2032, y\u2032', meaning: 'first derivatives of each coordinate with respect to t'},
    {symbol: 'x\u2032\u2032, y\u2032\u2032', meaning: 'second derivatives with respect to t'},
    {symbol: 'the 3/2 power', meaning: 'this is what makes curvature independent of how fast t sweeps the curve. Reparameterize the same shape and \u03ba is unchanged'},
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="idea" title="Which one should you use?">
**Bézier** when a human authors the path by eye — the handles are a great editor, and Pedro
Pathing-style tools expose them directly. **Quintic Hermite** when you have exact poses to hit (Road
Runner's `splineTo` is quintic internally) and want C² for free. **Natural cubic** when you have only
points (no slopes) and want the cheapest C² interpolation — one tridiagonal solve, at the cost of zero
component second derivatives at the ends. Bézier and Hermite are convertible: a cubic Bézier *is* a cubic Hermite with
$m_0 = 3(P_1-P_0)$, $m_1 = 3(P_3-P_2)$ — so editors can show handles while the math runs on Hermite
coefficients.
</Callout>

</Theory>

## Evaluate either curve on the Control Hub

Both reduce to evaluating a polynomial (and its derivatives) per coordinate each loop.

<JavaCode label="Cubic Bezier segment, one coordinate at a time">{`// Four control points, t running 0 -> 1. Returns one coordinate at t.
double bezier(double p0, double p1, double p2, double p3, double t) {
    double u = 1 - t;
    return u * u * u * p0
            + 3 * u * u * t * p1
            + 3 * u * t * t * p2
            + t * t * t * p3;
}
`}</JavaCode>

<JavaCode label="Quintic Hermite segment">{`private double c0, c1, c2, c3, c4, c5;

// Position, velocity and acceleration pinned at both ends: six constraints,
// so six coefficients.
void build(double p0, double v0, double a0,
           double p1, double v1, double a1) {
    c0 = p0;
    c1 = v0;
    c2 = a0 / 2;

    double d0 = p1 - c0 - c1 - c2;   // what c3..c5 must absorb at t = 1
    double d1 = v1 - c1 - 2 * c2;
    double d2 = a1 - 2 * c2;

    c3 =  10 * d0 - 4 * d1 + 0.5 * d2;
    c4 = -15 * d0 + 7 * d1 - 1.0 * d2;
    c5 =   6 * d0 - 3 * d1 + 0.5 * d2;
}

double pos(double t) {
    return c0 + c1 * t + c2 * t * t
            + c3 * t * t * t + c4 * t * t * t * t + c5 * t * t * t * t * t;
}

double vel(double t) { ... }   // the derivative of pos
double acc(double t) { ... }   // the derivative of vel
`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="Check the Bezier weights">

Those four weights are the binomial coefficients $1, 3, 3, 1$ with powers of $u$ and $t$ that always sum
to 3. Check the identity: at any $t$, the four weights add to exactly 1. Try $t = 0$, $t = 1$, and
$t = 0.5$ by hand.

<Solution>

They are the terms of $(u + t)^3$, and $u + t = 1$ by construction — so the sum is $1^3 = 1$ for every
$t$. At $t = 0$ the weights are $(1,0,0,0)$ and the curve is at `p0`; at $t = 1$ they are $(0,0,0,1)$
and it is at `p3`; at $t = 0.5$ they are $(0.125, 0.375, 0.375, 0.125)$. Because the weights are
non-negative and sum to 1, every point on the curve is a weighted average of the control points, which
is why the curve never leaves their convex hull. Look up "Bernstein polynomial" and "de Casteljau's
algorithm."

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Derive the six coefficients">

Start with the easy half. Write `pos(t)`, `vel(t)` and `acc(t)` as general polynomials, substitute
$t = 0$, and read off $c_0$, $c_1$, $c_2$. Then the hard half: substitute $t = 1$ into all three, which
gives three equations in $c_3$, $c_4$, $c_5$. Solving that $3\times3$ system is where the $10$, $-15$
and $6$ come from. Do it once by hand and you will never mistrust the numbers again.

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why quintic and not cubic?">

Count the constraints. A cubic pins position and velocity at both ends — four constraints, four
coefficients. Work out what that leaves free at every junction, and what it feels like on a robot.

<Solution>

Acceleration. With only position and velocity matched, acceleration can jump discontinuously at every
junction between segments. Acceleration is proportional to commanded force, so a jump is a step change
in wheel torque: the robot lurches at each waypoint, the wheels slip, and the odometry that slip
corrupts is the same odometry the follower is using to decide where it is. Pinning acceleration too
needs six constraints, which needs a quintic. Look up "Hermite interpolation" and "quintic Hermite
basis functions."

</Solution>

</Exercise>

Build one curve for $x$ and one for $y$ per segment. For a quintic chain, sharing endpoint velocity and
acceleration between adjacent segments is what makes the whole path C².

For a **natural cubic**, solve the tridiagonal moment system once, then evaluate whichever piece $i$ the
parameter falls in with its own cubic
$S_i(t) = a_i + b_i(t - t_i) + c_i(t - t_i)^2 + d_i(t - t_i)^3$ — twice, for $x(t)$ and $y(t)$, on a 2-D
path.

Once you have a curve you don't follow it as a continuous function — you **resample it into a list of
points** by arc length (the warning below) and feed that to a follower. Doing that *at speed* — tagging each
point with a curvature-limited velocity and a centripetal feedforward — is the velocity-planning job covered
in [Pure Pursuit → Following a spline at speed](./pure-pursuit.mdx#following-a-spline-at-speed).

</Deploy>

<Admonition type="warning" title="Hardware reality: t is not distance">

For these curves, the parameter $t$ is not arc length — equal steps in $t$ are unequal steps in metres,
fast on straights and bunched in tight turns. Before a follower uses the path, **reparameterize by arc
length** (integrate $\sqrt{x'^2 + y'^2}$ and resample) or your speed control will surge and stall around
curves.

</Admonition>
