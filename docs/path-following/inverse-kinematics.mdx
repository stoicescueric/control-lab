# Inverse Kinematics

<Difficulty level="Medium" />

<Abstract>
Inverse kinematics answers a practical question: you know where you want the mechanism to be, so what
joint angles or wheel speeds get it there? In FTC that comes up with arms and turrets, and also in
mecanum drive mixing, where a desired robot motion becomes four wheel powers.
</Abstract>

<Admonition type="note" title="Math used">

Right-triangle trigonometry, sine and cosine, `atan2`, and square roots.
[Jacobians and numerical solvers](../advanced-topics/inverse-kinematics-jacobians.mdx) are an optional
next step for mechanisms without a tidy geometric solution.

</Admonition>

Drivers and autonomous routines think in goals: put the claw here, point the wrist there, slide the
robot sideways. Motors think in angles and speeds. Inverse kinematics translates from the first to the
second. For a drivetrain, the goal is a robot motion $(v_x, v_y, \omega)$. For an arm, it's a claw
position $(x, y)$ measured from the shoulder pivot. Either way the question is the same: what should
the motors do?

## First ask: how many freedoms does the mechanism have?

Team 21229 Quality Control's workshop makes a good point before any equations: start by counting
**degrees of freedom**, the independent ways something can move. A slide has one. A single arm joint has
one. A turret with an arm has at least two. A claw that has to stay level adds a constraint.

Compare how many the **target** needs with how many the **mechanism** has:

| Case | What it means on a robot | What the solver must do |
|---|---|---|
| Too few mechanism freedoms | A one-joint arm cannot place a claw at every $(x, y)$ point. | Reject unreachable targets or redesign the mechanism. |
| Same number of freedoms | A two-link arm can often solve $(x, y)$ with geometry. | Use a closed-form trig/vector solution when possible. |
| Extra mechanism freedoms | A turret + arm + slide may have many ways to reach the same point. | Choose a configuration using constraints, preferences, or numerical IK. |

So there isn't always one answer. Sometimes there are two (elbow up and elbow down), and sometimes
infinitely many, and the code has to pick one that avoids collisions, stays within joint limits, keeps
the claw level, and doesn't jump between two very different poses.

## Forward kinematics: angles to position

It's easier to start with the opposite question, **forward kinematics**: if you know the joint angles,
where is the claw?

Take a two-link arm with a first link of length $L_1$ (shoulder to elbow) and a second of length $L_2$
(elbow to claw). The shoulder angle $\theta_1$ is measured from horizontal. The elbow angle $\theta_2$ is
measured from the first link, so $\theta_2 = 0$ means the arm is straight.

The first link is the hypotenuse of a right triangle, so it reaches:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_1 &= L_1\cos(\theta_1) \\
y_1 &= L_1\sin(\theta_1)
\end{aligned}
$$

The second link points at $\theta_1 + \theta_2$, because it's mounted on the end of the first:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_2 &= L_2\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) \\
y_2 &= L_2\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)
\end{aligned}
$$

Add the two together for the claw position:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
x &= L_1\cos(\theta_1) + L_2\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) \\
y &= L_1\sin(\theta_1) + L_2\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)
\end{aligned}
}
$$

Check it: with both links 10 in, $\theta_1 = 90°$ (straight up) and $\theta_2 = -90°$ (elbow bent back
down to horizontal), the claw is at $x = 0 + 10\cos 0° = 10$ and $y = 10 + 10\sin 0° = 10$.

### Inverse kinematics: position to angles

Robot code usually needs the reverse: the routine says "put the claw at $(x, y)$," and the motors need
angles.

Draw a triangle from shoulder to elbow to claw. The side from the shoulder to the claw has length

$$
r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}
$$

by Pythagoras. If $r$ is more than $L_1 + L_2$, the target is out of reach. If it's less than
$|L_1 - L_2|$, it's too close, because one link can't fold through the other.

The triangle's three sides are $L_1$, $L_2$, and $r$, so the law of cosines gives the angle at the
elbow:

$$
\boxed{
\cos(\theta_2) =
\frac{r^2 - L_1^2 - L_2^2}{2L_1L_2}
}
$$

(The sign is flipped from the textbook form because $\theta_2$ is measured from straight, not the
inside angle of the triangle.) Then:

$$
\theta_2 = \pm \cos^{-1}\!\left(
\frac{r^2 - L_1^2 - L_2^2}{2L_1L_2}
\right)
$$

The $\pm$ is the choice between elbow up and elbow down. Both put the claw in the same place, but one
might hit the robot frame and the other might not.

For the shoulder angle, aim straight at the target, then back off by the angle inside the triangle
between "straight at the target" and "along the first link":

$$
\boxed{
\theta_1 =
\operatorname{atan2}(y, x)
-
\operatorname{atan2}\!\left(
L_2\sin(\theta_2),
L_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_2)
\right)
}
$$

`atan2(y, x)` is like $\tan^{-1}(y/x)$, but it looks at the signs of both inputs, so it gets the angle
right even when the target is behind the shoulder. The second `atan2` is that inside angle: measured
along the first link, the claw is $L_1 + L_2\cos\theta_2$ out and $L_2\sin\theta_2$ to the side.

### The drivetrain version

The mecanum mixer from [Mecanum Kinematics](./mecanum-kinematics.mdx) is inverse kinematics too. The
goal is a robot motion $(v_x, v_y, \omega)$, and the wheel powers are:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} w_{FL} \\ w_{FR} \\ w_{BL} \\ w_{BR} \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & -1 & -1 \\
1 & \phantom{-}1 & \phantom{-}1 \\
1 & \phantom{-}1 & -1 \\
1 & -1 & \phantom{-}1
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \\ \omega \end{bmatrix}
$$

A desired motion goes in and motor commands come out. The difference is that they're wheel speeds
instead of joint angles.

## A clean FTC implementation

Keep inverse kinematics out of the OpMode. The OpMode asks for a target, and a separate class returns
the angles. That way you can test the math at a desk without the robot.

A check you can do in your head: both links 10 in, target $(10, 10)$. Then $\cos\theta_2 = 0$. Elbow up
uses $\sin\theta_2 = -1$ and gives shoulder $90°$, elbow $-90°$, with the elbow at $(0, 10)$. That's the
pose from the forward-kinematics check above. Elbow down gives shoulder $0°$, elbow $+90°$, with the
elbow at $(10, 0)$. Both put the claw at $(10, 10)$.

<JavaCode label="Two-link arm inverse kinematics">{`// x, y is the wrist target measured FROM THE SHOULDER PIVOT. Units are
// arbitrary but must match link1 and link2; angles come back in radians.
ArmSolution solve(double x, double y, boolean elbowUp) {
    if (!(link1 > 0 && link2 > 0)) throw new IllegalStateException("links");

    double r = Math.hypot(x, y);

    double minReach = Math.abs(link1 - link2);   // cannot fold tighter
    double maxReach = link1 + link2;             // cannot stretch past
    boolean reachable = minReach <= r && r <= maxReach;

    double cosElbow = (r * r - link1 * link1 - link2 * link2)
            / (2 * link1 * link2);
    cosElbow = clamp(cosElbow, -1, 1);           // nearest pose, not a NaN

    double sinMagnitude = Math.sqrt(Math.max(0, 1 - cosElbow * cosElbow));
    double sinElbow = elbowUp ? -sinMagnitude : sinMagnitude;
    double elbow = Math.atan2(sinElbow, cosElbow);

    double shoulder = Math.atan2(y, x)
            - Math.atan2(link2 * sinElbow, link1 + link2 * cosElbow);

    return new ArmSolution(shoulder, elbow, reachable);
}
`}</JavaCode>

The sign of `sinElbow` is the only difference between the two answers. With $+x$ to the right and $+y$
up, a negative sine puts the elbow above the line from shoulder to target.

<Exercise title="The workspace is a ring">

`minReach` and `maxReach` describe a ring, not a filled circle. When does the hole in the middle
disappear, and what does that suggest about choosing link lengths?

<Solution>

When `link1 == link2`, `|link1 - link2|` is zero, and the arm can fold back to reach the pivot. Equal
links give the most usable area for a given total length, which is why most two-link arms are close to
equal. Unequal links leave a dead zone right in front of the shoulder, which is often where you wanted
to pick something up.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Derive both angle lines">

The triangle has sides `link1`, `link2`, and `r`. Write $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$ with $C$ the inside
angle at the elbow, and solve for $\cos C$. The code's elbow angle is measured from straight, not the
inside angle. How are they related?

Then the shoulder: draw the triangle, mark the angle between "straight at the target" and "along link 1,"
and check that its two sides are `link2*sin(elbow)` and `link1 + link2*cos(elbow)`.

<Solution>

The law of cosines gives $\cos C = (l_1^2 + l_2^2 - r^2)/(2l_1l_2)$. That's the negative of the code's
formula, because the code's angle is $\pi - C$: zero when the arm is straight, not when it's folded
shut, and $\cos(\pi - C) = -\cos C$.

For the shoulder, drop a perpendicular from the wrist onto the line of link 1. Along link 1 the wrist is
$l_1 + l_2\cos(\text{elbow})$ out, and across it $l_2\sin(\text{elbow})$, so their `atan2` is the angle
between aiming at the target and aiming along link 1.

It has to be `atan2`, not `atan`, because `atan` gives the same answer for a target in front of the
shoulder and one directly behind it.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

Then turn angles into commands for your mechanism. That part is specific to your robot: encoder
offsets, gear ratios, feedforward, joint limits, and homing all go there, not in the solver.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Using the solver inside a subsystem">{`void moveWristTo(double xInches, double yInches) {
    ArmSolution target = solve(xInches, yInches, true);

    if (!target.reachable()) {
        report("arm target outside reachable workspace");
        return;                       // do not command a clamped pose
    }

    shoulderController.setTarget(toShoulderTicks(target.shoulder()));
    elbowController.setTarget(toElbowTicks(target.elbow()));

    // Radians are unreadable while tuning.
    log(Math.toDegrees(target.shoulder()), Math.toDegrees(target.elbow()));
}
`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="Where the offsets live">

The solver returns angles and knows nothing about encoders, gear ratios, zero offsets, or motor limits.
What does `toShoulderTicks` need to know, and where does a mechanical zero offset belong?

<Solution>

Ticks per revolution, the gear ratio between motor and joint, which direction is positive, and the
encoder reading at the geometric zero. Those are all facts about this robot, and none of them are in
`solve`, which is why `solve` can be checked with a calculator.

The zero offset goes in `toShoulderTicks`. If you put it in the solver, the math stops matching the
diagram it came from, and every check by hand disagrees with the code.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

</Deploy>

<Admonition type="warning" title="Reachable doesn't mean safe">

The math only knows the link lengths. It doesn't know your claw hits the slides or that the intake cable
gets pulled tight. After the solver gives an answer, still apply joint limits, collision rules, and
current limits before moving anything.

</Admonition>

<Admonition type="warning" title="Nearly straight or nearly folded is touchy">

When the arm is almost straight or almost folded, a small change in the target can need a big change in
the joint angles. These poses are called singularities. Avoid paths that pass through them unless the
controller is slow, current-limited, and well tested.

</Admonition>

<Admonition type="tip" title="Check angles before tuning">

Most IK bugs are angle-convention bugs: the zero is off, an encoder sign is flipped, or inches and ticks
got mixed up. Before tuning anything, print the measured shoulder and elbow angles, move the arm by
hand, and check that positive angles go the way the equations say.

</Admonition>
