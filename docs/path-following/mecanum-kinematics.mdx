# Mecanum Kinematics

<Difficulty level="Easy" />

<Abstract>
A mecanum drive can slide in any direction without turning. Each wheel can only push along a 45°
diagonal, and combining the four pushes gives any motion you want. This lesson turns that into the few
lines of code you actually write: mix the command into four wheel powers, rotate it for field-centric
driving, shape the joystick for fine control, and add a dead zone so a drifting stick doesn't creep the
robot.
</Abstract>

This is one of the easier lessons on the site. It's adding and subtracting, done every loop, and by the
end you should be able to write a teleop drive from memory.

<Problem>

## Three numbers in, four motors out

The driver gives you three numbers from two thumbsticks: how much to go **forward**, how much to
**strafe** (slide sideways), and how much to **turn**. The robot has four motors. Every loop you turn
those three numbers into four wheel powers, and if a sign is wrong the robot strafes when you meant to
spin.

The obvious "add the sticks together" code has two more problems. It drives relative to the robot, so
once the robot turns around the driver is fighting the controls. And pushing two sticks at once can ask a
wheel for more than 100% power, which the motor clips, bending a straight line into a curve. Both are
fixed below.

</Problem>

<Theory>

## One idea: each wheel pushes along a diagonal

A mecanum wheel has small rollers around its edge set at **45°**. Because of that angle, a spinning
wheel doesn't push straight forward like a normal wheel. It pushes along a **diagonal**. The four wheels
are mirrored so their diagonals make an **X** when you look down at the robot.

One wheel can only push along its diagonal, but add up all four pushes and interesting things happen.
The sideways parts can cancel, leaving pure forward motion. The forward parts can cancel, leaving pure
sideways motion. Or they can combine into a spin. Getting the motion you want is a matter of choosing the
four wheel speeds.

### Mixing: add the three things you want

Work in the robot's own frame first. Call the three parts of the command $v_x$ (forward), $v_y$
(strafe, positive to the **left**), and $\omega$ (turn, positive **counter-clockwise**). Each wheel gets
a share of all three. For front-left, front-right, back-left, and back-right:

$$
\begin{aligned}
w_{FL} &= v_x - v_y - \omega &
w_{FR} &= v_x + v_y + \omega \\
w_{BL} &= v_x + v_y - \omega &
w_{BR} &= v_x - v_y + \omega
\end{aligned}
$$

Check it one input at a time. Push only forward, $v_x = 0.5$: all four wheels get 0.5 and the robot
drives straight. Push only left, $v_y = 0.5$: FR and BL get +0.5 and FL and BR get −0.5, and the X
pattern pushes the robot sideways. Turn only, $\omega = 0.5$: the right wheels get +0.5 and the left
wheels −0.5, and it spins in place.

The same four lines written as one matrix multiplication, which is how the ECAM and gm0 references at
the end present it:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} w_{FL} \\ w_{FR} \\ w_{BL} \\ w_{BR} \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & -1 & -1 \\
1 & \phantom{-}1 & \phantom{-}1 \\
1 & \phantom{-}1 & -1 \\
1 & -1 & \phantom{-}1
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \\ \omega \end{bmatrix}
$$

In the full physical version the $\omega$ column is $\pm(L+W)$, where $L$ and $W$ are the distances from
the robot's center to the wheels, because wheels farther out have to travel further to turn the robot.
For teleop with normalized powers you can fold that into $\omega$ and use $\pm 1$. Push the sticks in the
demo and watch each wheel's number come from those sums:

### Field-centric: rotate the command first

Drivers think in field directions, like "toward the goal," not robot directions. So before mixing,
rotate the driver's command from the field frame into the robot's frame using the robot's heading
$\theta$ from the IMU.

Check with a case you can picture. The robot faces left, $\theta = 90°$, and the driver pushes the stick
away from them, toward the far wall. From the robot's point of view, the far wall is to its right, so
the robot should strafe right: $v_x = 0$, $v_y$ negative. The rotation by $-\theta$ does exactly that:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{bmatrix}_\text{robot}
=
\begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{bmatrix}_\text{field}
$$

With $\theta = 90°$, $\cos\theta = 0$ and $\sin\theta = 1$, so a field command of $(1, 0)$ becomes
$(0, -1)$ in the robot frame: no forward, full right. Now "push the stick away from you" means the same
direction on the field whichever way the robot faces. Turn field-centric on in the demo and spin the
heading to feel it.

### Desaturation: keep the robot driving straight

The sums can come out bigger than 1. Full forward plus full turn asks the front-right wheel for
$1 + 0 + 1 = 2$, and the front-left for $1 - 0 - 1 = 0$. Clip the front-right to 1 and leave the rest
alone, and the ratio between the wheels changes, so the robot curves away from where you aimed.

Instead, divide **all four** by the biggest one, but only when it's over 1. Here that's 2, so the wheels
become 1 and 0: the same turn-and-drive, just at half speed:

$$
\text{scale} = \max\big(1,\ |w_{FL}|, |w_{FR}|, |w_{BL}|, |w_{BR}|\big),
\qquad w_i \leftarrow \frac{w_i}{\text{scale}}
$$

Commands that already fit pass through unchanged, because of the 1 in the max. Oversized ones shrink but
keep their ratios, so the robot still goes the direction you asked. (gm0's tutorial divides by
$\max(|v_x| + |v_y| + |\omega|,\ 1)$ instead, which is a slightly more cautious version of the same
idea.) In the demo, push forward and turn all the way and watch the "desaturate ÷" readout go above 1.

### Scaling the joystick for fine control

A raw stick is linear: half stick gives half speed. But the moments that need precision, like creeping
up to score, are small-stick moments, and a linear response makes them twitchy. Bending the response
fixes it: small pushes give very small speeds, and full stick still gives full speed. Raise the stick's
**magnitude** to a power $k \ge 1$. (Shape the magnitude rather than each axis separately, or the
direction gets distorted.)

$$
m_\text{out} = m_\text{in}^{\,k}, \qquad m_\text{in} = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}
$$

$k = 1$ is linear; $k = 2$ (squared) is the classic FTC feel — half stick now gives a quarter speed, so
slow moves are gentle and the top end is untouched. Some drivers prefer cubic ($k = 3$).

### Dead zone: ignore a stick that won't sit still

Thumbsticks don't return exactly to zero. Let go and the stick might report 0.03 forever, so the robot
**creeps** on its own. A **dead zone** fixes that: any value smaller than a threshold $d$ counts as zero.

A plain dead zone causes a small lurch, though. The moment the stick passes $d$, the output jumps from 0
to $d$. The fix is to stretch what's left of the range so the output starts at 0 at the edge of the dead
zone and reaches 1 at full stick. Combined with the response curve:

$$
\text{shape}(s) =
\begin{cases}
0 & |s| \le d \\[4pt]
\operatorname{sign}(s)\,\left(\dfrac{|s| - d}{1 - d}\right)^{\!k} & |s| > d
\end{cases}
$$

Here $s$ is the raw stick value, $d$ the dead zone (around 0.06 is typical), and $k$ the curve exponent.
With $d = 0.06$ and $k = 2$, a stick at 0.53 gives $((0.53 - 0.06)/0.94)^2 = 0.5^2 = 0.25$.

Drag the input below. The flat red band is the dead zone, and past it the blue curve climbs to full. A
dead zone of 0.05 to 0.10 stops the drift without the driver noticing it.

## A clean field-centric teleop drive

The whole thing is short as long as you keep the order: **shape, rotate, mix, desaturate.**

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Stick shaping: dead zone and response curve">{`// One stick axis in [-1, 1] -> a shaped command in [-1, 1].
// DEADZONE ~ 0.06, DRIVE_CURVE ~ 2.0.
double shape(double s) {
    double a = Math.abs(s);
    if (a < DEADZONE) return 0;      // ignore stick drift at rest

    double scaled = (a - DEADZONE) / (1 - DEADZONE);
    return Math.signum(s) * Math.pow(scaled, DRIVE_CURVE);
}
`}</JavaCode>

`scaled` runs from 0 at the edge of the dead zone to 1 at full stick, so the output doesn't jump.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Field-centric mecanum drive">{`// fwd +1 away from the driver, strafe +1 to the driver's left,
// turn +1 counter-clockwise, all [-1, 1]. heading is field-frame radians.
void driveFieldCentric(double fwd, double strafe, double turn, double heading) {
    double mag = Math.min(1, Math.hypot(fwd, strafe));   // shape the MAGNITUDE
    double dir = Math.atan2(strafe, fwd);
    double m = shape(mag);

    double fwdC = m * Math.cos(dir);
    double strafeC = m * Math.sin(dir);
    double w = shape(turn);

    double c = Math.cos(heading), s = Math.sin(heading);  // rotate by -heading
    double vx =  fwdC * c + strafeC * s;
    double vy = -fwdC * s + strafeC * c;

    double fl = vx - vy - w;                             // mix the wheels
    double fr = vx + vy + w;
    double bl = vx + vy - w;
    double br = vx - vy + w;

    double scale = Math.max(1, Math.max(
            Math.max(Math.abs(fl), Math.abs(fr)),
            Math.max(Math.abs(bl), Math.abs(br))));
    setWheelPowers(fl / scale, fr / scale, bl / scale, br / scale);
}
`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="What the exponent does">

Make a table of `scaled^1` and `scaled^2` at 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1.0. Where does the driver gain fine
control, and what do they give up? Then, with `DEADZONE = 0.06` and no stretching, how big is the jump
when the stick leaves the dead zone?

<Solution>

Squaring gives 0.0625, 0.25, 0.5625, and 1.0, so the bottom half of the stick now covers only a quarter
of the output range. Slow, precise driving gets most of the stick's travel. The cost is at the top,
where the same stick movement changes the speed more, so full-speed driving is a bit twitchier.

Without stretching, the first output past the dead zone is 0.06 instead of 0, a 6% power jump. On a
drivetrain that's a visible twitch every time the driver starts moving.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why the magnitude is shaped, not each axis">

Push the stick to exactly 45° and square `fwd` and `strafe` separately. What angle does the robot drive
at? Now try 60°.

<Solution>

At 45° it still drives at 45°, because both parts are equal and get squared the same way. At 60° the
parts are 0.5 and 0.866. Squared, they're 0.25 and 0.75, and `atan2(0.75, 0.25)` is 71.6°, not 60°.
Shaping each axis separately bends every direction except straight along an axis or a diagonal.
Shaping the magnitude and rebuilding from the angle keeps the direction.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Derive the four signs">

Each roller turns wheel spin into a push at 45°. Draw all four from above and add up the pushes for
"all wheels forward," then "left side forward, right side backward," then one diagonal pair (FL, BR)
driven against the other (FR, BL).

<Solution>

All four forward: the sideways parts cancel in pairs and the forward parts add, so the robot drives
straight. Left side forward, right side backward: the forward and sideways parts both cancel, leaving
only a twist, so the robot spins in place. One diagonal against the other: the forward parts and the
twist cancel, leaving a sideways push, which is the strafe. Each sign in the mixer just says which of
those three a wheel contributes to, and in which direction.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why desaturate all four by one factor">

Why divide every wheel by the same number instead of clipping each one at 1?

<Solution>

The ratios between the four wheels are what set the direction and turn rate. Clip one wheel and the
robot goes somewhere you didn't ask, usually curving, and it happens exactly when you're pushing
hardest. Scaling all four keeps the ratios, so the robot goes the right way, just slower. When you've
asked for too much, slower is the right way to fall short.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

In an FTC OpMode the sticks map in like this. The gamepad's Y axis is upside down, so negate it:

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Reading the gamepad">{`double fwd    = -gamepad1.left_stick_y;   // up on the stick = forward
double strafe = -gamepad1.left_stick_x;   // left on the stick = +left (our convention)
double turn   = -gamepad1.right_stick_x;  // left = counter-clockwise
double heading = imu.getRobotYawPitchRollAngles().getYaw(AngleUnit.RADIANS);
driveFieldCentric(fwd, strafe, turn, heading);`}</JavaCode>

<Admonition type="warning" title="Strafing is weaker than driving forward">

The angled rollers lose a little grip sideways, so a mecanum robot strafes slower than it drives forward
at the same power. The usual fix, from gm0, is to multiply the strafe command by about **1.1** before
mixing. Tune the exact number on your robot.

</Admonition>

<Admonition type="warning" title="Mecanum drive encoders slip">

You can use mecanum wheel encoders for position tracking, but the rollers slip sideways, so strafing
distance and heading drift more than the ideal math suggests, especially when driving hard or pushing.
Before an autonomous follower relies on that position, set up your localization library and check it
with the [Pose Estimation Contract](./pose-estimation-contract.mdx), including how far off it is after
returning to the start.

</Admonition>

<Admonition type="tip" title="Most mecanum bugs are one reversed motor">

If one wheel fights the others, flip that motor's direction in the configuration, not the math. Pick
one convention (+x forward, +y left, +ω counter-clockwise), write it in a comment, and make every wheel
follow it.

</Admonition>
