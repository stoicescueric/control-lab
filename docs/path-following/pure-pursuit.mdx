# Path Following (Pure Pursuit)

<Difficulty level="Hard" />

<Abstract>
Pure pursuit turns a planned path into smooth steering with one geometric rule: continuously aim at a
"carrot" a fixed look-ahead distance along the path. This lesson derives the curvature that arc
requires and shows how the look-ahead distance trades tracking tightness against smoothness.
</Abstract>

The robot gets a current field pose from a source verified against the
[Pose Estimation Contract](./pose-estimation-contract.mdx), and it knows where it should go from a planned path. Path following is
the controller that connects them: it converts the path into a continuous stream of drive commands.
**Pure Pursuit** is the classic, deployable method — a single geometric rule that has steered FTC
and FRC robots for years.

<Problem title="Waypoint-to-waypoint driving is slow and discontinuous">

The obvious approach drives straight to waypoint 1, stops, rotates, drives to waypoint 2, and so on.
It is slow, jerky, and stresses the drivetrain at every stop. Competition autonomous needs to follow
a *continuous* curve at speed, correcting smoothly for the inevitable pose error rather than halting
to re-aim. The controller must answer, every loop: given my current pose and this path, what
curvature should I drive?

<Analogy>
<strong>A carrot on a stick.</strong> Fix a carrot a constant distance ahead of you, always on the
path, and simply walk toward it. As you advance, the carrot slides further along the path, so you
continuously curve to follow it. That moving target is the <strong>lookahead point</strong>.
</Analogy>

</Problem>

## Core checkpoint: Basic Pure Pursuit

<Theory title="One arc to a lookahead point">

The algorithm is three steps, run every loop:

1. **Find the lookahead point**: walk along the path until you are a fixed lookahead distance $L_d$
   away. That point is the carrot.
2. **Transform it into the robot frame**, where $+x$ is forward and $+y$ is left. Call the lateral
   offset $y$.
3. **Compute the arc**: exactly one circular arc starts at the robot (tangent to its heading) and
   passes through the carrot. Its curvature, and the resulting turn rate at speed $v$, are:

$$
\kappa = \frac{2y}{L_d^{\,2}}, \qquad \omega = \kappa\,v
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'y', meaning: 'the carrot\u2019s sideways offset in the ROBOT frame, not the field frame. Positive means the carrot is to the robot\u2019s left', unit: 'in'},
    {symbol: 'L_d', meaning: 'lookahead distance: the radius of the circle centred on the robot', unit: 'in'},
    {symbol: '\u03ba', meaning: 'curvature of the arc that reaches the carrot; 1 / turning radius', unit: '1/in'},
  ]}
/>

<Formula>
  curvature = <Rose>2·y / Ld²</Rose> &nbsp;&nbsp; turn rate <Amber>ω = curvature · v</Amber>
</Formula>

This drops out of geometry: the chord to the carrot has length $L_d$ and lateral offset $y$, so the
unique tangent circle through both has radius $r = L_d^2 / (2y)$, hence curvature $1/r = 2y/L_d^2$.
If the carrot is dead ahead ($y = 0$), curvature is zero — drive straight.

<Callout type="warning" title="This derivation assumes a nonholonomic robot — mecanum isn't one.">
The "follow one arc to the carrot" rule is built on a <strong>nonholonomic</strong> drive: a tank, car,
or differential robot can only move along its heading and must <em>steer</em> (change curvature) to go
sideways. That constraint is the whole reason a single arc — coupling forward speed to turn rate
$\omega = \kappa v$ — is the natural command. A <strong>mecanum</strong> drive is
<strong>holonomic</strong>: it can translate in any direction <em>independently</em> of where it is
facing. So on mecanum you have two choices. (a) Run pure pursuit as written — treat the chassis like a
diff-drive, steering toward the carrot and letting heading follow the path tangent; simple, and fine for
gentle paths. (b) Exploit the holonomy — drive the velocity vector <em>straight at the lookahead point</em>
and control heading with a <em>separate</em> loop. Option (b) is exactly what the holonomic followers in
this module do: <a href="./pid-to-point">PID-to-point</a> and the
<a href="./guided-vector-fields">guided vector field</a> command $(v_x, v_y, \omega)$ directly and never
need the curvature trick. Use pure pursuit's curvature form when the geometry genuinely couples steering
and motion (tank/diff); reach for decoupled translation + heading on mecanum.
</Callout>

The single tuning parameter is the lookahead distance, and it is a direct trade-off:

| Lookahead $L_d$ | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| **Too small** | tracks tightly but oscillates — over-corrects and weaves |
| **Too large** | very smooth but cuts corners and rounds off tight features |
| **Tuned** | smooth tracking with stable, gentle correction |

In the demo, the robot follows the blue path; the <span style={{ color: "#ffc24d" }}>amber
carrot</span> is its lookahead target. Drag the waypoints to reshape the path and sweep the lookahead
from small (watch it oscillate) to large (watch it cut corners).

### Finding the carrot precisely: line–circle intersection

"Walk along the path until you are $L_d$ away" is fine for densely-sampled waypoints, but the exact
carrot is where a **circle of radius $L_d$ around the robot crosses the path**. On a straight segment
from $\mathbf{A}$ to $\mathbf{B}$, parametrize the point as $\mathbf{E}(t) = \mathbf{A} + t(\mathbf{B} -
\mathbf{A})$ and solve $\lVert \mathbf{E}(t) - \mathbf{P} \rVert^2 = L_d^2$ for the robot at $\mathbf{P}$.
With $\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{B} - \mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{f} = \mathbf{A} - \mathbf{P}$ that is a quadratic
in $t$:

$$
(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{d})\,t^2 + 2(\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{d})\,t + (\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{f} - L_d^2) = 0
$$

<EquationLegend
  title="Symbols in the intersection test"
  items={[
    {symbol: 'A, B', meaning: 'the two endpoints of the path segment being tested'},
    {symbol: 'P', meaning: 'the robot\u2019s position, the centre of the lookahead circle'},
    {symbol: 'd', meaning: 'B \u2212 A, the segment as a vector'},
    {symbol: 'f', meaning: 'A \u2212 P, from the robot to the segment\u2019s start'},
    {symbol: 't', meaning: 'how far along the segment a solution lies. 0 is at A, 1 is at B, and anything outside [0,1] missed the segment'},
  ]}
/>

Solve with the quadratic formula and keep roots in $[0,1]$. Choose the larger root on the **first segment
at or ahead of stored continuous progress**. Skip duplicate zero-length segments before division. The
stored segment index plus $t$ makes progress monotone. If there is no intersection and the endpoint is
inside the lookahead circle, hand off to that exact endpoint. If the robot is simply far from the path,
retain the last accepted progress and recover toward it rather than irreversibly jumping to the end.

<JavaCode label="Segment-circle intersection">{`// Segment A->B, robot position P, lookahead radius Ld. Returns the
// parameter t in [0, 1] where the circle crosses, or null.
Double intersect(Vector2d A, Vector2d B, Vector2d P, double Ld) {
    if (!(Ld > 0)) throw new IllegalArgumentException("Ld");
    Vector2d d = B.minus(A);
    Vector2d f = A.minus(P);
    double a = d.dot(d);
    if (a < EPS) return null;          // duplicate point; skip this segment

    double b = 2 * f.dot(d);
    double c = f.dot(f) - Ld * Ld;
    double disc = b * b - 4 * a * c;
    if (disc < 0) return null;         // the circle never reaches this segment

    double tFar  = (-b + Math.sqrt(disc)) / (2 * a);
    double tNear = (-b - Math.sqrt(disc)) / (2 * a);
    if (tFar >= 0 && tFar <= 1) return tFar;
    if (tNear >= 0 && tNear <= 1) return tNear;
    return null;
}
`}</JavaCode>

<JavaCode label="Lookahead point, with progress carried between loops">{`// The "from" progress is where we were on the path last loop.
// Returns a point exactly Ld from the robot, plus the new progress.
Lookahead findLookahead(List<Vector2d> path, Vector2d robot, double Ld,
                        Progress from) {
    for (int i = from.segment(); i < path.size() - 1; i++) {
        double minT = (i == from.segment()) ? from.t() : 0;
        Double t = intersect(path.get(i), path.get(i + 1), robot, Ld);
        if (t != null && t >= minT) {
            return new Lookahead(pointAt(path, i, t), new Progress(i, t));
        }
    }

    Vector2d last = path.get(path.size() - 1);
    if (last.minus(robot).norm() <= Ld) {
        return new Lookahead(last, from);       // we are finishing
    }
    return new Lookahead(pointAt(path, from), from);  // hold; do NOT jump
}
`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="Derive the quadratic">

Write the condition "the point $A + t(B-A)$ is exactly $L_d$ away from $P$" as
$|A + t(B-A) - P| = L_d$, square both sides, and collect powers of $t$. You should get
$at^2 + bt + c$ with exactly the $a$, $b$, $c$ above. Then work out what a negative discriminant means
geometrically, and why `tFar` is preferred when both roots are valid.

<Solution>

Squaring gives $|f + td|^2 = L_d^2$, and expanding the dot product produces
$(d\cdot d)t^2 + 2(f\cdot d)t + (f\cdot f - L_d^2) = 0$ — the three coefficients above.

A negative discriminant means the line carrying the segment never comes within $L_d$ of the robot: the
circle and the line miss entirely, so there is no carrot on this segment.

`tFar` is preferred because when the circle crosses one segment twice, the far root is the one further
*along* the path. Taking the near root would aim the robot at a point behind the one it is already
driving toward, and progress would stall. Look up "line-circle intersection."

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="What the two guards prevent">

Starting the scan at `from.segment()` and refusing `t < min_t` is what stops the robot snapping onto an
earlier part of a path that crosses itself. Work out what a figure-eight path does without them.

<Solution>

At the crossing, the lookahead circle intersects both branches. An unguarded search returns whichever
comes first in the point list — the earlier branch — so the robot latches onto the part of the path it
already drove and loops the first half of the eight forever. The guards make progress monotonic: the
carrot can only ever move forward along the path.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

### Fixed vs. dynamic lookahead

The table above is the case *against* a single fixed lookahead: the value that smooths a fast straight
is the same value that cuts a tight corner. A **fixed** lookahead picks one compromise for the whole
path. A **dynamic** lookahead changes $L_d$ as the robot drives, escaping the trade-off:

- **Speed-scaled**: make lookahead grow with speed magnitude,
  $L_d = \operatorname{clamp}(k\,|v|,\ L_\text{min},\ L_\text{max})$. The magnitude matters when reverse
  motion is supported.
- **Curvature-scaled**: shrink lookahead where the upcoming path bends sharply (large $|\kappa|$ from the [spline](./quintic-splines.mdx)) and grow it on gentle sections, so tight features are tracked and straights stay calm.

$$
L_d = \operatorname{clamp}\big(k_v\, |v|,\ L_\text{min},\ L_\text{max}\big)
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'k_v', meaning: 'how much lookahead to buy per unit of speed. Because it multiplies a speed to give a distance, it is a time', unit: 's'},
    {symbol: 'L_min, L_max', meaning: 'the shortest and longest lookahead allowed, whatever the speed says', unit: 'in'},
  ]}
/>

Dynamic lookahead is strictly more capable but adds more settings to tune, and a lookahead that changes too
abruptly can itself induce steering jitter — rate-limit it. If you find yourself fighting a fixed
lookahead on a path with both fast and tight sections, that is the signal to make it dynamic, or to
switch to a [guided vector field](./guided-vector-fields.mdx) that drops the lookahead idea entirely.

## Production extension: adaptive speed and spline paths

### Adaptive pure pursuit: plan the speed, not just the steering

Plain pure pursuit answers *where to steer*. **Adaptive** pure pursuit — the version Team 1712 (DAWGMA)
popularised in FRC — adds the other half: *how fast to go at every point*, decided ahead of time from
the path's own geometry. The robot then "adapts" its speed to the curve, braking for tight turns and
opening up on straights, with no hand-authored velocity plan. It is a three-pass precompute over the
densely-sampled path, plus a one-line runtime limit.

**1 — Curvature at every point.** Take each point with its two neighbours $P_{i-1}, P_i, P_{i+1}$ and
fit the unique circle through them; the curvature is $\kappa_i = 1/R$. The compact way to get $R$ is the
circumscribed-circle (Menger) formula — with triangle side lengths $a, b, c$ and area $A$:

$$
R = \frac{a\,b\,c}{4A}, \qquad \kappa_i = \frac{1}{R} = \frac{4A}{a\,b\,c},
\qquad A = \tfrac12\big\lvert (P_i - P_{i-1}) \times (P_{i+1} - P_i)\big\rvert
$$

<EquationLegend
  title="Symbols in the Menger curvature formula"
  items={[
    {symbol: 'P_i', meaning: 'the i-th point of the densely resampled path. The subscript counts points along the path, not loops in time'},
    {symbol: 'a, b, c', meaning: 'the three side lengths of the triangle formed by P\u1d62\u208b\u2081, P\u1d62, P\u1d62\u208a\u2081', unit: 'in'},
    {symbol: 'A', meaning: 'the AREA of that triangle. Not the segment endpoint A above, and not a state matrix', unit: 'in\u00b2'},
    {symbol: 'R', meaning: 'radius of the unique circle through the three points', unit: 'in'},
  ]}
/>

A straight run gives $A = 0$, hence $\kappa = 0$ (infinite radius); the two endpoints have no neighbour
pair, so their curvature is set to zero.

Drag the three points below to feel it: the tighter you bend them, the smaller the circle and the larger
$\kappa$ — pull them into a line and the curvature collapses to zero.

<CurvatureCircle />

**2 — A speed cap from curvature.** If a physical lateral-acceleration limit is known, use:

$$
v_i^{\max} = \min\!\left(v_\text{path},\ \sqrt{\frac{a_{\mathrm{lat,max}}}{|\kappa_i|}}\right)
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'v_i^max', meaning: 'the fastest the robot may travel at path point i'},
    {symbol: 'v_path', meaning: 'the cruise speed you asked for, before any corner limits it', unit: 'in/s'},
    {symbol: 'a_lat,max', meaning: 'the sideways acceleration the tires can hold before sliding. This is what a corner spends', unit: 'in/s\u00b2'},
  ]}
/>

This follows from $a_\text{lat}=v^2|\kappa|$. An empirical alternative sometimes used in team code is
$v=k_\kappa/|\kappa|$, where $k_\kappa$ has units $1/\mathrm{s}$. It has a different curvature dependence
and is not a discrete version of the square-root physical limit. The live planner uses the physical cap
and labels the lateral-acceleration units.

**3 — Forward acceleration and backward braking passes.** Start at the real initial speed and walk
forward:

$$
v_i \leftarrow \min\!\left(v_i,\ \sqrt{v_{i-1}^2 + 2a_\text{max}d_i}\right).
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'd_i', meaning: 'the straight-line distance across the gap this pass steps over: from point i-1 to i in the forward pass, and from i to i+1 in the backward pass. Each pass pairs a speed with the gap it must change speed across', unit: 'in'},
    {symbol: 'a_max', meaning: 'the forward acceleration and braking limit, a positive magnitude in both passes', unit: 'in/s\u00b2'},
  ]}
/>

With $v_0=0$, point 1 is positive whenever $d_1>0$ and $a_\text{max}>0$; the forward pass does not trap
the robot at zero. A speed cap alone can still reach a slow corner too fast, so next walk the path
*backwards* from the requested zero end speed and clamp each point to the
fastest speed from which it could still decelerate to its successor — the very
[braking kinematics](./pid-to-point.mdx) $v_f = \sqrt{v_i^2 + 2 a d}$, run over the path
offline:

$$
v_i \leftarrow \min\!\left(v_i,\ \sqrt{v_{i+1}^2 + 2\,a_\text{max}\,d_i}\right)
$$

with $d_i$ the distance between consecutive points. After this pass every stored velocity is guaranteed
decelerable to a stop at the end.

**4 — An additional runtime actuator limit.** The offline passes make the plan kinematically feasible.
Also cap how fast the command may change each measured loop:

$$
\lvert \Delta v\rvert \le a_\text{max}\,\Delta t
$$

a one-line rate limiter applied to the target velocity. It protects execution when timing or actuators
depart from the plan; it is not what makes a zero-start profile move.

### Building the path: inject, then smooth

Adaptive pure pursuit needs a *dense, smooth* list of points — the curvature and velocity passes are
only as good as the spacing. You can get one from the [splines](./quintic-splines.mdx) in the previous
lesson, or with the lighter trick the DAWGMA paper uses:

1. **Inject** evenly-spaced points along each straight segment between your hand-placed waypoints (a
   spacing of ~6 in works well), turning a few corners into hundreds of closely-spaced points.
2. **Smooth** them with a gradient-descent pass that trades staying near the original points against
   straightening local bends, iterated to convergence:

$$
P_i \leftarrow P_i + \underbrace{a\,(P_i^\text{orig} - P_i)}_{\text{stay near input}}
  + \underbrace{b\,(P_{i-1} + P_{i+1} - 2P_i)}_{\text{pull toward neighbours}}
$$

<EquationLegend
  title="Symbols in the smoothing pass"
  items={[
    {symbol: 'a, b', meaning: 'the two smoothing weights, and a different a and b from the triangle sides above. a is how hard a point is held near where it started; b is how hard it is pulled toward its neighbours'},
    {symbol: 'P_i^orig', meaning: 'where point i sat before any smoothing, kept so the path cannot drift away from what you drew'},
  ]}
/>

with $b$ near $0.75$–$0.98$ (larger = smoother), $a = 1 - b$, repeated until the total change drops below
a tolerance. Update the points **in place**, so that each sweep already sees the new $P_{i-1}$ while it
is still reading the old $P_{i+1}$. That detail is load-bearing: copying the array and updating every
point simultaneously diverges for any $b > 1/3$, so at the weights recommended here the "smoothed" path
blows up to infinity within a few dozen sweeps. In place, it converges for any $b < 1$.

It is a cheap alternative to fitting a spline — no overshoot, though it does pull slightly away from
your original waypoints.

### Following a spline at speed

The [splines lesson](./quintic-splines.mdx) gives you a smooth curve; to *follow* it, **resample it into a
list of points by arc length**, tagging each with its curvature radius $R = 1/\kappa$ (computed from the
spline's derivatives). The curvature speed cap from the adaptive plan above applies per point — the lateral
acceleration magnitude on a curve is $a_\text{lat} = v^2/|R|$, so the slip limit gives a per-point speed
$v \le \sqrt{a_\text{lat}^{\max}\, |R|}$: gentle (big $|R|$) ⇒ full speed, tight (small $|R|$) ⇒ slow down.
(FTCclueless uses a cheaper ramp to the same effect, `speed = 0.25 + 0.75·min(|R|, 200)/200`.)

One refinement a *curved* follow needs that a straight lookahead does not is a **centripetal feedforward**.
To *stay* on a curve of radius $R$ at speed $v$ the robot needs a sideways acceleration toward the centre;
without it, feedback alone always lags and the robot drifts to the outside of every turn. Add it as a strafe
feedforward:

$$
u_\text{strafe} \mathrel{+}= k_c\,\frac{v^2}{R}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'k_c', meaning: 'centripetal feedforward gain: how many seconds of centripetal acceleration to fold into the strafe velocity. It multiplies an acceleration (in/s\u00b2) and the result is added to a velocity (in/s), so it carries seconds', unit: 's'},
    {symbol: 'u_strafe', meaning: 'the sideways component of the chassis command, the one this term adds to'},
    {symbol: 'v\u00b2 / R', meaning: 'the centripetal acceleration a turn of radius R at speed v demands', unit: 'in/s\u00b2'},
  ]}
/>

Then follow the resampled list with PID-to-point's [point-advance follower](./pid-to-point.mdx) — drive
toward the current point, advance to the next within a radius — so the whole curve is tracked as one
continuous motion. The code is in the Deploy section below.

</Theory>

<Deploy title="The loop, and what production followers add">

One iteration on the Control Hub: locate the carrot ahead, transform it into the robot frame, convert
to curvature, and command forward speed plus turn rate.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Basic Pure Pursuit, one loop iteration">{`private Progress progress = new Progress(0, 0);   // persistent across loops

void start() {
    progress = new Progress(0, 0);
    timer.reset();
}

MoveStatus update(List<Vector2d> path, Pose2d endPose) {
    if (path.size() < 2 || !(LOOKAHEAD > 0)) throw new IllegalStateException();

    PoseSample sample = poseSource.getSample();   // once, at the loop top
    Pose2d robot = sample.pose();

    if (moveStatus(sample, endPose, timer.seconds()) == TIMED_OUT) {
        drive(0, 0, 0);
        return TIMED_OUT;              // the deadline applies in every phase
    }

    Lookahead look = findLookahead(path, robot.vec(), LOOKAHEAD, progress);
    progress = look.progress();

    Vector2d d = look.carrot().minus(robot.vec());   // into the robot frame
    double yLocal = -Math.sin(robot.heading()) * d.x()
            + Math.cos(robot.heading()) * d.y();
    double L = d.norm();

    if (progress.segment() == path.size() - 2
            && (progress.t() >= 1.0
                || (progress.t() > TERMINAL_T
                    && robot.vec().minus(endPose.vec()).norm() < HANDOFF))) {
        return terminalController.update(sample, endPose);
    }

    if (L < EPS) {
        drive(0, 0, 0);
        return RUNNING;                // zero chord: wait for a real target
    }

    double curvature = 2 * yLocal / (L * L);
    double v = CRUISE;                 // inches/second
    double omega = curvature * v;      // radians/second
    drive(v / MAX_SPEED, 0, omega / MAX_TURN);   // normalize at the mixer boundary
    return RUNNING;
}
`}</JavaCode>

The handoff is the only place `REACHED` can come from.

<Exercise title="Derive the curvature">

In the robot frame the arc from the robot to the carrot is a circle through the origin, tangent to the
$+x$ axis. Its radius satisfies $R = L^2 / (2 y_{local})$, and curvature is $1/R$. Draw the circle, drop
the perpendicular, and use the intersecting-chords relation.

<Solution>

The circle is tangent to $+x$ at the origin, so its centre lies on the $y$ axis at $(0, R)$. The carrot
lies on the circle, so $x^2 + (y - R)^2 = R^2$, which expands to $x^2 + y^2 = 2Ry$. The left side is
$L^2$ and the right is $2Ry_{local}$, so $R = L^2/(2y_{local})$ and
$\kappa = 2y_{local}/L^2$ — the line above. Look up "pure pursuit curvature derivation."

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why REACHED comes from only one place">

Work out what a follower that can report `REACHED` mid-path does to an autonomous routine that advances
on `REACHED`.

<Solution>

It advances early. The routine believes the path finished, starts the next action — scoring, or another
drive — while the robot is somewhere in the middle of the field, and every subsequent move compounds
the error. Funnelling `REACHED` through the terminal controller means the word can only be produced by
something that has actually checked the end pose, the heading, and the velocity.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Callout type="tip" title="What the production libraries do.">
Pure Pursuit is the foundation. Road Runner and Pedro Pathing follow full <em>trajectories</em> —
paths combined with a motion profile and timing — and layer feedforward plus PID on top. The
principle is identical: look ahead, curve toward the target, with tighter accuracy and explicit speed
control.
</Callout>

For the **adaptive** velocity plan, precompute it once over the dense path: curvature per point, a
curvature speed cap, then the backward braking pass.

<JavaCode label="Adaptive velocity profile (offline, over the dense path)">{`// aLatMax is the sideways acceleration the tires hold; aMax the forward
// acceleration and braking limit; startSpeed how fast the robot is now.
void planProfile(List<PathPoint> pts, double vPath, double aLatMax,
                 double aMax, double startSpeed) {
    for (int i = 1; i < pts.size() - 1; i++) {     // endpoints stay at zero
        pts.get(i).curvature =
                mengerCurvature(pts.get(i - 1), pts.get(i), pts.get(i + 1));
    }

    for (PathPoint p : pts) {                      // slower where it bends
        p.speed = Math.abs(p.curvature) < EPS
                ? vPath
                : Math.min(vPath, Math.sqrt(aLatMax / Math.abs(p.curvature)));
    }

    pts.get(0).speed = Math.min(pts.get(0).speed, startSpeed);
    for (int i = 1; i < pts.size(); i++) {         // cannot speed up past aMax
        double ds = pts.get(i).minus(pts.get(i - 1)).norm();
        double reachable = Math.sqrt(
                pts.get(i - 1).speed * pts.get(i - 1).speed + 2 * aMax * ds);
        pts.get(i).speed = Math.min(pts.get(i).speed, reachable);
    }

    pts.get(pts.size() - 1).speed = 0;
    for (int i = pts.size() - 2; i >= 0; i--) {    // must still brake to a stop
        double ds = pts.get(i + 1).minus(pts.get(i)).norm();
        double reachable = Math.sqrt(
                pts.get(i + 1).speed * pts.get(i + 1).speed + 2 * aMax * ds);
        pts.get(i).speed = Math.min(pts.get(i).speed, reachable);
    }
}

double mengerCurvature(Vector2d A, Vector2d B, Vector2d C) {
    double area = Math.abs(B.minus(A).cross(C.minus(A))) / 2;
    double denom = B.minus(C).norm() * A.minus(C).norm() * A.minus(B).norm();
    if (denom < EPS) return 0;         // the three points are collinear
    return 4 * area / denom;
}
`}</JavaCode>

Menger curvature is $1/R$ for the circle through three points, computed without ever constructing the
circle. At runtime, on top of the plan, rate-limit against the *measured* loop time:

<Exercise title="The corner cap, and the two passes">

The curvature cap is $a = v^2/r$ rearranged for $v$. Work out why a tighter corner must mean a lower
speed, and what physically happens when you exceed it. Then: both passes are the same kinematic
relation $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ run in opposite directions. Work out what the profile looks like with only
the *forward* pass, and say where the robot ends up.

<Solution>

Holding a curve of radius $r$ at speed $v$ requires a sideways acceleration $v^2/r$, and the only thing
supplying it is tire friction. Halve the radius and you need twice the acceleration at the same speed —
past the friction limit the tires break loose and the robot slides straight on, wide of the corner and
with its odometry now lying about where it is.

With only the forward pass, the plan says "accelerate to cruise and stay there" and nothing ever brings
the speed back down. The robot arrives at the last point at full speed and drives straight past it,
braking only once the terminal controller notices. The backward pass exists to make the *plan* end at
zero, so the deceleration is scheduled rather than reactive.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

To follow a **resampled spline**, advance through its points, cap speed by each point's curvature radius,
and add the centripetal feedforward:

<JavaCode label="Spline follower: resample by arc length">{`// Each SplinePoint holds x, y, heading, radius and speed.
List<SplinePoint> resample(Spline spline, double step) {
    if (!(step > 0)) throw new IllegalArgumentException("step");

    List<Double> distances = new ArrayList<>();
    for (double d = 0; d < spline.length(); d += step) distances.add(d);
    distances.add(spline.length());          // the exact endpoint, always

    List<SplinePoint> out = new ArrayList<>();
    double previousHeading = 0;
    for (double d : distances) {
        double t = spline.tAtDistance(d);
        double dx = spline.dx(t), dy = spline.dy(t);
        double ddx = spline.ddx(t), ddy = spline.ddy(t);
        double speedSq = dx * dx + dy * dy;

        double heading, curvature;
        if (speedSq > EPS) {
            heading = Math.atan2(dy, dx);
            curvature = (dx * ddy - dy * ddx) / Math.pow(speedSq, 1.5);
        } else {
            heading = previousHeading;   // a cusp; atan2(0,0) is meaningless
            curvature = 0;
        }
        previousHeading = heading;

        double radius = Math.abs(curvature) < EPS
                ? Double.POSITIVE_INFINITY
                : 1 / curvature;
        out.add(new SplinePoint(spline.x(t), spline.y(t), heading, radius, 0));
    }
    return out;
}
`}</JavaCode>

The spline parameter $t$ is not proportional to distance along the curve, which is the whole reason an
arc-length lookup has to exist. Look up "arc length parameterization."

    double speed = p.speed();          // the final planned point is exactly 0

    double ex = p.x() - robot.x(), ey = p.y() - robot.y();
    double c = Math.cos(robot.heading()), s = Math.sin(robot.heading());
    double dir = Math.atan2(ey * c - ex * s, ex * c + ey * s);

    double vx = speed * Math.cos(dir);
    double vy = speed * Math.sin(dir);
    vy += K_CENTRIPETAL * speed * speed / p.radius();   // lean into the corner

    // headingController already returns normalized power; vx and vy are in/s.
    drive(vx / MAX_SPEED, vy / MAX_SPEED, headingController(p.heading()));

<Exercise title="Why the exact endpoint is appended separately">

Take a spline of length 10.5 with `step = 1.0` and list the distances without that last line. Which
point is missing, and what does the follower do when it never arrives?

<Solution>

The distances run 0 through 10, so the final half-inch is never sampled and the last planned point sits
at 10.0 rather than 10.5. The follower advances to it, hands off to the terminal controller against a
target that is not the end of the spline, and the robot settles half an inch short of every path it
ever runs — a small, consistent bias that looks like a tuning problem and is not.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Derive the centripetal term">

A robot tracking a curve of radius $r$ at speed $v$ needs a sideways acceleration of $v^2/r$ just to
stay on it. Work out what the follower does without that feedforward, then confirm the units of
`K_CENTRIPETAL` work out to seconds.

<Solution>

Without it the follower is purely reactive: it only turns harder once it has already drifted to the
outside of the corner, so every bend is entered wide and corrected late, and the error grows with
$v^2$. The feedforward supplies the known part of the demand before the error appears.

For units: `speed^2 / radius` is $\text{in}^2/\text{s}^2 \div \text{in} = \text{in}/\text{s}^2$,
an acceleration, and it is being added to `vy`, which is $\text{in}/\text{s}$. So `K_CENTRIPETAL`
carries seconds — it is effectively how far ahead in time the feedforward is looking.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

The speed plan is computed entirely in inches per second and radians per second, and is converted to
motor units at exactly one boundary: the `drive(...)` call. That helper consumes **normalized power**,
so every physical speed is divided by `MAX_SPEED` or `MAX_TURN` on the way in — pass raw inches per
second and the desaturator inside `drive` compares them against `1.0` and pins all four wheels to full
power. Near the endpoint, the follower hands off to the shared terminal pose controller and distinguishes
`REACHED` from `TIMED_OUT`.

<Admonition type="tip" title="Why C² matters for the speed plan">

The curvature speed cap is only as smooth as $\kappa$ itself. A **C¹** path — a cubic Hermite or
Catmull-Rom spline that matches only *tangents* at each waypoint — has $\kappa$ jump at every join, so the
*speed limit* jumps too. A **C²** path matches position, first derivative, and second derivative; its
curvature is continuous only where the tangent remains nonzero (a regular curve). Under that regularity
condition, quintic and natural-cubic paths both support a continuous curvature plan.

</Admonition>

<Callout type="idea" title="On a tank (differential) drive">
Pure pursuit outputs a signed curvature and a speed; a differential drive turns those straight into
wheel speeds. With $+\kappa$ meaning a counter-clockwise (left) turn and track width $T$,
$\;L = v(2 - \kappa T)/2\;$ and $\;R = v(2 + \kappa T)/2$. (Our
mecanum stack instead feeds the same $(v,\ \omega = \kappa v)$ into the
[mixer](./mecanum-kinematics.mdx).) Use a track width a few inches larger than the real one to absorb
turning scrub.
</Callout>
