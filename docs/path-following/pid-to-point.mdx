# PID-to-Point

<Difficulty level="Medium" />

<Abstract>
PID-to-point drives the robot toward a target pose by running feedback controllers on the position and
heading error, expressed in the robot's own frame, and handing the result to the drivetrain mixer. It is
the smallest complete autonomous follower — here we also harden it with a kStatic nudge to beat friction
and **predictive deceleration** to brake on time, leaving only the "aim at one point" limit that splines
and pure pursuit remove.
</Abstract>

You now have the three pieces autonomous needs: a pose source verified against the
[Pose Estimation Contract](./pose-estimation-contract.mdx),
a [mecanum mixer](./mecanum-kinematics.mdx) that turns a chassis command
into wheel powers, and [angle wrapping](./angle-wrapping.mdx) so heading errors behave. PID-to-point is
the loop that connects them.

<Problem title="Get to a target pose, with nothing but feedback">

The task: drive from wherever the robot is to a target pose $(x^*, y^*, \theta^*)$ and stop there.
No precomputed path, no velocity plan — just look at how far off you are and push toward the goal each
loop.

The simple version "drive toward the point at full power until you're close" overshoots, then reverses,
then overshoots again, circling the target like a moth. We want it to *converge*: approach quickly when
far, ease in when close, and hold the final heading without spinning the long way around.

</Problem>

<Theory title="Feedback on the error, in the robot's frame">

Start with the **field-frame error**: how far the target is from the robot right now:

$$
e_x = x^* - x, \qquad e_y = y^* - y, \qquad e_\theta = \operatorname{wrap}(\theta^* - \theta)
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'x*, y*, \u03b8*', meaning: 'the TARGET pose. A star always marks the value you want, never the one you measured'},
    {symbol: 'e_x, e_y', meaning: 'position error in the field frame: how far the target sits from the robot along each field axis', unit: 'in'},
    {symbol: 'e_\u03b8', meaning: 'heading error, wrapped so the robot always turns the short way', unit: 'rad'},
  ]}
/>

The heading error is wrapped into $[-\pi, \pi)$ so the robot always turns the short way — exactly the
[angle-wrapping](./angle-wrapping.mdx) lesson. But the drivetrain does not take field commands; it takes
forward/strafe in the **robot frame**. So rotate the translational error by $-\theta$, the same rotation
as field-centric driving:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} e_x^\text{robot} \\ e_y^\text{robot} \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} e_x \\ e_y \end{bmatrix}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'e_x^robot', meaning: 'the same error expressed FORWARD of the robot rather than along the field axis'},
    {symbol: 'e_y^robot', meaning: 'the same error expressed to the robot\u2019s LEFT'},
    {symbol: '\u2212\u03b8', meaning: 'the rotation is by minus the heading. The robot sits at +\u03b8 in the field, so undoing that puts a field vector into its frame'},
  ]}
/>

<PidToPointIllustration />

That rotation is the whole trick: the same field error becomes a **forward** distance and a **strafe**
distance in the robot's own axes — the two numbers the drivetrain can act on. Now run an independent
controller on each axis. A proportional term is often enough for the translation, with the heading on its
own gain:

$$
v_x = k_p^{xy}\, e_x^\text{robot}, \qquad
v_y = k_p^{xy}\, e_y^\text{robot}, \qquad
\omega = k_p^{\theta}\, e_\theta
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'k_p^xy', meaning: 'one proportional gain shared by both translation axes, so forward and sideways respond identically'},
    {symbol: 'k_p^\u03b8', meaning: 'a SEPARATE gain for heading, because its error is in radians and its actuator authority differs'},
    {symbol: 'v_x, v_y, \u03c9', meaning: 'the chassis command handed to the mecanum mixer: forward, left, and counter-clockwise'},
  ]}
/>

If a plain proportional term leaves the robot drifting short of the goal or overshooting, promote each
axis to a full discrete PID — proportional on the error, integral on its running sum, derivative on its
change per loop:

$$
u_k = k_p\, e_k \;+\; k_i \sum_{j=0}^{k} e_j\,\Delta t \;+\; k_d\,\frac{e_k - e_{k-1}}{\Delta t}
$$

<EquationLegend
  title="Symbols in the discrete PID"
  items={[
    {symbol: 'u_k', meaning: 'the command this loop. Lowercase k gains here are the same PID gains written as k_P, k_I, k_D elsewhere'},
    {symbol: 'e_k, e_{k-1}', meaning: 'the error this loop and the error last loop'},
    {symbol: 'j', meaning: 'a summation counter running over every past loop from 0 to now. It is not a separate quantity'},
    {symbol: '\u03a3 e_j \u0394t', meaning: 'the integral, approximated as a stack of rectangles: each loop contributes its own error times its own measured \u0394t'},
    {symbol: '(e_k \u2212 e_{k-1}) / \u0394t', meaning: 'the derivative, approximated as the change in error across one loop'},
  ]}
/>

where $\Delta t$ is the measured loop time. In practice the derivative term damps the approach, while a
small, clamped integral defeats the static friction that strands a proportional-only controller just
short of the target.

### kStatic: getting over the last bit of friction

There is a cleaner fix for that "stranded just short" problem than an integral term. Near the goal the
error is tiny, so the $k_p\,e$ command shrinks below the voltage needed to overcome **static friction**
and the robot simply stops moving while still a centimetre off. A **static feedforward**: a constant
push in the *direction* of the error — defeats it directly:

$$
u_k = k_p\, e_k + k_d\,\dot e_k + \underbrace{k_S\,\operatorname{sign}(e_k)}_{\text{only when } |e_k| \text{ is small}}
$$

The method most FTC implementations use is to apply $k_S$ **only when the error is already small** (inside
a threshold), so it acts as a final nudge rather than a constant bias that would cause limit-cycling far
from the target. This is exactly what production drive code does — for example the SimplicityFTC
`PDFSController` adds `sign(error) * kStatic` once `|error| < threshold`, and FTCclueless's drivetrain
adds a flat `0.05 * sign(error)` near the goal. It is the same $k_S$ term you met in
[feedforward](../control-theory/feedforward.mdx), now applied to a positional drive.

Those three numbers — $v_x$, $v_y$, $\omega$ — are precisely the chassis command
$\mathbf{u} = (v_x, v_y, \omega)$ that the
[mecanum mixer](./mecanum-kinematics.mdx) expects — including its desaturation step, which keeps the
approach direction straight when the gains ask for more than full power. The full PID adds the usual
integral and derivative terms per axis, but on a well-localized drive the proportional + derivative pair
usually does the job; integral mainly fights steady-state friction near the goal.

<Callout type="idea" title="Three little loops, one drivetrain.">
PID-to-point is just three 1-D controllers — forward, strafe, heading — sharing one mixer. Tune them
roughly independently: heading first (so the frame is right), then translation.
</Callout>

### Braking on time: predictive deceleration

Proportional control has one stubborn flaw. The command $v = k_p\,e$ is large when far and small when
near — which sounds right, but the robot has a maximum deceleration. Far out, $k_p\,e$ is *way* above the
speed from which the robot could still stop in time, so it charges in, brakes too late, and sails past.
Lower $k_p$ to kill the overshoot and the whole approach turns sluggish. No single gain is both fast and
clean.

Ask a better question: *given the distance left, how fast am I allowed to go?* Constant-acceleration
kinematics answers it. To bring speed $v$ to zero over distance $d$ at deceleration $a$:

$$
v^2 = 2 a d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_\text{cap} = \sqrt{2 a d}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'd', meaning: 'the distance still to travel', unit: 'in'},
    {symbol: 'a', meaning: 'the braking deceleration, taken as a positive magnitude', unit: 'in/s\u00b2'},
    {symbol: 'v_cap', meaning: 'the fastest you may currently be going and still stop within d'},
  ]}
/>

That is the fastest speed from which you can still stop on the point. Make the commanded speed the
smallest of three limits — the braking cap, the robot's top speed, and the proportional term for a soft
final approach:

$$
v_\text{cmd} = \min\big(\sqrt{2 a d},\ v_\text{max},\ k_p\, d\big)
$$

<BrakingProfileIllustration />

Far out the square-root cap exceeds $v_\text{max}$, so the robot cruises; as it nears the point the cap
drops below $v_\text{max}$ and throttles it smoothly to zero at the target in the ideal model. Once the
cap is active, following it corresponds to a constant-magnitude deceleration; it is the maximum speed
compatible with stopping over the remaining distance, not a command whose braking grows near the end.

<Callout type="idea" title="This is motion profiling, lazily.">
A full motion profile precomputes position, velocity, and acceleration for every instant. The
$\sqrt{2 a d}$ cap is the same physics evaluated on demand from the current distance — cheaper to write,
and enough for point-to-point moves.
</Callout>

:::note[The constant-deceleration model is an idealization]
$v = \sqrt{2ad}$ assumes the robot brakes at one fixed rate. Measure a real drivetrain and its
stopping distance fits $D(v) = av^2 + bv$ instead — back-EMF braking fades as the robot slows, and
viscous drag adds the linear term. The idealization is the right way to *learn* the technique. When
you want the production version, [the Pedro Pathing deep-dive](../advanced-topics/how-pedro-pathing-works.mdx)
shows how Pedro fits the real curve and inverts it with the quadratic formula.
:::

**The slicker version: predict where you'll glide to.** The same physics solves for *distance* instead of
speed: a robot moving at speed $v$ needs $d_\text{glide} = v^2/2a$ to coast to a stop. So don't cap the
speed at all — add that glide vector to the current pose to get a **predicted stopping pose**, and run the
ordinary PID against *that*:

$$
\mathbf{p}_\text{predicted} = \mathbf{p}_\text{now} + \frac{\mathbf{v}\,\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert}{2 a}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'p_now', meaning: 'the measured pose right now, as a vector'},
    {symbol: 'v', meaning: 'measured field velocity. Filter this: raw velocity is noisy, and it is squared below'},
    {symbol: 'v \u2016v\u2016 / 2a', meaning: 'the glide vector. Its LENGTH is the braking distance \u2016v\u2016\u00b2/2a and its DIRECTION is the way the robot is travelling'},
    {symbol: 'p_predicted', meaning: 'where the robot would coast to a stop if you cut power now'},
  ]}
/>

While the robot races in, the predicted pose sits ahead of it; as it slows, the prediction pulls back onto
the target. Going too fast, the predicted pose overshoots the goal, the error flips sign, and the
controller brakes — automatically, with no separate cap. This is how Cyliis's `Odo` computes its
`predictedX/Y` (from low-pass-filtered velocity) and how FTCclueless feeds an `expected` pose into a
"predictive PID."

### Chaining points: follow a list without stopping

A real autonomous routine is a *list* of poses, not one. The simple approach — `goToPose` each one and
wait for it to settle — works, but the robot decelerates to a halt at every intermediate point, which is
slow and jerky. The fix is **point-advance sequencing**: keep an index into the list, drive toward the
current target, and **advance to the next point as soon as you are within an advance radius** of this one
(rather than fully settling). Only the *final* point uses the strict settle test.

$$
\text{if } \lVert \mathbf{p}_\text{target}^{(i)} - \mathbf{p}_\text{robot}\rVert < r_\text{advance}
\ \text{ and not last } \Rightarrow\ i \leftarrow i + 1
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'superscript (i)', meaning: 'which point in the target list, counting along the path'},
    {symbol: 'r_advance', meaning: 'the advance radius: get this close and move on. It is deliberately looser than the settle tolerance, so the robot never stops mid-path', unit: 'in'},
  ]}
/>

Because the robot switches targets while still moving, it flows through the intermediate points and only
brakes for the last — turning a list of stop-and-go moves into one continuous motion. This is exactly how
SimplicityFTC's `PidToPointFollower` (`pointAdvanceRadius`) and FTCclueless's drivetrain (`pathRadius`)
sequence their waypoints, and it is what turns a [spline](./quintic-splines.mdx) — once resampled into a
list of points — into something PID-to-point can follow.

### What comes next

kStatic and predictive deceleration fix how PID-to-point *arrives*; the remaining limit is that it always
aims straight at one distant target. To thread many waypoints along a smooth, drivable curve, you move on
to **[splines](./quintic-splines.mdx)** and **[pure pursuit](./pure-pursuit.mdx)**: following a path
instead of chasing a point.

</Theory>

### Core checkpoint

This is the required artifact: one pose sample becomes wrapped field error, robot-frame P commands, a
mecanum command, and an explicit `MoveStatus`. Length is inches, speed is inches per second, heading is
radians, and the mixer input is normalized power.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="PID-to-point (proportional core)">{`void startMove(Pose2d target) {
    activeTarget = target;
    timer.reset();               // never reset this inside updateMove()
}

MoveStatus updateMove() {
    if (activeTarget == null) return null;
    PoseSample sample = poseSource.getSample();
    MoveStatus status = moveStatus(sample, activeTarget, timer.seconds());

    if (status != RUNNING) {
        drive(0, 0, 0);          // REACHED and TIMED_OUT both stop
        return status;
    }

    commandToward(activeTarget, sample.pose());
    return RUNNING;
}

void commandToward(Pose2d target, Pose2d poseForError) {
    double ex = target.x() - poseForError.x();          // field frame
    double ey = target.y() - poseForError.y();
    double eh = wrap(target.heading() - poseForError.heading());

    double c = Math.cos(poseForError.heading());
    double s = Math.sin(poseForError.heading());
    double exBot =  ex * c + ey * s;                    // into the robot frame
    double eyBot = -ex * s + ey * c;

    double vx = kPxy * exBot + kStatic(exBot);
    double vy = kPxy * eyBot + kStatic(eyBot);
    double w = kPheading * eh;

    drive(vx, vy, w);                                   // the mixer desaturates
}
`}</JavaCode>

Note the signature of `commandToward`: the desired target and the pose that *supplies* the error are
separate arguments. They are the same thing here, and the predictive version below is exactly this
function with a different second argument — which is the whole reason it is written this way.

<Exercise title="Why rotate by minus the heading">

The robot sits at $+\text{heading}$ in the field, so expressing a field vector in the robot's frame
undoes that. Check it: the robot faces $+90°$ and the target is straight ahead of it, which in the field
is $+y$. What must `exBot` and `eyBot` come out to?

<Solution>

`exBot` must be the full distance and `eyBot` zero, because "straight ahead" in the robot frame is
$+x$. Substituting $\text{heading} = 90°$ gives $c = 0$, $s = 1$, so `ex_bot = ey` and
`ey_bot = -ex` — and with the target purely at $+y$ in the field, that is exactly the distance in
`exBot` and nothing in `eyBot`. Rotating by $+\text{heading}$ instead would send the robot
sideways, at right angles to the target, at full commanded speed.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

The settle test matters as much as the controller: check position **and** heading **and** that the robot
is actually slow, or an action will report "done" while the robot is still coasting through the point. Add
a **timeout** as a backstop so a stuck move — jammed on a wall, or a bad pose estimate — still yields the
field instead of hanging the routine. That trio (pose tolerance, low velocity, timeout) is exactly the
shared [`MoveStatus` outcome](./common-implementation.mdx) every follower in this module calls. A reached
move and a timed-out move both stop, but only the reached move is success.

To follow a *list* of poses continuously, wrap that loop in point-advance sequencing:

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Following a list of poses (point-advance)">{`void startPath(List<Pose2d> newPoints) {
    points = List.copyOf(newPoints);
    index = 0;
    timer.reset();
}

MoveStatus follow() {
    if (points.isEmpty()) {
        drive(0, 0, 0);
        return REACHED;                   // nothing to do is not a failure
    }

    PoseSample sample = poseSource.getSample();
    Pose2d robot = sample.pose();
    boolean last = index == points.size() - 1;

    if (!last && robot.vec().minus(points.get(index).vec()).norm()
            < ADVANCE_RADIUS) {
        index++;
        timer.reset();                    // each leg gets its own deadline
        last = index == points.size() - 1;
    }

    MoveStatus status = commandTowardAsInUpdateMove(points.get(index));

    if (status == TIMED_OUT) return TIMED_OUT;
    if (last) return status;              // only the last leg may say REACHED
    return RUNNING;
}
`}</JavaCode>

Advancing *early* through intermediate points is what keeps the robot from having to stop in the middle
of a path.

<Exercise title="Why ADVANCE_RADIUS is looser than POS_TOL">

Trace a robot that has to settle inside `POS_TOL` at every intermediate point, and describe how the
path looks and sounds.

<Solution>

`POS_TOL` also requires the robot to be *slow* — that is the velocity term in `moveStatus` — so
settling at every waypoint means decelerating to a stop, waiting to fall inside tolerance, then
accelerating again. The path becomes a series of lurches with a pause at each point, it takes several
times as long, and each stop-start is a current spike. `ADVANCE_RADIUS` says "close enough to start
aiming at the next one," which lets the corners round off and the robot flow through.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

For an overshoot-free approach, shape the command's *magnitude* by the braking cap before the mixer:

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Distance-capped approach speed">{`static final double MAX_SPEED = 40;   // inches/second
static final double MAX_DECEL = 60;   // inches/second^2, positive magnitude

double brakingCap(double distance) {
    double vStop = Math.sqrt(2 * MAX_DECEL * distance);
    double vProportional = kPxy * distance * MAX_SPEED;
    return Math.min(Math.min(vStop, MAX_SPEED), vProportional) / MAX_SPEED;
}

void driveCapped(double exBot, double eyBot, double w) {
    double dist = Math.hypot(exBot, eyBot);
    double cap = brakingCap(dist);
    double dir = Math.atan2(eyBot, exBot);
    drive(cap * Math.cos(dir), cap * Math.sin(dir), w);
}
`}</JavaCode>

<Exercise title="Derive the braking cap">

`vStop` is $v^2 = 2as$ solved for $v$. Derive it: a robot at speed $v$ braking at `MAX_DECEL` needs
$v^2/(2a)$ inches to stop. Invert that into "the fastest I may be going, given I am this far out."

Then work out why the direction is computed once and the *speed* is capped, rather than capping
`exBot` and `eyBot` separately. Take `ex_bot = 30`, `ey_bot = 40`, and clamp each to 20.

<Solution>

Setting the stopping distance equal to the distance remaining gives $v^2/(2a) = s$, so
$v = \sqrt{2as}$ — the speed from which the robot can still stop exactly on the target.

For the second half: $(30, 40)$ points at 53.1° off the robot's nose. Clamped componentwise to
$(20, 20)$ it points at 45°. The robot drives somewhere it was never asked to go, and the error grows
with how lopsided the two components are. Capping the magnitude and rebuilding from `dir` leaves the
direction untouched — the same reason the mecanum mixer desaturates all four wheels together.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

### Production extension: predictive stopping

The predicted-pose variant changes the **robot state used to calculate error**, never the desired target:

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Glide-distance predicted pose">{`// Where this sample says the robot will coast to a stop.
Pose2d predictedPose(PoseSample sample) {
    Pose2d p = sample.pose();
    Vector2d v = sample.fieldVelocity();   // filter this; raw velocity is noisy
    double speed = v.norm();
    if (speed < EPS) return p;             // standing still: no glide

    double glide = speed * speed / (2 * MAX_DECEL);   // the braking distance
    return new Pose2d(
            p.x() + (v.x() / speed) * glide,
            p.y() + (v.y() / speed) * glide,
            p.heading());                  // only translation is predicted
}

MoveStatus updatePredictiveMove() {
    if (activeTarget == null) return null;
    PoseSample sample = poseSource.getSample();
    MoveStatus status = moveStatus(sample, activeTarget, timer.seconds());
    if (status != RUNNING) {
        drive(0, 0, 0);
        return status;
    }

    commandToward(activeTarget, predictedPose(sample));
    return RUNNING;
}
`}</JavaCode>

Read that last call carefully: the *target* is unchanged, and only the pose supplying the error is
predicted. Worked example — target $x = 10$, actual robot $x = 2$, predicted $x = 8$. The error becomes
$+2$ rather than $+8$, so the robot eases off early instead of charging the target and overshooting.

<Exercise title="The unit vector, and a noisy velocity">

Work out why `v` is divided by `speed`, and what the prediction does if you forget. Then work out what
this controller does when `fieldVelocity()` is noisy and briefly reads twice its true value.

<Solution>

`v / speed` is the unit vector along travel, so multiplying by `glide` moves exactly `glide` inches in
the direction of motion. Skip the division and you move `speed * glide` inches — a prediction that
scales with the *cube* of speed and puts the robot's imagined position across the field.

A doubled velocity reading quadruples `glide`, since it goes as $v^2$. The predicted pose leaps past
the target, the error changes sign, and the robot brakes hard or reverses for one loop. That is why the
filter note is not optional: the prediction amplifies velocity noise quadratically, and velocity is the
noisiest thing the pose source produces.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

</Deploy>

<Admonition type="warning" title="Hardware reality: integral windup and the dead band">

Static friction means a small position error produces a command too weak to move the robot, so it sits
just short of the target. An integral term cures that — but if you keep integrating while saturated or
stuck against a wall, the term winds up huge and the robot lurches when it frees. Clamp the integral and
only accumulate it near the goal.

</Admonition>

<Admonition type="tip" title="Tune heading first">

A wrong heading rotates your whole translational command, so a heading bug looks like a translation bug.
Get $k_p^{\theta}$ holding heading cleanly, then tune the translational gain.

</Admonition>

<Admonition type="warning" title="Hardware reality: your real decel is smaller than you think">

`MAX_DECEL` is limited by traction, battery voltage, and weight transfer, not the motors. Measure it,
then choose and validate a conservative margin on the actual surface so changing traction does not turn
your computed stop into a slide. **Filter the velocity** before computing glide — it is squared, so encoder noise gets
amplified into a jittery predicted pose (Cyliis low-pass-filters the pod velocity first).

</Admonition>
