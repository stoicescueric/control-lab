# Guided Vector Fields

<Difficulty level="Hard" />

<Abstract>
A guided vector field (GVF) assigns, to **every** point of the field, a desired direction of travel that
both pulls the robot **onto** the path and pushes it **along** the path. In a regular neighborhood where
the closest point is unique, the ideal field has a clean convergence argument. We build it two ways (the
closest-point form you'll code, and the implicit level-set form used in the proof), add curvature and
endpoint speed caps, and ship a working FTC follower.
</Abstract>

<Admonition type="note" title="Prerequisites and a starting path">

Begin with [Pure Pursuit](./pure-pursuit.mdx) and [Splines](./quintic-splines.mdx), then confirm that
your localization meets the [Pose Estimation Contract](./pose-estimation-contract.mdx). The closest-point
implementation is the starting point; implicit curves, gradients, and convergence proofs are optional
mathematical depth.

**Math used:** vectors, normalization, dot products, and closest-point projection. The proof later adds
an implicit path function, a **gradient**, and a local-convergence argument; those are optional for the
deployed closest-point follower.

</Admonition>

[Pure pursuit](./pure-pursuit.mdx) follows a path by chasing a carrot a fixed distance ahead. It works, but
it tracks the *carrot*, not the path, so it cuts corners and its behavior swings with the lookahead
distance. Guided vector fields reframe following entirely: instead of a point to chase, build a field of
arrows the robot flows along. Drag the robot around the demonstrated path and watch the sampled flow line
bend onto it. This picture illustrates recovery on both sides; the convergence claim is local to the
unique-projection neighborhood stated below.

<Problem>

## Chasing a point is not the same as being on the path

A lookahead follower only ever aims at a point ahead of itself. On a curve, the straight line to that point
lies *inside* the curve, so the robot drifts to the inside and cuts the corner. Shorten the lookahead to hug
the path and the robot oscillates; lengthen it to smooth the oscillation and it cuts more. You are trading
one error for another, because the controller never has "distance from the path" as a quantity it directly
drives to zero.

A GVF fixes exactly that: the cross-track error becomes the thing the field cancels. Knock the robot
sideways mid-run and it simply re-enters the flow and slides back on — *"you could run up and kick the robot
during auto, and it will move back to the programmed path automagically"* (the THOR library's own
description of the behavior).

</Problem>

<Theory>

## Two desires, blended into one direction

At the robot's current position $\mathbf{p}$, find the **closest point** $\mathbf{c}$ on the path. That
gives two local facts: the path's unit **tangent** $\hat{\mathbf{t}}$ (the along-track direction) and the
signed **cross-track error**: how far, and which side, the robot is off the path — measured along the unit
**normal** $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ (the tangent rotated 90°):

$$
e = (\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{c}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}
$$

    {symbol: 'e', meaning: 'cross-track error. Dotting with n\u0302 makes it SIGNED, so its sign says which side of the path the robot is on', unit: 'in'},

Projection is the geometric step the controller depends on. Drag the query point and first make sure the
highlighted closest point, tangent, and signed normal behave as you expect:

For a regular path (nonzero tangent), that closest point is unique only inside a local **tubular
neighborhood**. A self-approaching spline or a robot far from the path can have multiple equally plausible
projections, so production code constrains the search around its previous progress.

A GVF combines two urges into one desired direction of travel:

$$
\boldsymbol{\chi} \;=\; \underbrace{\hat{\mathbf{t}}}_{\text{flow along}} \;-\; \underbrace{k_N\,e\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}}_{\text{converge onto}}
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: '\u03c7 (chi)', meaning: 'the guiding vector field: the direction the robot should travel right now. It is not a speed; the next equation supplies that'},
    {symbol: 't\u0302', meaning: 'the unit tangent, pointing along the path. On its own it would make the robot fly parallel to the path without ever joining it'},
    {symbol: 'k_N', meaning: 'the CONVERGE gain: how hard cross-track error bends \u03c7 back toward the path. Large k_N approaches steeply, small k_N approaches gently', unit: '1/in'},
  ]}
/>

<GuidedVectorFieldIllustration />

When the robot is on the path ($e = 0$) the field is pure tangent — it flows along. When it drifts off, the
second term turns the arrows back toward the path, harder the farther off it is. The gain $k_N$ (units of
$1/\text{length}$) sets how aggressively the field reels the robot in: too small and it converges lazily; too
large and the field points nearly straight at the path, so the robot overshoots and zig-zags across it.
That trade-off is the one setting worth tuning — slide $k_N$ in the demo and watch the flow line go from a long
graceful merge to a tight, slightly overshooting snap.

### Provable convergence (in brief)

This is GVF's headline property, with important conditions. Inside a regular tubular neighborhood where
projection is smooth and unique, treat the cross-track error as an energy $V = \tfrac12 e^2$: the ideal
converging term makes it nonincreasing ($\dot V \propto -k_N e^2 \le 0$). This is a **local** result for the
modeled field, not a global promise for an arbitrary spline, projection search, or saturated drivetrain.
The full Lyapunov proof, and the cleaner **implicit / level-set** formulation
$\boldsymbol{\chi} = E\nabla\varphi - k\varphi\nabla\varphi$ it is usually proved with, are in
Kapitanyuk, Proskurnikov and Cao's [*A Guiding Vector Field Algorithm for Path Following
Control*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.04391). Normalize
$\boldsymbol{\chi}$ and scale by your commanded speed to get the chassis velocity:

$$
\mathbf{u}_{xy} = v\,\frac{\boldsymbol{\chi}}{\lVert \boldsymbol{\chi}\rVert}, \qquad
v = k_S\,v_{\max}\,s_{\mathrm{end}}(t)
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'u_xy', meaning: 'the translational chassis command. \u03c7 divided by its own length is direction only, so v alone sets the speed'},
    {symbol: 'k_S', meaning: 'speed scale, a fraction from 0 to 1 of the mechanism\u2019s maximum'},
    {symbol: 's_end(t)', meaning: 'a slowdown factor that eases from 1 to 0 over the last stretch of the path, so the follower arrives rather than crashes'},
  ]}
/>

Here $s_{\mathrm{end}}(t)$ smoothly falls from 1 to 0 near $t=1$. At the endpoint, hand control to the
shared terminal pose controller and report `REACHED` or `TIMED_OUT`; do not force a minimum through-speed
past the end of the path.

### Slow down for the bends: the curvature speed cap

Convergence says *where* to go, not *how fast*. Just like [adaptive pure pursuit](./pure-pursuit.mdx), real
GVF followers cap speed where the path bends so the robot can physically make the turn. With path curvature
$\kappa = 1/R$ and a lateral-acceleration budget $a_{\max}$, the fastest feasible speed through a bend is
$v \le \sqrt{a_{\max}/|\kappa|}$. KookyBotz's follower writes this as a tunable cap

$$
v_{\max} \;=\; \min\!\left(v_{\max},\;\sqrt{\frac{a_{\max}}{k_C\,|\kappa|}}\right)
$$

<EquationLegend
  items={[
    {symbol: 'k_C', meaning: 'the CURVATURE CAUTION gain, distinct from k_N above. It inflates the apparent curvature so the cap bites earlier; larger k_C means slower through every bend'},
    {symbol: 'a_max', meaning: 'the lateral-acceleration budget: how much sideways grip the tires have before sliding', unit: 'in/s\u00b2'},
    {symbol: '\u03ba', meaning: 'the path\u2019s curvature at the current point. Its absolute value is used, because a left and a right bend cost the same'},
  ]}
/>

where $k_C$ trades cornering speed against grip. On a straight ($\kappa\to 0$) the cap lifts and you run at
full $v_{\max}$; in a tight arc it clamps the speed down automatically.

</Theory>

<Deploy>

## Sampling the field each loop

Three pieces every loop: **project** the robot onto the path (closest point + tangent + curvature), **blend**
the field and cap the speed, then **rotate** the field-frame velocity into wheel commands.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Gains and path lifecycle">{`// kN  converge gain, 1/inch: how hard the field pulls back to the path
// kS  speed scale, 0..1;  kC  curvature caution: bigger is slower in bends
// kStatic  stiction feedforward on the strafe axis

void startPath(Path nextPath) {
    path = nextPath;
    lastT = 0;
    timer.reset();
}

Vector2d unitTangent(double t) {
    Vector2d raw = path.tangent(t);
    if (!raw.isFinite() || raw.norm() < EPS) {
        drive(0, 0, 0);
        throw new IllegalStateException("cusp: no forward direction at " + t);
    }
    return raw.times(1 / raw.norm());
}
`}</JavaCode>

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Guided vector field velocity">{`// Returns the desired FIELD-frame chassis velocity.
Vector2d gvfVelocity(Pose2d robot) {
    double prior = lastT;
    double candidate = path.project(robot.vec(), prior);
    double t = clamp(candidate, prior - MAX_BACKTRACK, prior + MAX_FORWARD);
    lastT = t;

    Vector2d c = path.get(t);                     // the closest point
    Vector2d tangent = unitTangent(t);            // along-track direction
    Vector2d normal = tangent.rotate(Math.PI / 2);

    Vector2d offset = robot.vec().minus(c);
    double e = offset.dot(normal);          // + means on the +normal side

    Vector2d chi = tangent.minus(normal.times(kN * e)).unit();

    double k = Math.abs(path.curvature(t));
    double vmax = MAX_LINEAR_SPEED;
    if (k > EPS) vmax = Math.min(vmax, Math.sqrt(MAX_ACCEL / (kC * k)));

    double endScale = clamp((1 - t) / END_SLOWDOWN, 0, 1);
    return chi.times(vmax * kS * endScale);
}
`}</JavaCode>

A zero tangent means the path has a cusp or a duplicated point: there is no "forward" there, so the
follower stops rather than normalizing a zero vector and picking a direction at random.

<Exercise title="Why the projection is seeded and clamped">

Seeding from last loop's $t$ and then clamping to a window is what keeps the projection *local*. Work
out what an unseeded global search does on a path that doubles back near itself — which of the two
nearby points does it pick, and what does the robot then do?

<Solution>

Whichever is closer by a hair, which can be the *later* branch even though the robot has not driven the
loop yet. The follower jumps its parameter forward, decides it is nearly finished, and cuts across the
loop it was supposed to drive. Worse, a wobble of a few millimetres can flip the answer back and forth
between the two branches on consecutive loops, so the commanded direction oscillates. The window makes
progress physically continuous: $t$ can only move as far in one loop as the robot could actually travel.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="The one line that is the whole field">

Work through the two extremes of `chi`. With $e = 0$ the robot is on the path — what is `chi`? With
large $e$, which term dominates and where does `chi` point? Then work out what happens as `kN` grows
without bound, and compare the approach angle for `kN = 0.1` against `kN = 5`.

<Solution>

At $e = 0$ the normal term vanishes and `chi` is exactly the tangent: pure flow along the path. For
large $e$ the normal term dominates and `chi` points almost straight at the path, so the robot attacks
it perpendicularly. `kN` sets how sharply it trades one for the other.

As `kN` grows the approach angle goes to 90° for any nonzero error, so the robot dives at the path,
overshoots because it arrives with all its speed across the path rather than along it, and oscillates
about the line. At `kN = 0.1` a one-inch error produces a lean of about 6° off tangent — a gentle,
slow convergence. At `kN = 5` the same inch produces roughly 79°, which is nearly a right-angle attack.
Look up "vector field guidance."

</Solution>

</Exercise>

Progress is driven by geometry, not a clock. The snippet both seeds from last loop's $t$ and clamps the
accepted result to a small backward/forward window. Choose those bounds from maximum motion per loop; they
keep an ambiguous curve branch from silently teleporting progress.

Heading is its own controller, and a nice touch from KookyBotz is to profile it: instead of a raw P term on
heading error, command $\omega = \min\!\big(\omega_{\max}, \sqrt{2\,a_\theta\,|\Delta\theta|}\big)$, the
speed a constant-deceleration stop would need — it eases into the target heading without overshoot. A small
**kStatic** feedforward, $\operatorname{sign}(u)\cdot k_{\text{static}}$, breaks stiction so tiny corrections
near the path don't stall.

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Lifecycle-aware GVF update and terminal handoff">{`MoveStatus update(PoseSample sample) {
    Pose2d robot = sample.pose();
    Vector2d v = gvfVelocity(robot);          // this also advances lastT

    MoveStatus completion = moveStatus(sample, path.endPose(), timer.seconds());
    if (completion == TIMED_OUT
            || (completion == REACHED && lastT >= 1 - TERMINAL_TOL)) {
        drive(0, 0, 0);
        return completion;
    }

    if (lastT >= 1 - TERMINAL_HANDOFF) {
        return terminalController.update(sample, path.endPose());
    }

    Vector2d body = v.rotate(-robot.heading());
    double fwd = body.x() / MAX_LINEAR_SPEED;
    double str = body.y() / MAX_LINEAR_SPEED;

    Vector2d tangent = unitTangent(lastT);
    double targetHeading = Math.atan2(tangent.y(), tangent.x());
    double dTheta = wrap(targetHeading - robot.heading());
    double turn = Math.min(MAX_TURN, Math.sqrt(2 * A_THETA * Math.abs(dTheta)))
            * Math.signum(dTheta) / MAX_TURN;

    // Strafe is the axis mecanum wheels fight hardest.
    str += Math.signum(str) * kStatic;

    drive(fwd, str, turn);
    return RUNNING;
}
`}</JavaCode>

The GVF speed goes to zero at $t = 1$, so it can approach the end but never settle on it. That is what
the handoff to a point controller is for.

<Exercise title="Read the terminal condition closely">

A timeout is terminal anywhere, but `REACHED` is only honoured once the path parameter says the robot is
actually at the end. Work out the failure that prevents.

<Solution>

A path that loops back near its own start would otherwise report `REACHED` on loop one: the robot is
within `POS_TOL` of the end pose and not yet moving, so every condition in `moveStatus` is satisfied
before the robot has driven anywhere. Requiring `last_t >= 1 - TERMINAL_TOL` says the robot must have
travelled the path, not merely be standing near where it finishes.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Why the turn uses a square root">

That `sqrt` is $v^2 = 2as$ again, applied to the rotational axis: the fastest turn rate that can still
stop on the target heading. Work out what a plain proportional turn does differently on a large heading
error.

<Solution>

A proportional turn commands rate in *linear* proportion to the error, so on a large error it saturates
and then decelerates far too late — the robot arrives at the target heading still turning fast and
overshoots. The square-root profile is the braking curve: at every heading error it commands exactly
the rate from which it can still decelerate to zero at `A_THETA`. Large errors get a much lower
commanded rate than proportional would give, which is the point.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

[angle wrapping](./angle-wrapping.mdx) on the heading error so it always turns the short way. The
`Vector2d`/`Pose2d` types and the `MoveStatus` completion outcome come from

### Tune by symptom

| What you see | Likely cause | First change |
|---|---|---|
| Long, lazy merge onto the path | $k_N$ too small | raise $k_N$ slightly |
| Zig-zag across the path | $k_N$ or speed too high | lower $k_N$, then lower speed |
| Closest point jumps to another branch | projection is nonunique/search is too broad | constrain the search around prior $t$ |
| Direction becomes zero or undefined | degenerate/near-zero path tangent | repair or reject that path segment |
| Robot sails through the endpoint | no terminal slowdown/handoff | taper $s_{\mathrm{end}}$ and use `MoveStatus` |

<Admonition type="warning" title="Hardware reality: projection and path regularity">

The whole method rests on the projection. Finding the closest point on a spline every loop is a small
optimization — **seed it from last loop's parameter** and take a few local iterations, or the cost adds up
and (worse) it can jump to a far branch of a self-approaching path. In the closest-point field the unit
tangent and normal terms are orthogonal, so they do not cancel each other. A zero direction instead signals
a degenerate tangent; discontinuities usually signal a nonunique or jumping projection. Reject those path
segments rather than hiding them with a minimum speed. And don't let $k_N$ run away: a huge gain makes the
field point almost straight at the path, so the robot overshoots and zig-zags.

</Admonition>

<Admonition type="tip" title="Where GVF sits in the toolbox">

For point-to-point moves, [PID-to-point](./pid-to-point.mdx) is plenty. For pre-planned curves at speed, GVF
(or a well-tuned pure pursuit over a [quintic spline](./quintic-splines.mdx)) gives the tightest, most
repeatable tracking — and GVF's disturbance rejection (get bumped, slide right back on) is its standout
trait. The difference between a routine that scores and one that clips the structure.

</Admonition>
