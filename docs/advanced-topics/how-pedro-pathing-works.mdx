# How Does Pedro Pathing Work?

<Difficulty level="Hard" />

<Abstract>
Pedro Pathing reacts to where the robot actually is. Every loop it finds the robot's place on the path
and adds up a few correction vectors into one drive command. This lesson explains those pieces using
Pedro's documentation and its developers' paper: finding the closest point, tracking progress, pulling
back onto the path, leaning into curves, controlling heading, and deciding when it's done.
</Abstract>

## Four jobs, and what goes wrong without each

This lesson explains how the follower is put together. It isn't Pedro's source code. Each loop does four
jobs and adds the results together before the mecanum mixer:

| Component | Physical job | FTC symptom if wrong or missing |
|---|---|---|
| Tangential drive | move forward along the curve | robot corrects but crawls or stalls |
| Attractive correction | pull and damp motion back toward the curve | robot stays displaced or snakes after a bump |
| Centripetal feedforward | anticipate inward acceleration on bends | robot runs wide only on fast curves |
| Heading correction | track orientation independently | position is right but the robot faces wrong |

| Symptom on the robot | Check first in the official workflow |
|---|---|
| Pose axes/scale drift before following | [localization setup](https://pedropathing.com/docs/pathing/tuning/localization) |
| Straight paths oscillate or recover lazily | translational follower tuning |
| Fast curves run wide but slow curves work | centripetal correction and velocity constraints |
| Heading oscillates while translation is clean | heading controller tuning |
| The robot reaches the end but stays busy | completion constraints and terminal velocity |

Work through Pedro's own [localization](https://pedropathing.com/docs/pathing/tuning/localization) and
[tuning](https://pedropathing.com/docs/pathing/tuning) guides before changing anything inside it. If you
can name the four jobs and pick which tuner to try from a symptom, that's enough to use Pedro well. The
rest of the lesson goes into the math.

## The FTC problem Pedro is solving

Pure pursuit chases a point ahead of the robot. When the robot gets bumped, it's torn between catching
that point and getting back onto the path. Pedro aims to follow Bézier curves quickly and consistently
and to recover from bumps. Each loop:

1. The path is not just a list of waypoints. It is a smooth curve.
2. The robot estimates its current pose from localization.
3. The follower projects the robot onto the curve to find the closest or most relevant path parameter.
4. It computes correction vectors around that point.
5. It converts the desired field motion into mecanum drive power.

That's what makes Pedro reactive. It isn't replaying a plan timed in advance. Every loop, it starts from
where the robot actually is.

## A path becomes a field of arrows

Each arrow shows which way a robot at that spot should move. Near the path, the arrows point along it.
Farther away, they lean back toward it.

<GuidedVectorField />

The Pedro paper goes a step further. Its field depends on how fast the robot is already moving, not
just where it is, so it can decide whether to brake, lean into a curve, or correct heading.

## The pieces in more detail

### The path is a curve, not a waypoint list

Pedro's paths are smooth curves, usually Bézier curves. Any such path can be written as a point that
depends on a parameter $t$:

$$
\phi(t) =
\begin{bmatrix}
x(t) \\
y(t)
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad 0 \le t \le 1
$$

$t$ runs from 0 at the start to 1 at the end. It isn't time or distance; $t = 0.5$ isn't necessarily
halfway along. The derivative $\phi'(t)$ points the way the path is going, and dividing by its length
gives a length-1 direction, the tangent:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{T}}(t) = \frac{\phi'(t)}{\|\phi'(t)\|}
$$

Because the path is a formula instead of a list of points, you can ask for its direction, curvature,
remaining length, and target heading anywhere along it.

### Projection: which point of the curve the robot belongs to

With the robot at position $\mathbf{p}$, the follower needs the $t$ whose path point is closest. The paper
writes that as making this as small as possible:

$$
D(t) = \frac{1}{2}\|\phi(t) - \mathbf{p}\|^2
$$

That's half the squared distance. Squaring avoids a square root, the half makes the derivative neater,
and neither changes which $t$ is closest. The closest point is either at an end of the path or where the
derivative is zero:

$$
D'(t) = \left(\phi(t) - \mathbf{p}\right) \cdot \phi'(t) = 0
$$

A dot product of zero means perpendicular: at the closest point in the middle of the path, the line from
the path to the robot meets the path at a right angle. That condition also holds at the *farthest*
points, so the follower compares all the candidates and the two ends and keeps the closest. It can start
its search from last loop's answer, since the robot can't jump to a different part of the path between
loops.

Drag the robot below. The green point is the path point the follower thinks is closest, written
$\phi(\psi(\mathbf{p}))$, where $\psi(\mathbf{p})$ is the closest $t$. The yellow arrow is the tangent,
used for forward motion, and the blue arrow is the normal, used for correction.

<Callout type="idea" title="Everything starts from the closest point">
Once the follower knows which point on the path the robot is nearest, every other term comes from that
spot: the tangent, the normal, the curvature, the target heading, and the distance left.
</Callout>

### Cross-track error and its damped spring

Define the positional error used in the paper:

$$
\mathbf{e}_p = \phi(\psi(\mathbf{p})) - \mathbf{p}
$$

This vector points from the robot to its closest point on the path. The pull-back term acts like a
spring toward the path, with damping. Simplified:

$$
\mathbf{a}_{\text{attract}} \approx k_p^t\,\mathbf{e}_p - k_d^t\,\mathbf{v}_{\perp}
$$

$k_p^t$ and $k_d^t$ are the translation gains (the superscript $t$ is for translation; heading gets its
own pair later). $\mathbf{v}_{\perp}$ is the part of the robot's velocity sideways to the path, so the
damping term resists drifting away and also keeps the robot from swinging back too hard. The paper's
exact version handles more edge cases, but the idea is the same: if the robot is off the path, push it
back, and if it's already heading back fast, ease off.

### Leaning into a bend before the robot drifts

On a curve, the robot needs acceleration toward the inside even when it's exactly on the path. Without
it, the robot runs wide and only corrects after it's already off. For curvature $\kappa$ at speed $v$,
the inward acceleration needed is

$$
a_c = v^2 \kappa
$$

At 40 in/s around a 20 in radius, that's $1600 / 20 = 80$ in/s². Drag the points below: a tighter curve
has a smaller radius, a larger $\kappa$, and needs more inward acceleration at the same speed. (The demo
fits a circle through three separate points, so it matches the path's curvature at one spot best when
the points are close together.)

<CurvatureCircle />

The paper writes the same thing in terms of the path's formula:

$$
\xi_C(x) =
k_c
\left(
\operatorname{comp}_{\hat{\mathbf{T}}}( \mathbf{v} )
\right)^2
\frac{\hat{\mathbf{T}}'(\psi(\mathbf{p}))}{\|\phi'(\psi(\mathbf{p}))\|}
$$

$\xi_C$ is the paper's name for the centripetal part of its field, and $k_c$ is a tuning gain. The
squared term is the robot's speed along the path, the $v^2$ from above. The fraction is how fast the
path's direction turns per inch traveled, which is the curvature. So it's $k_c v^2 \kappa$, pointed
toward the inside of the curve: when the path bends and the robot is moving fast, it leans in before it
drifts out.

### Tangential speed from a measured braking model

The follower also decides how hard to push along the path, based on how much path is left. The paper
measures that by adding up the path's length from the robot's point to the end:

$$
d =
\int_{\psi(\mathbf{p})}^{1}
\|\phi'(s)\|\,ds
$$

(The integral adds up small pieces of length, because equal steps in the parameter aren't equal
distances.) The paper then uses $v = \sqrt{2ad}$, the constant-braking formula from
[PID-to-point](../path-following/pid-to-point.mdx). The paper keeps it general on purpose, since it's
meant for any kind of robot. The follower Pedro actually shipped goes further and uses how the robot
really stops.

Real drivetrains don't brake at a constant rate. Motor braking changes with speed, and friction adds
more drag at higher speeds. Measure stopping distance against starting speed on a real robot and it
fits a curve with a squared term and a straight term:

$$
D(v) = a v^2 + b v
$$

$D(v)$ is the distance to stop from speed $v$. The $a v^2$ part is the constant-braking piece; with
constant deceleration $a_\text{decel}$ you'd have $a = 1/(2a_\text{decel})$. The $b v$ part covers the
losses that grow with speed. On Pedro's test data $b$ was in the hundredths. It's small, but without it
the model gets short stops wrong. The braking data came from Pedro Pathing and was collected by Jacob
Ophoven.

There are two ways to use it:

- **Pedro 2.1.x** (starting with v2.1.0) runs a position controller and subtracts the stopping distance
  from the error: $e = \lVert \mathbf{x}_{\text{target}} - \mathbf{x} \rVert - D(v)$. When the distance
  left equals the stopping distance, the error is zero and the controller stops pushing.
- **Solving for speed**, which the paper describes as future work. Given the distance left, $d$, solve
  $a v^2 + b v = d$ with the quadratic formula and keep the positive answer:

$$
v_{\text{desired}} = \frac{-b + \sqrt{b^2 + 4ad}}{2a}
$$

  and track that speed with a velocity controller. It's the same fit, used as a speed target instead of
  an error offset. As of v2.1.2 the released library used the error-offset version.

:::warning[The numbers depend on how you brake]
Pedro brakes actively, at about $-0.2$ motor power. If you just let go and coast at zero power, the
robot goes farther. The same $D(v) = av^2 + bv$ shape fits both, but the $a$ and $b$ values are different,
so measure them for the way you actually brake.
:::

<Admonition type="tip" title="Why this differs from a timed motion profile">

A timed trajectory decides speed from where the plan says you should be at this moment. Pedro decides it
from where the robot actually is. If the robot gets bumped or slips, the distance left is still
correct.

</Admonition>

### Heading runs on its own controller

FTC autos often move one way while facing another: intaking while strafing, or approaching with a
camera pointed a certain way. The paper gives the target heading as its own smooth function along the
path, $\theta(t)$. (In the paper's general version it's a rotation matrix; on an FTC field it's just an
angle.) The heading correction is the usual heading loop:

$$
\alpha_{\text{cmd}}
=
k_p^r\,\operatorname{wrap}(\theta(\psi(\mathbf{p})) - \theta_{\text{robot}})
- k_d^r\,\omega
$$

$k_p^r$ and $k_d^r$ are the heading gains, $\omega$ is the measured turn rate, and the target heading is
looked up at the robot's closest point on the path. Translation and heading are handled separately, so
the robot can follow the curve while facing whichever way the path asks.

That fills in all four jobs from the table at the top. The combined command then goes through the
[mecanum mixer](../path-following/mecanum-kinematics.mdx).

## Implementation shape

Pedro has its own classes and tuners. A follower built the same way would look something like this:

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Pedro-style follower decomposition">{`void startPath() {
    lastT = 0;
    timer.reset();
}

MoveStatus update(PoseSample sample) {
    Pose2d pose = sample.pose();
    Vector2d velocity = sample.fieldVelocity();

    lastT = path.project(pose.vec(), lastT);     // seeded from last loop

    MoveStatus completion = moveStatus(sample, path.endPose(), timer.seconds());
    boolean atEnd = lastT >= 1 - TERMINAL_TOL;
    if (completion == TIMED_OUT || (completion == REACHED && atEnd)) {
        drive(0, 0, 0);
        return completion;
    }

    Vector2d tangent = path.tangent(lastT).unit();
    Vector2d leftNormal = new Vector2d(-tangent.y(), tangent.x());
    double signedCurvature = path.curvature(lastT);   // + left, - right bend
    double remaining = path.remainingArcLength(lastT);

    double tangentSpeed = velocity.dot(tangent);
    double brakingSpeed = Math.sqrt(2 * MAX_DECEL * remaining);
    double endScale = clamp(remaining / END_SLOWDOWN_DISTANCE, 0, 1);
    double desiredSpeed = Math.min(CRUISE_SPEED, brakingSpeed) * endScale;

    Vector2d alongPath = tangent.times(
            DRIVE_KP * (desiredSpeed - tangentSpeed));
    Vector2d towardPath = path.position(lastT).minus(pose.vec())
            .times(TRANSLATION_KP);
    Vector2d centripetal = leftNormal.times(
            CENTRIPETAL_K * tangentSpeed * tangentSpeed * signedCurvature);

    Vector2d fieldCommand = alongPath.plus(towardPath).plus(centripetal);
    double turn = HEADING_KP * wrap(path.heading(lastT) - pose.heading());

    driveFieldCentric(fieldCommand, turn);
    return RUNNING;
}
`}</JavaCode>

If the tangent's length is near zero, the path has a sharp point there, so stop and report it instead
of dividing by zero. Each of the three vectors has one job: control speed along the path, pull back onto
it, and lean into bends.

<Exercise title="Delete each vector in turn">

What does the robot do with each of the three vectors removed?

<Solution>

Without `alongPath` nothing regulates speed: the robot still converges to the path and still points
the right way, but it crawls or overruns depending only on the other two terms, and the braking plan
does nothing.

Without `towardPath` there is no correction for lateral error: push the robot off the line and it
happily continues parallel to the path forever, because tangent speed and curvature are both still
satisfied.

Without `centripetal` the robot enters every bend on the old tangent and drifts wide, then
`towardPath` yanks it back — so corners are cut on the outside and the path is tracked with a
characteristic lag that gets worse the faster you go.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Heading as a scalar, and the squared speed">

Why is heading a separate number instead of a fourth vector? What kind of drivetrain does that assume,
and what changes on a tank drive? And in the centripetal term, which part sets how hard the robot leans,
`tangentSpeed` squared or the signed curvature, and which sets the direction?

<Solution>

Separating heading assumes a holonomic drivetrain, where translation and rotation are independent
commands. On a differential drive they are not: heading *is* how you translate, so the follower has to
solve for a curvature the chassis can actually execute rather than adding a turn on top.

For the centripetal term, required centripetal acceleration is $v^2\kappa$ — the squared speed sets
the magnitude, which is why the lean grows quadratically as you speed up, and the sign of $\kappa$
sets which side of the path to lean toward.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Admonition type="warning" title="This is architecture, not drop-in Pedro source">

The code shows how the pieces fit, not Pedro's real implementation. It uses one convention on purpose:
the normal always points left of the path, and curvature is positive for left bends. A left bend then
leans left, into the curve, and a right bend's negative curvature flips it to the right. For a real
robot, still follow Pedro's own setup and tuning.

</Admonition>

## What completion really means

A path isn't done just because $t$ reached 1. A reliable follower checks several things:

- path progress is at or near the end;
- position error is small enough;
- heading error is small enough;
- velocity has settled enough for the next action;
- a timeout or correction window has elapsed.

Pedro's docs describe these as end constraints, checked through `follower.isBusy()`. The sketch above
slows down near the end and then uses the shared `MoveStatus`: `REACHED` and `TIMED_OUT` both stop the
robot, but only `REACHED` lets the routine carry on normally. Moving on too early starts the next action
while the robot is still settling, and too late wastes match time.

## What the second-order field adds

The paper gives the theory behind all of this. Its main idea is a field that depends on the robot's
velocity as well as its position, so it says what acceleration to apply, not just which way to go:

$$
\xi(x) = (v, R\Omega, \xi_P(x), \xi_R(x))
$$

Here the state $x$ is position, rotation, velocity, and turn rate together. The four entries say how
each of those should change: position changes by the velocity $v$, rotation changes by the turn rate
($R\Omega$), and $\xi_P$ and $\xi_R$ are the commanded accelerations for moving and turning. For an FTC
mecanum robot, in words:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\text{next command}
=
\text{drive along}
+ \text{pull back} \\[2pt]
+ \text{curve feedforward}
+ \text{turn to heading}
\end{gathered}
$$

Each term is one of the four jobs from the table at the top, with its own constants: $k_p^t$ and $k_d^t$
for pulling back, $k_c$ for curves, $k_p^r$ and $k_d^r$ for heading, and the fitted $D(v)$ for speed along
the path. Those are the numbers a team tunes.
