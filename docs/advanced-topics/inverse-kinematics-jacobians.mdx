# Inverse Kinematics: Jacobians & Numerical Solvers

<Difficulty level="Hard" />

<Abstract>
The main [Inverse Kinematics](../path-following/inverse-kinematics.mdx) lesson solves a two-link arm with
triangle geometry. This lesson covers a more general method: work out how a tiny change in each joint
angle moves the claw, use that to correct the angles a little, and repeat until the claw reaches the
target.
</Abstract>

<Admonition type="note" title="Readiness and prerequisite recap">

Read [Inverse Kinematics](../path-following/inverse-kinematics.mdx) first. This lesson uses the two-link
forward equations from there, plus derivatives (rates of change) and multiplying a matrix by a vector.
The arm demo below is the same geometric solver as before, as a reminder; the step-by-step method is
worked out in the text.

</Admonition>

The two-link solution is neat because everything fits in one triangle. Real mechanisms are often
messier: there's a wrist, an offset linkage, a turret that rotates everything, or a slide that changes a
link's length. Once the geometry gets that complicated, it's easier to ask a smaller question: if I nudge
each joint a tiny bit, which way does the claw move? The answer to that is a table of numbers called
the **Jacobian**.

## Where Jacobians fit among IK algorithms

The Quality Control workshop groups IK methods by how much structure the mechanism has:

| Method | Best when | Core idea |
|---|---|---|
| Closed-form trigonometry | The mechanism has about the same freedoms as the target, like a two-link arm reaching $(x, y)$. | Break links into vector components and solve the equations directly. |
| FABRIK | You have a chain of links and want a geometric, constraint-friendly approximation. | Move the end to the target, then sweep backward and forward while preserving link lengths. |
| Cyclic coordinate descent | You can adjust one joint at a time and want a simple iterative solve. | Rotate each joint so the end effector points closer to the target, then repeat. |
| Jacobian transpose / pseudoinverse | You want a general local solver that also connects to control theory. | Use derivatives to turn a small end-effector error into small joint corrections. |

FABRIK (Forward And Backward Reaching Inverse Kinematics) is popular in graphics because it's easy to
picture: pull the end of the chain to the target, rebuild the chain back toward the base, then back out
again. CCD rotates one joint at a time.

Step-by-step methods need a rule for choosing between answers. If many poses reach the same target, the
solver can jump between them or curl into something awkward unless you add constraints: joint limits, a
fixed wrist angle, "keep the claw level," "prefer elbow up," or "stay close to the last pose." A virtual
four-bar linkage is a hardware version of that: it removes a degree of freedom so the end stays level.

## The Jacobian is a sensitivity matrix

For the same two-link arm, forward kinematics gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= L_1\cos(\theta_1) + L_2\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) \\
y &= L_1\sin(\theta_1) + L_2\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)
\end{aligned}
$$

Now ask: if only $\theta_1$ changes a tiny bit, how fast do $x$ and $y$ change? And the same for $\theta_2$.
Each of those rates is a *partial derivative*, the rate of change when one input moves and the others
stay put. The Jacobian is the table of all four:

$$
J(\theta_1,\theta_2) =
\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial \theta_1} & \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial \theta_2} \\[8pt]
\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial \theta_1} & \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial \theta_2}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Rows are directions the claw can move, and columns are joints. Differentiating the two forward
equations gives, for this arm:

$$
\boxed{
J =
\begin{bmatrix}
-L_1\sin(\theta_1) - L_2\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) &
-L_2\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) \\[6pt]
\phantom{-}L_1\cos(\theta_1) + L_2\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) &
\phantom{-}L_2\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)
\end{bmatrix}
}
$$

Each column is an experiment: the first says how the claw moves if only the shoulder turns a little,
the second if only the elbow does. For small moves:

$$
\Delta \mathbf{x} \approx J\,\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}
$$

$\Delta\mathbf{x}$ is a small change in claw position and $\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}$ the small change in
joint angles that causes it. The $\approx$ matters: it only holds for small steps, and $J$ has to be
worked out again at each new pose.

### One numerical correction

Let $L_1=L_2=1$, $(\theta_1,\theta_2)=(0,\pi/2)$, and the current claw position be $(1,1)$. For a nearby
target $(0.9,1.1)$, the desired task-space change is
$\Delta\mathbf{x}=[-0.1,\ 0.1]^T$. At that pose,

$$
J=\begin{bmatrix}-1&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad
J^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-1&-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}=J^{-1}\Delta\mathbf{x}
=\begin{bmatrix}0.1\\0\end{bmatrix}\ \text{rad}.
$$

So one small step raises the shoulder by about 0.1 rad and leaves the elbow alone. That makes sense if
you picture it: the arm goes right along the first link and straight up along the second, and rotating the whole thing slightly
counter-clockwise moves the claw left and up. After moving, recompute $J$ and repeat.

The same table relates speeds, too. Going from joint speeds to claw speed is easy:

$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}} = J\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
$$

Inverse velocity kinematics asks for the reverse:

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = J^{-1}\dot{\mathbf{x}}
$$

when $J$ is square and has an inverse. It's the same idea as mecanum mixing: a desired motion goes in,
motor commands come out.

### What if the matrix cannot be inverted?

When the arm is almost straight or almost folded, the claw can't easily move in one direction, and
$J^{-1}$ blows up or doesn't exist. These poses are called singularities, and near them the solver asks
for huge joint changes to make tiny claw movements. The usual fix is to add damping:

$$
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}
=
J^T\left(JJ^T + \lambda^2 I\right)^{-1}\Delta\mathbf{x}
$$

$J^T$ is the transpose (rows and columns swapped), $I$ is the identity matrix, and $\lambda$ is a damping
constant. Adding $\lambda^2$ keeps the matrix invertible even at a singularity. The correction comes out
slightly less accurate but never enormous, and a larger $\lambda$ is safer but slower. With
$\lambda = 0$ and an invertible $J$, it's the same as $J^{-1}$. This is called **damped least squares**.

### Jacobian transpose: the gradient-descent view

An even simpler option skips the inverse entirely and nudges the joints in whichever direction reduces
the error fastest:

$$
\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta} = \alpha J^T\mathbf{e}
$$

$\mathbf{e}$ is the claw error (target minus current) and $\alpha$ is a small step size. This is a kind of
gradient descent. It's less exact than the damped version and takes more steps, but it never blows up,
and it's simple enough to run every loop on a slow mechanism.

### Numerical IK loop

Each loop:

1. Compute the current claw position with forward kinematics.
2. Compute the error $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{x}_{target} - \mathbf{x}_{current}$.
3. Compute the Jacobian at the current angles.
4. Solve for a small correction $\Delta\boldsymbol{\theta}$.
5. Add the correction to the joint targets.
6. Clamp joint limits and repeat next loop.

It's a control loop, just working on geometry instead of motor speed.

## A practical numerical IK skeleton

This only computes the angle correction. Your subsystem still handles encoder offsets, feedforward,
limits, and motor commands.

<JavaCode label="Damped least-squares IK step">{`// One small joint delta from the current angles toward a target wrist
// position. Returns null for a degenerate step; commands no motors.
double[] step(double theta1, double theta2, double targetX, double targetY) {
    double x = link1 * Math.cos(theta1) + link2 * Math.cos(theta1 + theta2);
    double y = link1 * Math.sin(theta1) + link2 * Math.sin(theta1 + theta2);

    double errorX = targetX - x;
    double errorY = targetY - y;

    double j00 = -link1 * Math.sin(theta1) - link2 * Math.sin(theta1 + theta2);
    double j01 = -link2 * Math.sin(theta1 + theta2);
    double j10 =  link1 * Math.cos(theta1) + link2 * Math.cos(theta1 + theta2);
    double j11 =  link2 * Math.cos(theta1 + theta2);

    double a00 = j00 * j00 + j01 * j01 + LAMBDA * LAMBDA;
    double a01 = j00 * j10 + j01 * j11;
    double a10 = a01;                          // A is symmetric
    double a11 = j10 * j10 + j11 * j11 + LAMBDA * LAMBDA;

    double det = a00 * a11 - a01 * a10;
    if (Math.abs(det) < EPS) return null;      // try again next loop

    double w0 = ( a11 * errorX - a01 * errorY) / det;
    double w1 = (-a10 * errorX + a00 * errorY) / det;

    return new double[] {
        clamp(j00 * w0 + j10 * w1, -MAX_STEP, MAX_STEP),
        clamp(j01 * w0 + j11 * w1, -MAX_STEP, MAX_STEP),
    };
}
`}</JavaCode>

The first two lines are forward kinematics, `j00` through `j11` are the Jacobian, the `a` values are
$JJ^\top + \lambda^2 I$, and the last lines solve $Aw = e$ and return $\Delta\theta = J^\top w$.

Differentiate the two forward-kinematics equations: $x$ with respect to $\theta_1$ and then $\theta_2$, and
the same for $y$. Check your answers against `j00` to `j11`.

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="What LAMBDA is protecting">

Without `LAMBDA`, the code would invert $JJ^\top$ directly. What is its determinant for a fully
extended arm, and what size step would a plain inverse ask for?

<Solution>

Fully extended, both links point the same way, so the two columns of the Jacobian are parallel and the
determinant of $JJ^\top$ is zero. A plain inverse divides by it, so the solver asks for an enormous joint
step to chase a direction the arm can't move in. Adding $\lambda^2$ keeps the matrix invertible, trading
a slightly wrong direction for a step that stays a reasonable size.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="What MAX_STEP is for">

This is one step of a loop that runs every cycle, so it doesn't have to get there this loop. What could a
single unlimited step do to a real arm?

<Solution>

Near a singularity, or with a target out of reach, the step can come out as several radians. Sent as a
joint target in one loop, that's the arm trying to swing its whole range instantly, into a hard stop,
the robot, or whatever's in front of it. Limiting the step means it gets there over tens of loops, which
at 50 Hz is still well under a second.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

In FTC this is useful when the mechanism has more going on than the two-link formula covers:

- a turret plus an arm, where the turret chooses the plane and the arm solves within that plane;
- an arm plus slides, where extension changes the reachable workspace;
- a wrist that must preserve end-effector angle while the shoulder and elbow move;
- a linkage whose exact geometry is easier to evaluate with forward kinematics than to solve by hand.

</Deploy>

<Admonition type="warning" title="It can chase noise">

The solver is only as good as the measured joint angles. Backlash, flex, and loose chain make the
computed claw position wiggle. Use damping, step limits, and a reasonable tolerance so the loop doesn't
keep sending big corrections into a floppy mechanism.

</Admonition>

<Admonition type="tip" title="Use the geometric solution when you can">

For a simple two-link arm, use the geometric solution from
[Inverse Kinematics](../path-following/inverse-kinematics.mdx). Use Jacobians when the mechanism is too
complicated to solve cleanly with geometry.
