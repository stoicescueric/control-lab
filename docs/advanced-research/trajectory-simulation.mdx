# Build the Offline Flight Model

<Difficulty level="Medium" />

<Abstract>
A launch command becomes useful when it predicts the right event: the ball descending through the
opening. This lesson turns the paper's force model into an offline solver with a clear input/output
contract, then separates numerical accuracy from physical accuracy.
</Abstract>

<Admonition type="note" title="Builder outcome and prerequisites">

Build an offline `simulateShot(...)` function that returns the descending rim crossing, entry
velocity, time of flight, and geometric entry result. The full derivation uses vectors, ordinary
differential equations, RK4, and cubic Hermite interpolation. You can implement a tested solver
without deriving each method, but you must keep the event definition and units explicit.

</Admonition>

A shot aimed at the front lip can look correct as a line on a screen and still clip the goal. The ball has a radius. Its **center** must pass far enough inside both edges, and it must be descending.

## First, draw the available opening

<EntryGeometry />

The paper’s opening is 0.465 m deep and the ball’s diameter is 0.127 m. The usable center interval is therefore:

$$
0.465-0.127=0.338\ \mathrm{m}.
$$

For a front lip at $d=1.778\ \mathrm{m}$ (70 inches), the center must cross between **1.8415 m and 2.1795 m**. The launch height is 0.400 m and the rim plane is 0.984 m above the floor. These are the case-study geometry, from paper §§3 and 5.3.

## Worked example: a 22.5 cm shift

At **5.25 m/s and 58°**, a vacuum calculation places the descending crossing at **2.084 m**. With the paper’s effective drag coefficient, $C_D=0.47$, it crosses at **1.859 m**: approximately **22.5 cm earlier**, a 10.8% reduction.

Both nominal predictions enter this particular opening. But the drag-aware prediction leaves only about **1.8 cm** of front-lip center clearance. That is the reason the next lesson tests neighboring commands instead of celebrating one successful line. Source: paper §4, Figure 3.

:::tip[Predict, change, explain]
Before touching the controls, predict what increasing drag will do to the crossing. Set the drag coefficient to zero, restore it to 0.47, then lower the exit speed. Watch for the difference between **missing the opening** and **never reaching a descending rim event**.
:::

In the **Hermite event locator**, press **Test midpoint** one step at a time. Each test asks whether the reconstructed height is still above the rim. On a descending segment, “above” keeps the right half and “below” keeps the left. After 16 decisions, the original 20 ms interval is only about **0.305 µs** wide; the same fractional time is then used to reconstruct horizontal position and velocity.

## Two forces change the velocity

Gravity points down. Drag points opposite the ball’s velocity relative to the air. For still air, the two-dimensional equations are:

$$
\dot x=v_x,\qquad \dot z=v_z,\qquad
\dot v_x=-k\|\mathbf v\|v_x,\qquad
\dot v_z=-g-k\|\mathbf v\|v_z.
$$

<EquationLegend items={[
  {symbol: 'x, z', meaning: 'horizontal position and height', unit: 'm'},
  {symbol: 'v_x, v_z', meaning: 'horizontal and vertical velocity', unit: 'm/s'},
  {symbol: 'g', meaning: 'gravitational acceleration, 9.80665', unit: 'm/s²'},
  {symbol: 'k', meaning: 'rho C_D A / (2m), approximately 0.0479 for this ball', unit: '1/m'},
  {symbol: '|v|', meaning: 'speed: the magnitude of the velocity vector', unit: 'm/s'},
]} />

The model uses a 0.0748 kg ball, air density 1.204 kg/m³, and frontal area $A=\pi(0.127/2)^2$. The constant $C_D$ is an effective modeling choice, not a drag measurement from the tracking video. Spin, lateral forces, and goal contact are outside this flight model.

For horizontal wind $v_w$, use $\mathbf u=(v_x-v_w,v_z)$ in the drag term. Keep $v_x$ in
$\dot x=v_x$: the position is measured relative to the ground, while drag depends on air-relative
motion. Mixing those two frames changes the trajectory even when the force equation looks familiar.

Paper §4 provides two useful sensitivity checks at the representative command. Varying $C_D$ from
0.40 to 0.55 shifts the crossing by 6.3 cm; varying measured ball diameter from 0.124 to 0.127 m
shifts it by 0.9 cm. These compare model assumptions at one command. They do not measure the
distribution of physical misses.

## Find the event between samples

RK4 advances the state in 20 ms steps. Scoring happens at the instant the ball descends through the rim plane, which usually lies **between** two samples. Cubic Hermite interpolation uses both endpoint positions and velocities to reconstruct that short segment. Sixteen binary-search halvings locate the crossing inside the known bracket.

The same crossing supplies the horizontal coordinate, flight time, and entry velocity. The time when the ball later hits the floor describes a different event and cannot replace it.

A small bisection bracket only controls the root search inside the reconstructed segment. It cannot
remove the integration error in the segment's endpoints or uncertainty in the drag coefficient.

<details>
<summary>Explore the integration method</summary>

Euler takes one starting slope for a whole step. RK4 evaluates four slopes, including estimates inside the step, and combines them with weights $1,2,2,1$ divided by six. With smooth dynamics its global error decreases as the fourth power of the step size.

<IntegratorExperiment />

For an endpoint position $p$ with endpoint velocity $v$, the Hermite segment is:

$$
H(\tau)=(2\tau^3-3\tau^2+1)p_0+(\tau^3-2\tau^2+\tau)hv_0
+(-2\tau^3+3\tau^2)p_1+(\tau^3-\tau^2)hv_1.
$$

Here $h$ is the step duration and $\tau$ runs from zero to one inside the step. Apply this formula to height to find the root $H_z(\tau)=z_{\rm rim}$, then use that same $\tau$ for horizontal position. Source: paper §5.2, Figure 4.

</details>

## Implement the solver in this order

In the [pinned physics engine](https://github.com/stoicescueric/Trajectory-Simulation/blob/09663d6868fd0cc781dc28448cc3f6ace828da1a/backend/physics.py),
follow the calculation in this order:

1. **ShotParams** describes speed, angle, release geometry, target geometry, wind, and drag enablement.
2. **_accel_np** evaluates the same gravity/drag law over arrays of active trajectories.
3. **_integrate** advances them with RK4 and detects a descending height bracket.
4. **_hermite_root** bisects that bracket; **_hermite_derivative** gives the entry velocity.
5. **_made_from** compares the event coordinate with the radius-shrunk opening.
6. **simulate** exposes event time separately from the end of the displayed path.

The source uses 0.98425 m rim height and 0.46482 m depth; the paper and teaching examples round
these to 0.984 m and 0.465 m. State the geometry when comparing last-decimal results. The source's
spin parameters are compatibility fields; they do not add Magnus forces to the equations.

## Verify the mathematics before calibrating the hardware

Paper §5.6 reports 993 vacuum comparisons with an analytic parabola and 158 drag-event comparisons
with an independently implemented DOP853 solver. Maximum position errors are approximately
$7.11\times10^{-7}$ m and $5.41\times10^{-7}$ m respectively. Those are numerical comparisons,
not sub-micrometre physical aiming accuracy.

The [verification harness](https://github.com/stoicescueric/Trajectory-Simulation/blob/09663d6868fd0cc781dc28448cc3f6ace828da1a/backend/verify.py)
also checks caching, exhaustive selection, input validation, and concurrent requests. Its DOP853
comparison is optional and requires SciPy; a skipped comparison is not a passed independent check.

### Acceptance checks before you trust the solver

| Check | Expected result |
|---|---|
| Disable drag and compare with an analytic parabola | Crossing position converges to the analytic answer. |
| Repeat with a smaller integration step | Rim position and time stabilize within a declared tolerance. |
| Use a command that never descends through rim height | The solver returns no event, rather than a fabricated range. |
| Cross outside the radius-shrunk opening | The event exists, but the geometric result is a miss. |
| Continue the display path to the floor | Rim-event time remains distinct from ground-impact time. |

## Java: turn the crossing into an entry test

Once the solver has found a descending event, the geometric test is small:

<JavaCode label="Check the descending center crossing">{`boolean entersOpening(double crossingX, boolean descending,
                      double frontLip, double openingDepth,
                      double ballRadius) {
    if (!descending || !Double.isFinite(crossingX)) return false;
    double firstCenter = frontLip + ballRadius;
    double lastCenter = frontLip + openingDepth - ballRadius;
    return crossingX >= firstCenter && crossingX <= lastCenter;
}`}</JavaCode>

All distances in this method use metres. An unavailable event must not be substituted with a ground range.
