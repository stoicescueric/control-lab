# From One Successful Shot to Reliable Shooting

A ball can enter once and still be a poor shot to repeat. This elective follows the Quantum Robotics launcher through a more useful question: **which command will keep working when the speed and angle change a little?**

The robot launches a light, perforated ball through a raised opening. Its simulator predicts the ball’s flight; robust selection searches a family of nearby shots; calibration connects that prediction to the actual wheels and hood.

<Workflow />

## Follow one shot through five lessons

1. **[Predict Where the Ball Enters](./trajectory-simulation.mdx).** See why drag changes the crossing, and why the ball’s center needs clearance from both lips.
2. **[Choose a Shot That Tolerates Errors](./robust-shot-selection.mdx).** Compare nearby speed–angle commands and find the one with the most successful perturbations.
3. **[Turn the Model into Launcher Commands](./launcher-calibration.mdx).** Measure the wheel-to-ball transfer, interpolate calibrated settings, and recover after a shot.
4. **[Aim While the Robot Moves](./shoot-on-the-move.mdx).** Shift a virtual target opposite inherited chassis velocity and iterate the distance-dependent flight time.
5. **[Model-Guided Shot or Two Empirical LUTs?](./model-vs-empirical-lut.mdx).** Compare simulation-guided calibration with a denser physical wheel-and-hood campaign.

The first two lessons share the paper’s **70-inch (1.778 m) front-lip distance** and its selected **5.25 m/s, 58°** command. Every experiment has a reset button so you can return to its worked example.

## What you need first

Start with vectors, [differential equations](../preface/why-math-matters/differential-equations.mdx), [measured loop timing](../software-architecture/discretization.mdx), and [feedforward with feedback](../control-theory/feedforward.mdx). The pictures and experiments come before the derivations. Expand the mathematics when you want to understand the implementation in more detail.

By the end, you should be able to explain why a simulator can be numerically accurate yet still need calibration, why a wide stress box is not a probability model, how chassis velocity changes the aim point, and when additional empirical LUT data is worth the field time.

The source trail includes the [trajectory simulator repository](https://github.com/stoicescueric/Trajectory-Simulation) and [archived season firmware at commit 75db349d](https://github.com/stoicescueric/YaoMing/tree/75db349d). The manuscript is author reviewed rather than peer reviewed. It is the source for reported experiments; the pinned firmware supplies the deployed lookup knots. The full supplementary experiment archive referenced by the manuscript is not published with this site, so readers can inspect the public code and stated methods but cannot independently replay every reported trial from this repository alone.

:::note[Keep three kinds of numbers separate]
**Calculated predictions** come from the flight model. **Teaching examples** let you inspect an algorithm or do arithmetic. **Reported observations** are the paper’s physical launch counts. The lessons label these roles wherever confusing them would change the conclusion.
:::
