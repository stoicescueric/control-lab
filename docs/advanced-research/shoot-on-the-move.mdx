# Compensate for Robot Motion

<Difficulty level="Medium" />

<Abstract>
When the robot is moving, the ball keeps some of the robot's velocity, so you have to aim somewhere
other than the goal. This lesson builds the aim correction the paper's robot used (a gain of 0.9, a
50 ms feeder delay, seven flight-time values, and up to five update steps) and the checks that decide
whether it's safe to fire.
</Abstract>

<Admonition type="note" title="Builder outcome and prerequisites">

You'll build a `movingAim()` function that returns where to aim, how far that is, the flight time, and
whether the calculation settled. You need field-frame vectors, an up-to-date robot velocity, a flight-time
table, and the feeder delay. The optional math section explains why the calculation settles.

</Admonition>

Throw a ball to someone who is running and you do not aim at where they are. You lead them. A launcher on a moving robot has the same problem even when the field target is fixed: the ball leaves with part of the chassis velocity, so the target moves relative to the projectile.

A draft series of WPILib articles on fire control, [currently only on a preview build](https://frc-docs--3242.org.readthedocs.build/en/3242/docs/software/advanced-controls/fire-control/index.html) and not yet in the published docs, calls this **lead-and-adjust**. It works for any shooter, because the shooter's behavior is already captured in a firing table.

## First build a firing table

A stationary firing table maps target distance to three values:

1. the flywheel command;
2. the hood command;
3. the measured or modeled time for the ball to reach the scoring event.

The first two make the shot happen. The flight time is what makes aiming on the move possible: the moving-shot calculation only needs geometry and time, and everything messy about the launcher, like drag, spin, and contact, is already baked into the measured table.

Keep neighboring distances on the same kind of shot. If nearby rows jump between a high arc and a low one, interpolating between them gives nonsense, and so does the moving-shot calculation built on them. The draft WPILib [static firing-table article](https://frc-docs--3242.org.readthedocs.build/en/3242/docs/software/advanced-controls/fire-control/static-shooting.html) suggests starting with a coarse table, adding rows where testing shows you need them, and recording flight time while you're at it.

For this robot, the archived table stores the descending rim-event time:

| Distance (in) | 50 | 60 | 76 | 85 | 95 | 110 | 130 |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Rim flight time (s) | 0.50 | 0.54 | 0.60 | 0.70 | 0.80 | 0.95 | 1.05 |

These are the seven knots in `Sensors.java` at firmware commit `75db349d`. They describe time to the rim event, not the later time when a plotted path reaches the floor.

## Lead, look up, and adjust

The ball leaves with part of the robot's velocity, so during the feeder delay $t_d$ plus the flight time $t_f$, it drifts by about the robot's velocity $\mathbf v_R$ times that time. Aim that far the other way. This robot also used a measured gain $G$ for how much of the robot's velocity the ball actually keeps:

$$
\mathbf p_v=\mathbf p_g-G\mathbf v_R(t_d+t_f).
$$

$\mathbf p_g$ is the real goal and $\mathbf p_v$ is the adjusted aim point, the "virtual target." $G=0.9$ and $t_d=0.05\ \mathrm{s}$. The minus sign moves the aim point opposite to the robot's motion, and with the robot stopped the correction is zero.

There's a catch: the flight time depends on the distance, and the distance depends on where you aim. So it goes in a loop:

1. **Guess** flight time from the real-target distance.
2. **Lead** by shifting the virtual target through the predicted platform displacement.
3. **Adjust** by looking up flight time at the virtual distance.
4. **Repeat** until successive flight times differ by less than the chosen tolerance.

This is the [dynamic shooting method](https://frc-docs--3242.org.readthedocs.build/en/3242/docs/software/advanced-controls/fire-control/dynamic-shooting.html) from the draft WPILib articles, with a fixed FTC goal and this robot's measured gain.

:::tip[Predict the correction direction]
Choose **Sideways** and predict which side of the real target becomes the virtual target. Compare **Approaching** and **Retreating**, then step through the rows. Direct approach and retreat change virtual distance strongly, so they also expose more of the time map.
:::

## Worked example: the first shift is 1.1475 m

At **95 inches**, the real-target lookup starts at $t_f=0.80\ \mathrm{s}$. For a sideways chassis speed of $1.5\ \mathrm{m/s}$,

$$
\lVert\Delta\mathbf p\rVert
=0.9(1.5)(0.05+0.80)
=\mathbf{1.1475\ m}.
$$

That first aim point is farther from the shooter than the real goal, so its flight time is longer, and the next step moves the aim point a bit more. You don't need wheel and hood settings for the in-between steps. Only the final distance is used to look up the actual shot.

## Decide whether the iteration is trustworthy

The robot's code starts from the real goal's flight time, stops when two estimates in a row differ by
0.05 s or less, and gives up after five steps. Report that last difference with the result, and don't
fire if it didn't settle.

<details>
<summary>Optional math: why the fixed-point update converges</summary>

The scalar update is

$$
t_{k+1}=t_f\!\left(\left\lVert
\mathbf p_g-G\mathbf v_R(t_d+t_k)-\mathbf p_s
\right\rVert\right).
$$

Once you have three estimates, you can see how fast they're closing in:

$$
\widehat\rho=\left|\frac{t_{k+1}-t_k}{t_k-t_{k-1}}\right|.
$$

A small $\widehat\rho$ means the estimates are settling fast. Close to one means the answer is fragile, and a small error in velocity or timing can move the aim point a lot. (Skip this when the bottom of the fraction is basically zero.) The demo shows it as information, not a fire or no-fire rule.

The paper also gives a guaranteed bound. The steepest part of the flight-time table changes by $M=0.4867\ \mathrm{s/m}$, so

$$
q=G\lVert\mathbf v_R\rVert M<1
$$

guarantees the loop settles on one answer. With $G=0.9$ that holds up to **2.28 m/s**. A computer check of 1,399,392 cases up to 1.7 m/s all settled within five steps, and 170 of them needed all five. That's a check of the math, not of how often shots go in.

</details>

Before releasing, check all of the following:

- pose and velocity are finite, recent, and expressed in the same field frame;
- the virtual distance remains inside the calibrated table or clamping is explicitly permitted;
- the iteration converged within its budget and the final residual is below the tuned threshold;
- chassis speed and estimated contraction rate lie inside a range validated on the real robot;
- flywheel and hood have reached their final commands.

There's no universal threshold for these. Set them from real shots at the speeds and angles your drivers actually use.

## Read the residual in metres as well as seconds

Stopping at a 0.05 s difference doesn't mean the aim point is exact. At 1.7 m/s, the paper's worst-case
bound is 0.146 s, or 0.223 m. In practice, running the robot's code over a grid of cases, the aim point
was within 8.3 cm of the fully settled answer 99% of the time and 17.1 cm in the worst case.

Three different numbers get called "error" here, so keep them apart: the change between the last two
flight-time estimates, how far the final estimate is from satisfying the equation exactly (at most
0.031 s in the paper), and how far the aim point is from where it would settle.

The demo shows each step of a simplified version in metric units. The robot's Java differs in small
ways, like reading distance from `getX()`/`getY()`, and the lesson's code returns the status
explicitly instead of copying every detail.

## What changed in the newer robot?

The [newer Sensors.java](https://github.com/stoicescueric/YaoMing/blob/8374312fa7ba75b87197c2dbaeee5c46624722fa/TeamCode/src/main/java/org/firstinspires/ftc/teamcode/Hardware/Sensors.java)
splits velocity along the target line and perpendicular to it. Radial drift changes the virtual
distance used for wheel/hood commands; lateral drift changes turret lead.

| Setting | Paper's season snapshot | Newer snapshot defaults |
|---|---|---|
| Inheritance gain | 0.9 | 1.30 |
| TOF at 95 inches | 0.80 s | 0.70 s |
| TOF at 130 inches | 1.05 s | 0.90 s |
| Flight-time weighting | one full TOF | 0.55 radial, 0.7 lateral |
| Acceleration correction | disabled | radial factor 0.17; lateral factor 0 |
| Position reference in the update | robot getters | shooter world position with turret offset |

Both use a 0.05 s feeder delay and five steps, but the newer formula is different enough that the
season's 2.28 m/s bound and scoring results don't carry over. Recheck both before relying on them.

## Next step: Newton can solve faster

The repeat-until-it-settles loop is easy to write and usually takes three to five steps, but in some directions it settles slowly. A follow-on draft WPILib article turns it into a root-finding problem:

$$
E(t)=t-t_f(D(t))=0,
$$

and solves it with [Newton's method](https://frc-docs--3242.org.readthedocs.build/en/3242/docs/software/advanced-controls/fire-control/newton-shooting.html). For a measured table, it uses a smooth estimate of the slope instead of differentiating the noisy table values.

Newton's method takes fewer steps. It doesn't make a fragile shot any less fragile, and it depends more on a good starting guess. Use it if running out of steps is actually your problem, and keep all the same safety checks.

## Next step: decide how drag changes inherited motion

The draft WPILib [linear-drag article](https://frc-docs--3242.org.readthedocs.build/en/3242/docs/software/advanced-controls/fire-control/linear-drag.html) replaces the time $t$ in the drift calculation with

$$
\alpha(t)=\frac{1-e^{-kt}}{k},
$$

so the drift becomes $\mathbf v_R\alpha(t)$, which accounts for air slowing the ball's sideways motion.

This robot's ball has holes in it and is modeled with drag that grows with speed squared, and its code
used the measured gain $G=0.9$ instead of that formula. The paper shows 0.9 is a reasonable value, but
that doesn't make the two approaches interchangeable. Pick one, measure its setting from moving shots,
and test it, rather than stacking both.

The season code didn't correct for acceleration. If the robot is still speeding up or slowing down
during the delay, the paper bounds the extra drift by

$$
\lVert\delta\mathbf p_a\rVert\le
\lVert\mathbf a_R\rVert\left(t_dt_f+\frac{t_d^2}{2}\right),
$$

which is **2.6 to 5.4 cm for every 1 m/s²** across the robot's flight-time table. So the first thing to do about acceleration is measure it and avoid firing while it's large, before adding another term to the controller.

## What the controlled launches establish

The paper alternated compensation OFF and ON within each commanded-speed block, with 15 launches per state:

| Chassis state | Stationary | 0.5 m/s | 0.8 m/s | 1.5 m/s | 1.7 m/s |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| SOTM OFF | 15/15 | 13/15 | 10/15 | 5/15 | 2/15 |
| SOTM ON | 15/15 | 15/15 | 15/15 | 13/15 | 11/15 |

Standing still, both scored 15/15, so turning compensation on didn't break the stationary shot. While
moving, entries went from **30/60 to 54/60**. That supports the method as a whole, though it can't say
which part (the gain, the delay, the velocity filter, the table, or the firing checks) mattered most.
Source: research paper §8.3, Table 3.

Only the totals were kept, not a log of each shot. The paper reports a 40-point difference in percentage
(95% interval 24.1 to 53.4 points). That's good evidence compensation helps, but it can't tell you why any
individual shot missed.

## Java: return the solution and its evidence

<JavaCode league="FTC" label="Solve and report a moving-shot aim point">{`AimResult movingAim(Vec2 shooter, Vec2 target, Vec2 robotVelocity) {
    Lookup initial = flightTime.lookup(target.minus(shooter).norm());
    double time = initial.value();
    Vec2 virtualTarget = target;
    double residual = Double.POSITIVE_INFINITY;
    boolean clamped = initial.clamped();

    for (int update = 0; update < 5; update++) {
        double totalTime = 0.05 + time;
        virtualTarget = target.minus(robotVelocity.times(0.9 * totalTime));

        Lookup next = flightTime.lookup(virtualTarget.minus(shooter).norm());
        residual = Math.abs(next.value() - time);
        clamped |= next.clamped();
        if (residual <= 0.05) {
            return new AimResult(virtualTarget, true, clamped, residual, update + 1);
        }
        time = next.value();
    }
    return new AimResult(virtualTarget, false, clamped, residual, 5);
}`}</JavaCode>

The firing logic should check the status, not just the aim point. An aim point from a calculation that
didn't settle, or that ran off the end of the table, shouldn't be trusted unless you've tested that case.

## Put it in the robot loop in this order

1. Verify pose and velocity timestamps, units, and field frame.
2. Compute the shooter world position, including its offset from the robot center.
3. Solve the virtual target and expose convergence, clamping, residual, and iteration count.
4. Reject invalid, stale, out-of-range, or unconverged results.
5. Use virtual distance for wheel and hood; use virtual bearing for turret aim.
6. Require wheel, hood, turret, and robot-state gates before feeding.
7. Log the solution and the measured release event.

Before field testing, check that zero velocity gives the real goal, reversing the velocity reverses the
correction, old velocity data blocks firing, a distance off the table reports clamping, and one example
matches a hand calculation. These catch sign and frame mistakes that otherwise look like bad tuning.

<Exercise title="Choose the sign">
The robot moves at $+1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ along the field's $y$ axis. The delay plus flight time is $0.75\ \mathrm{s}$ and $G=0.9$. What $y$ correction should be applied to the real target?
<Solution>
$-0.9(1.0)(0.75)=-0.675\ \mathrm{m}$. The virtual target moves 0.675 m in the negative $y$ direction, opposite the inherited chassis velocity.
</Solution>
